## Medicine's History of Error, Reversal, and Self-Correction
### A Complete Textbook Structure & Authoring Manual — From Zero to Mastery

> *"The most dangerous phrase in medicine is not 'I don't know.' It is 'It makes sense.'"*

## A LETTER TO YOU, THE AUTHOR

Before we build anything, sit with me for a minute.

You want to write a book about how medicine gets things wrong. Good. But I need you to understand something first, because it determines the shape of everything that follows: **this is not a book about stupid doctors.**

If you write it as a parade of idiots — the bloodletters, the lobotomists, the men who told mothers to put babies face-down — you will have written a cheap book. It will be fun to read and it will teach nothing, because the reader will close it thinking *"well, thank God we're smarter now."* And they are not smarter now. Neither are you. Neither am I.

The real book — the one worth five years of your life — is about something much more unsettling: **intelligent, careful, well-meaning people, using the best reasoning available to them, arriving confidently at conclusions that killed patients.** Not despite their intelligence. Often *because* of it. The lobotomy won a Nobel Prize in 1949. The man who promoted prone infant sleeping was the most trusted pediatrician in America. The antiarrhythmic drugs that killed more Americans than the Vietnam War were prescribed by cardiologists who had read the literature carefully and reasoned impeccably from it.

That is the horror at the centre of this book, and it is also the gift. Because if error is a product of *normal* thinking rather than defective thinking, then error is **structural**, and structures can be studied, mapped, and defended against.

So here is our thesis, and I want you to write it on a card and tape it above your desk:

> **Medicine's errors are not random. They are the predictable output of a small number of recurring thinking flaws, interacting with a small number of recurring institutional pressures. Learn the flaws and the pressures, and you can often see the error before the bodies pile up.**

Everything in this structure serves that thesis.

## THE BOOK'S THREE QUESTIONS

Every chapter answers at least one. State which, at the top of your draft, every time.

1. **WHY DID WE BELIEVE IT?** — The manufacture of conviction. Where wrong ideas come from, and why they felt *right* at the time.
2. **WHY WAS IT WRONG?** — The specific, nameable thinking flaw. Not "they lacked evidence" — that's lazy. *Which* flaw? *Operating how?*
3. **HOW DID WE FIND OUT — AND WHY DID IT TAKE SO LONG?** — The correction machinery, and the friction that resists it.

A fourth question hovers over all of them and is the moral spine of the book:

4. **WHAT ARE WE DOING RIGHT NOW THAT WILL LOOK LIKE THIS IN FORTY YEARS?**

## THE AUTOPSY PROTOCOL
### *(Your single most important structural device — read this twice)*

Every major case in this book gets dissected the same way. This repetition is not laziness; it is **pedagogy**. By chapter twelve the reader will start running the protocol in their own head before you do. That's the moment they stop being a reader and start being a thinker. That is the book working.

**The eleven-step autopsy:**

1. **THE CLAIM** — State the belief in one flat sentence, as its believers would have stated it. No irony. No foreshadowing. Respect it.
2. **THE WORLD THAT MADE IT** — What was the medical, technological, financial, and cultural landscape? What problem was this belief *solving*?
3. **THE MECHANISM STORY** — The biological narrative that made it feel obvious. Draw it. Almost every disaster in this book has a beautiful mechanism story.
4. **THE EVIDENCE AT ADOPTION** — Precisely what was known, and what its actual epistemic weight was. Be forensic. Case series? Uncontrolled trial? Surrogate endpoint? Expert consensus?
5. **THE HUMAN FORCES** — Who benefited? Whose career, income, identity, or institution was built on this? Not conspiracy — *incentive*.
6. **THE ADOPTION CURVE** — How fast did it spread, and by what route? Guidelines? Journals? Marketing? Malpractice fear? Patient demand?
7. **THE FLAW, NAMED** — The specific error, using the book's vocabulary. Usually two or three interacting. *This is the heart of the autopsy.*
8. **THE UNRAVELLING** — Who doubted, when, why, and what did it cost them? What study finally broke it? Why was that study possible only then?
9. **THE LAG** — Time from first credible refutation to actual abandonment. Quantify it. Then quantify the harm inside the lag.
10. **THE FAIR VERDICT** — Was this *blameless* error (unknowable at the time) or *culpable* error (the tools to know existed and were refused)? Be honest. Be fair. Some of your villains are innocent and some of your heroes are not.
11. **THE TRANSFER** — Where is this exact pattern operating in medicine today? Name a live example. Every autopsy must point at the present or it is just entertainment.

**Author's discipline:** if you cannot fill step 10 and step 11, you do not understand the case well enough to write it. Go back to the primary sources.

---

## THE BOOK'S VOICE — RULES YOU MUST NOT BREAK

1. **No hindsight sneering.** The past did not know what you know. Write about 1955 as though you might have been in the room and agreed.
2. **Name the flaw every time.** Vague indignation is worthless. "This was confounding by indication" is worth a hundred pages of outrage.
3. **A human being on every page.** Not "patients were harmed" — *a* patient. Named where records allow. Medicine's errors have addresses.
4. **Steel-man before you strike.** Present the wrong belief so persuasively that the reader half-believes it. Then dismantle it. If the reader never felt the pull, they'll never notice the pull in themselves.
5. **Show the counterfactual.** What could a careful thinker have known *at the time*? This is what makes the book useful rather than merely tragic.
6. **Include the successes.** A book only about failure teaches nihilism. Nihilism kills patients too — it's the fuel of anti-vaccine movements and "doctors know nothing" quackery. You must show self-correction working.
7. **End every chapter pointing forward.** The reader should feel the ground moving under present-day practice.

# PART 0 — ORIENTATION: LEARNING TO SEE

> *Word budget: ~25,000. Four chapters. This Part must be readable by a curious seventeen-year-old with no science background, and still respected by a professor of epidemiology. That's hard. Write it last, after you understand the whole book, but place it first.*

**The job of Part 0:** install the reader's eyes. They arrive believing medicine is a body of knowledge. They must leave Part 0 believing medicine is a **process of belief revision under uncertainty** — which is a completely different thing and changes how they read every subsequent page.

## CHAPTER 0.1 — The Scandal at the Heart of Medicine

**The Argument:** A substantial fraction of what medicine confidently does at any given moment is wrong, and this is a permanent, structural feature — not a passing embarrassment.

**Why this chapter exists:** To create the disorientation the rest of the book will organise. The reader must feel the floor give way on page 3, or they will read the case studies as curiosities rather than warnings.

### 0.1.1 — Open with a body

Do not open with statistics. Open with one person.

**Recommended cold open:** a woman in 1998 with metastatic breast cancer, receiving high-dose chemotherapy with autologous bone marrow transplant. Describe the treatment honestly: near-lethal doses of chemotherapy, her marrow harvested and frozen beforehand, weeks in isolation, mouth ulcerated from lip to stomach, roughly a 3–5% chance the treatment itself kills her. Her insurer fought paying; her state legislature passed a law forcing them to. Activists marched for access to this. Oprah covered it.

Then the sentence that detonates the chapter: **it did not work.** It never worked. Between roughly 1988 and 2000, somewhere in the region of 30,000–40,000 American women underwent it. When the randomised trials reported at ASCO in 1999, there was no survival benefit. The one apparently positive trial, from Werner Bezwoda in South Africa, was found in 2000 to be fabricated.

Now ask the reader the question the whole book answers: **how does that happen?** Not in a backwater. In the best hospitals in the richest country on earth, with the smartest oncologists, with patients and advocates and courts all pushing in the same direction.

### 0.1.2 — The scale of the problem, quantified

Teach the reader that this is not anecdote-fishing. Give the meta-research:

- **Prasad et al., *Mayo Clinic Proceedings*, 2013.** Ten years of the *New England Journal of Medicine* (2001–2010) reviewed. Of the original articles testing an established practice, **~40% were reversals** — the study showed the existing practice was no better than, or worse than, a lesser alternative. Roughly the same number reaffirmed practice; the rest were inconclusive.
- **Herrera-Perez et al., *eLife*, 2019.** Extended to three top journals over 15 years: ~3,000 RCTs screened, **396 medical reversals** identified.
- **Ioannidis, *JAMA*, 2005.** Of the most highly cited clinical research studies, a substantial minority were later contradicted or found to have exaggerated effects.
- **Ioannidis, *PLoS Medicine*, 2005** — "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False." The most-downloaded paper in the journal's history. Explain its argument in plain language later (Chapter 9); here just plant the flag.

**Teaching note for you:** Handle these numbers carefully and honestly. "40% of medicine is wrong" is *not* what the Prasad study says — it studied practices that journals chose to test, which is a biased sample (you test what you doubt). Say so. **Your credibility in this book is your only asset.** A reader who catches you overclaiming in Chapter 1 will discount Chapter 30. Being scrupulous about the limits of your own evidence is also a *demonstration* of the book's method. Model the thinking you're teaching.

### 0.1.3 — Introduce the term: *medical reversal*

Define it precisely, because it will recur 300 times:

> **Medical reversal:** when a medical practice that has been widely adopted is found, by a subsequent and better study, to be no better than — or worse than — a lesser or no intervention.

Distinguish it carefully from its neighbours:

- **Replacement** — a good thing is superseded by a better thing. (Streptokinase → alteplase → primary PCI.) *This is progress, not reversal.* Do not conflate them.
- **Refinement** — the practice works but the indication narrows. (Tight glycaemic control: right target, wrong population.)
- **Reversal** — the practice never worked, or actively harmed, and should never have been adopted at that level of evidence.
- **De-adoption** — the *act* of stopping. Reversal is an epistemic event; de-adoption is a sociological one. **They are separated by years, sometimes decades.** That gap is where Part V lives.

### 0.1.4 — The moral stakes

Introduce the idea of **harm inside the lag**. This is the book's most important quantitative move. For each case, you will ask: from the moment a competent observer *could* have known, to the moment practice actually stopped, how many people were treated?

Preview with the infant sleep case: cumulative meta-analysis (Gilbert et al., *International Journal of Epidemiology*, 2005) indicates that the evidence against prone infant sleeping was sufficient by around 1970. Systematic "Back to Sleep" campaigns arrived in the early-to-mid 1990s. The authors estimated that this delay cost on the order of **tens of thousands of infant lives** across the developed world.

Sit with that. Not one doctor did anything malicious. It was a **reasoning failure** distributed across a profession, and it killed more children than most diseases you can name.

### 0.1.5 — The promise of the book

End with the contract:

> By the end of this book you will not be able to hear a medical claim without automatically asking: *What kind of evidence is this? What would it look like if it were wrong? Who is measuring the thing that actually matters to a human being?* You will be, in the useful sense, harder to fool. Including by yourself.

**Word budget:** 6,000
**Reader exercise:** Write down three things your own doctor (or your own practice) does routinely. For each, write one sentence on the evidence you believe supports it. Seal the envelope. Reopen at the end of the book.

## CHAPTER 0.2 — What "Wrong" Means: A Taxonomy of Being Mistaken

**The Argument:** "Wrong" is not one thing. Precision about *kinds* of wrongness is the first real skill of the discipline.

**Why this chapter exists:** Without it, the book becomes a mush of indignation. With it, the reader acquires a filing system that makes 400 pages of cases feel orderly instead of overwhelming.

### 0.2.1 — Nine species of medical wrongness

Give each a name, a definition, a canonical case, and a "how you'd catch it."

1. **The Ineffective** — does nothing, costs something. *Gastric freezing for peptic ulcer.*
2. **The Net-Harmful** — works on the surrogate, kills on the outcome. *Class IC antiarrhythmics post-MI (CAST).*
3. **The Overextended** — genuinely works, in a narrow group, applied to a wide one. *Antibiotics; tight glycaemic control; opioids.*
4. **The Overdiagnosed** — detects real abnormalities that were never going to hurt anyone. *Thyroid cancer screening in South Korea.*
5. **The Misattributed** — the effect is real but the cause is not what we think. *Almost every uncontrolled surgical series.*
6. **The Premature** — right idea, adopted before it was known to be right; sometimes vindicated, often not. *Extracorporeal life support for years; many device approvals.*
7. **The Inverted** — we had the causal arrow backwards. *Stress causes ulcers → Helicobacter pylori causes ulcers.*
8. **The Fabricated** — the data were not real. *Bezwoda; Fujii; Boldt; Wakefield; Potti.*
9. **The Unfalsifiable** — structured so no evidence can dislodge it. *Much of pre-modern therapeutics; some of modern integrative medicine; certain "personalised" claims.*

**The Deep Why (for you, the author):**
A beginner thinks the interesting question is "true or false?" The expert knows the interesting question is *"in what way, and by what mechanism, is this claim failing to track reality?"* Species 2 and 3 are the ones that kill the most people, because both start from something that is genuinely true. Species 4 is the hardest to teach because it is genuinely counterintuitive — *finding a real cancer can be a harm* — and you'll need a whole chapter for it (Chapter 12). Species 9 is the one that separates medicine from its imitators. Spend real time on it.

### 0.2.2 — Degrees of culpability

Introduce the ethical spine early. Three tiers:

- **Blameless error** — the tools to know did not exist. *Puerperal fever before germ theory.* Compassion is the correct response.
- **Negligent error** — the tools existed, were affordable, and were not used. *Continuing to adopt surgical procedures without sham controls in 2005, fifty years after Cobb's internal mammary study showed why you need them.*
- **Culpable error** — the evidence existed, was seen, and was suppressed or ignored for gain. *Vioxx's cardiovascular signal; Purdue's marketing of OxyContin; the tobacco playbook applied to medicine.*

**Teaching note:** Your reader will want to collapse everything into tier 3, because villains are satisfying. Resist. **Most of this book is tier 2, and tier 2 is the one we can actually fix.** Tier 3 is a legal and regulatory problem; tier 2 is a *thinking* problem, and thinking is what you're teaching.

### 0.2.3 — The asymmetry of error

Introduce the two ways to be wrong and why medicine is structurally biased toward one:

- **Type I in practice:** treating something that shouldn't be treated. Harm is *committed*, diffuse, delayed, statistical, invisible.
- **Type II in practice:** failing to treat something that should be. Harm is *omitted*, concentrated, immediate, personal, visible.

A doctor who withholds a treatment and the patient dies will remember that face forever. A doctor who gives an ineffective treatment to 400 patients, three of whom are harmed by it, will never know which three. **Medicine's error-detection apparatus is blind in exactly one direction, and this is the single most important structural fact in the book.** It explains commission bias, the technological imperative, defensive medicine, over-screening, and roughly half the case files.

Foreshadow heavily. You'll return to this in 8.4, 13.2, 21.1, and 47.

**Word budget:** 6,000
**Reader exercise:** Take three historical practices (bloodletting, lobotomy, HRT for cardioprotection). Classify each by species (0.2.1) and by culpability tier (0.2.2). Justify in three sentences each. There is no single right answer — the *justification* is the skill.

## CHAPTER 0.3 — The Vocabulary Kit: Minimum Viable Epistemics

**The Argument:** Eleven concepts will carry ninety percent of the book. Learn them here, in plain language, with no mathematics.

**Why this chapter exists:** Your reader has zero prior knowledge. If they hit "confounding by indication" on page 200 unarmed, you lose them. This chapter is the armoury. Keep it ruthlessly plain — **every concept gets a kitchen-table analogy before it gets a technical definition.**

### 0.3.1 — The eleven concepts

Write each as a two-page mini-essay with: the analogy, the definition, the medical example, and the "how it fools you."

**1. Correlation is not causation — and *why* not, specifically.**
Don't just assert it. Show the three alternatives to causation: reverse causation, confounding, and chance. Analogy: ice cream sales and drowning deaths (confounder: summer). Medical version: people who take vitamins live longer (confounder: people who take vitamins are the sort of people who wear seatbelts).

**2. The counterfactual.**
The single most important idea in the book. The question is never "did the patient get better?" It is always **"would this patient have gotten better anyway?"** You can never observe the counterfactual for one person — this is the *fundamental problem of causal inference* — which is why we need groups, and randomisation, and why single-case reasoning is so treacherous. Analogy: you took a cold remedy and got better in a week. Without it, you'd have gotten better in seven days.

**3. Natural history & regression to the mean.**
Most illness is self-limiting. Most measurements fluctuate. People seek care at their worst moment, so their next measurement will usually be better *whatever you do*. This single fact has validated more useless treatments than fraud ever has. Analogy: the sports commentator's "curse" of the magazine cover.

**4. The placebo response — and what it actually is.**
Be careful here; the popular understanding is wrong. Beecher's 1955 "The Powerful Placebo" claimed ~35% of patients respond to placebo — a figure that is repeated everywhere and is largely an artefact of confusing placebo response with natural history and regression. Hróbjartsson & Gøtzsche (*NEJM*, 2001) compared placebo groups with *no-treatment* groups and found little effect on objective outcomes, and modest effects on subjective ones, especially pain. **Teach the reader that even the classic critique of medicine has itself been reversed.** That's a beautiful early lesson in humility.

**5. Bias vs. chance.**
Chance is noise: random, symmetric, shrinks with sample size. Bias is a systematic tilt: it does *not* shrink with sample size — a bigger biased study is a more confidently wrong study. Analogy: a bathroom scale that's noisy vs. one that's set 5 kg light. Buying a bigger scale doesn't help.

**6. Confounding.**
A third factor causing both. Then the specialist versions the book depends on:
- **Confounding by indication** — sicker patients get the aggressive treatment, so the treatment looks lethal.
- **Healthy user bias** — the sort of person who adheres to a prescribed regimen is the sort of person who does everything else right. *This is the killer of nutritional and preventive epidemiology, and it is the whole story of the HRT disaster.*
- **Healthy adherer effect** — demonstrated most elegantly by trials showing that people who adhere to *placebo* have better outcomes than people who don't.

**7. Selection bias.**
Who got into your study, and who is missing? Sub-forms: volunteer bias, survivorship bias, referral/Berkson bias, loss to follow-up. Analogy: Abraham Wald and the returning bombers — armour the places with no bullet holes, because the planes hit *there* didn't come back.

**8. Surrogate vs. patient-important outcomes.**
The most lethal concept in the book. A **surrogate** is a measurable stand-in — blood pressure, cholesterol, tumour shrinkage, bone density, viral load, amyloid plaque, PVC frequency on a Holter monitor. A **patient-important outcome** is one a person would notice from inside their life: death, stroke, symptomatic fracture, dementia, being able to climb the stairs.

Teach the golden rule: **a surrogate is a hypothesis, not a result.** Sometimes the hypothesis is right (viral load in HIV). Sometimes it is catastrophically wrong (PVC suppression in CAST; HDL raising in torcetrapib; bone density with fluoride). And crucially — *you cannot tell which from the biology.* You have to test it.

**9. Absolute vs. relative risk, and NNT.**
A drug that "halves your risk" of something that had a 2-in-1,000 chance is preventing one event per thousand people treated. **Relative risk is the language of marketing; absolute risk is the language of decisions.** Teach NNT (number needed to treat) and NNH (number needed to harm) with worked arithmetic, slowly, no algebra. Have the reader compute three by hand.

**10. Statistical significance: what a p-value is *not*.**
Plain language: a p-value is *"if there were truly no effect, how surprising would data like mine be?"* It is **not** the probability the hypothesis is true. It is not a measure of effect size or importance. The 0.05 threshold is a convention Fisher offered casually and has hardened into a gate through which careers pass. Everything about publication, hype, and p-hacking flows from that hardening.

**11. Prior probability / base rates.**
The most under-taught idea in medicine and the one that makes everything else click. A positive test on a rare disease is usually a false positive. A "significant" result in an implausible field is usually a false positive. Teach with the classic 1-in-1,000 disease, 95%-accurate test worked example. **Have the reader do the arithmetic themselves — this is the exercise that changes people.**

**The Deep Why (for you):**
Notice the sequencing. Concepts 1–4 dismantle the reader's default reasoning ("I took it and got better"). Concepts 5–7 explain why *studies* fail. Concept 8 explains why *good studies of the wrong thing* fail. Concepts 9–11 explain why *good studies of the right thing* get misinterpreted. That's a ladder: personal reasoning → study design → measurement → inference. Every rung must be climbed in order. Do not reorder them.

**Word budget:** 9,000
**Reader exercise:** A "spot the flaw" set of twelve short fictional news items — each contains exactly one of the eleven flaws. Answer key with explanation of *why*, not just *which*.

## CHAPTER 0.4 — Fairness to the Dead: Against Hindsight

**The Argument:** Judging the past by present knowledge is itself a thinking flaw — and indulging it will make you the next generation's cautionary tale.

**Why this chapter exists:** It is your ethical licence to write the rest of the book. Without it, you are a scold. With it, you are a colleague of the dead.

### 0.4.1 — Hindsight bias, defined and demonstrated

Once we know an outcome, we cannot un-know it, and we systematically overestimate how predictable it was. Cite the classic experimental literature (Fischhoff, 1975). Then apply it: everyone "knew" prone sleeping was dangerous — after 1994. Nobody knew before.

### 0.4.2 — Chronocentrism

The unstated assumption that we stand at the end of history. Every generation of physicians has believed the previous one was primitive and their own is essentially correct. Quote them believing it. **Find at least four quotations, spread across two centuries, of physicians declaring that medicine has finally become scientific.** Put them in a box, undated, and let the reader guess the years. This is one of the best pages in your book.

### 0.4.3 — The counterfactual standard

Here is the fair test, and I want you to apply it rigorously in every autopsy:

> **Not:** "Was this belief true?" (We know; they didn't.)
> **But:** "Given the tools, concepts, and data available at the time, was this belief *justified*, and was the *strength* of conviction proportional to the evidence?"

That second clause is where nearly all the culpability lives. Semmelweis's contemporaries can be forgiven for not knowing about bacteria. They cannot be so easily forgiven for dismissing a fourfold mortality difference that was sitting in their own ledgers.

### 0.4.4 — The uncomfortable mirror

End Part 0 by turning the lens. Ask the reader to hold in mind, for the entire book, a list of current practices that carry the same epistemic signature as the historical disasters: adopted on mechanism, validated on surrogates, defended by eminence, protected by incentive. You'll fill that list in Part VI. Plant the seeds now.

**Word budget:** 4,000
**Reader exercise:** Find a medical claim in this week's news. Apply the counterfactual standard from 0.4.3. Is the confidence in the reporting proportional to the evidence described?

# PART I — WHY WE BELIEVE
## The Manufacture of Medical Conviction

> *Word budget: ~45,000. Six chapters.*

**The job of Part I:** Before you can explain why medicine was wrong, you must explain why it was *sure*. This Part is the psychology and sociology of conviction. It is the part most books skip, and skipping it is why most books on this subject fail — they treat wrong beliefs as absences of evidence, when they are actually *presences* of something else: a compelling story, a trusted authority, a vivid memory, a moral imperative.

**Structural note:** Part I is thematic, not chronological, and it is deliberately light on statistics. Part II brings the machinery. Part I brings the human being. Keep them separate — a reader who meets confounding and cognitive dissonance in the same chapter learns neither.

## CHAPTER 1 — The Seduction of Mechanism
### *Why "it makes sense" is the most dangerous sentence in medicine*

**The Argument:** A plausible biological mechanism generates conviction wildly out of proportion to its evidential value, and this single fault is the root cause of more medical harm than any other.

**Why this chapter exists:** This is the book's keystone chapter. If the reader takes one thing away from 450,000 words, it must be this. Put it first in Part I and make it the best chapter you write.

### 1.1 — What mechanistic reasoning is

Define it plainly: **inferring that a treatment will help patients from an understanding of how the body works.**

Give the general form so the reader can recognise it anywhere:

> Disease D is caused by (or associated with) process P.
> Intervention I modifies P in the favourable direction.
> **Therefore** intervention I will benefit patients with D.

Then teach the reader that the word "therefore" is doing an enormous amount of unearned work. It is a **leap across a biological system of staggering complexity** — thousands of interacting feedback loops, redundancies, compensations, and time-dependencies — and it is treated as if it were a logical deduction.

### 1.2 — Why the leap fails: five structural reasons

This is the intellectual core. Explain each in depth, with a case.

**(a) The body is a system with feedback, not a machine with parts.**
Push on one node and the system pushes back. Suppress arrhythmias, and you may destabilise the myocardium in another way. Lower blood sugar aggressively, and you cause hypoglycaemia that kills. Raise HDL pharmacologically and you may also raise aldosterone and blood pressure (torcetrapib). **Analogy for the reader:** an ecosystem, not an engine. You do not fix an ecosystem by removing the wolves.

**(b) The marker is not the disease.**
The measurable correlate of a disease process is not the same thing as the process, and is often a *bystander*, or even a *compensation*. PVCs after a heart attack mark an unstable, scarred myocardium — they are the smoke, not the fire. Suppress the smoke and the fire is unaffected; if your smoke-suppressant is itself pro-arrhythmic, you have made things worse. **This is the single most transferable insight in the book.**

**(c) Mechanisms are always incomplete, and we cannot see the gaps.**
Any mechanism we can articulate is a cartoon of the real biology. The dangerous part is not what the cartoon shows; it is what the cartoon omits, and *we do not know what it omits*. Introduce the reader to **unknown unknowns** here — gently, without jargon.

**(d) Every mechanism has an equally plausible opposite.**
Teach this as a party trick that will change how they read papers forever. Take any intervention and construct a compelling mechanism for benefit and, in five minutes, an equally compelling mechanism for harm. Do it live on the page with three examples (beta-blockers in heart failure — "you're weakening a failing pump" vs. "you're rescuing it from catabolic adrenergic drive"; the second turned out right, but *not because it sounded better*). **Conclusion: if you can always generate a mechanism for either direction, mechanisms cannot be evidence of direction.**

**(e) Dose, timing, and population are invisible to mechanism.**
Mechanism tells you *direction*, never *magnitude*, *timing*, or *in whom*. Oestrogen may well be cardioprotective in a 50-year-old with clean arteries and harmful in a 70-year-old with unstable plaque. Mechanism cannot see that difference; trials can.

### 1.3 — The historical arc: how mechanism became king

Tell this as a story, because it explains why the profession is built this way.

- **Humoral medicine (Hippocrates → Galen → 1800s).** A complete, internally consistent mechanism: four humours, imbalance causes disease, therefore evacuate the excess. Bleeding, purging, blistering, sweating. **It was rational.** It was systematic. It was taught in universities for 1,700 years. It was wrong in every particular. *Emphasise: rationality is not the same as correctness. A perfectly logical system built on a false axiom produces confident, systematic harm at scale.*
- **The laboratory revolution (Virchow, Bernard, Koch, ~1850–1900).** Cellular pathology, experimental physiology, germ theory. Genuine, spectacular success. Mechanism *earned* its authority here — and then over-extended it.
- **The mid-20th-century golden age (~1935–1965).** Sulphonamides, penicillin, cortisone, insulin, streptomycin, polio vaccine. In this window, mechanistic reasoning worked repeatedly and dramatically. **This is the trauma that made the profession over-confident.** A generation learned that if you understand the biology, the cure follows.
- **The reckoning (1965– ).** The remaining diseases are chronic, multifactorial, and slow — heart disease, cancer, dementia, depression. Mechanistic reasoning performs terribly here, but the professional habits formed in the golden age persist.

**Teaching note for you:** This arc is the answer to "why do smart people keep doing this?" *Because it used to work.* Institutional habits outlive the conditions that justified them. Make the reader feel the tragedy of that.

### 1.4 — Case File: Gastric Freezing (1962–1969)

The cleanest small demonstration in medicine. Run the full Autopsy Protocol.

- **Claim:** Cooling the stomach with a balloon of refrigerated fluid reduces acid secretion and heals peptic ulcers.
- **Mechanism story:** Beautiful. Acid causes ulcers; cold suppresses secretory glands; therefore freeze the stomach. Non-surgical alternative to gastrectomy or vagotomy — a genuine humanitarian goal.
- **Authority:** Owen Wangensteen, Chair of Surgery at Minnesota, one of the most respected surgeons in America, published in *JAMA* in 1962.
- **Evidence at adoption:** Uncontrolled case series with impressive symptomatic improvement.
- **Spread:** Thousands of machines sold; procedure disseminated rapidly across the US.
- **Unravelling:** A multicentre, double-blind, **sham-controlled** trial (Ruffin et al., *NEJM*, 1969) in which the sham group received a balloon circulating fluid at body temperature. No difference.
- **Flaw named:** Mechanistic reasoning + no counterfactual + natural history of a relapsing-remitting disease + placebo/expectation effects on a subjective endpoint.
- **The transfer:** Everything with a device, a plausible mechanism, and a subjective endpoint.

Then the punchline for Chapter 25: **the acid mechanism itself was wrong.** Most peptic ulcers are caused by *Helicobacter pylori*. So they were freezing the wrong organ for the wrong reason to treat a bacterial infection. Let the reader sit with that.

### 1.5 — Case File: Internal Mammary Artery Ligation (1955–1960)

The trial that should have changed surgery forever, and didn't.

- **Claim:** Tying off the internal mammary arteries redirects blood to the heart and relieves angina.
- **Evidence at adoption:** Case series reporting ~three-quarters of patients improved. Patients threw away their nitroglycerine. Some showed *ECG improvement*.
- **Unravelling:** Leonard Cobb (*NEJM*, 1959) and Dimond (1960) performed **sham surgery** — skin incision, arteries exposed, then closed without ligation. The sham patients did just as well. In some reports, better.
- **The flaw:** Angina is a subjective, fluctuating symptom. Surgery is the most powerful placebo humans have devised — ritual, cost, danger, authority, commitment, and a scar as proof.
- **The lesson that wasn't learned:** It took another **fifty years** before sham-controlled surgical trials became even occasionally routine (vertebroplasty, 2009; meniscectomy, 2013; ORBITA, 2018). Ask the reader directly: *why?* Answer honestly — ethics committees, surgeon resistance, funding, and the deep professional conviction that "we can see that it works."

**Word budget:** 9,000
**Reader exercise:** Choose any intervention you believe in. Write the most persuasive one-paragraph mechanism for why it *harms* patients. Notice how easy it was.

## CHAPTER 2 — The Body Heals Itself
### *The three impostors: natural history, regression to the mean, and expectation*

**The Argument:** Most patients get better. Any treatment given to most patients will therefore appear to work. Distinguishing real effect from this background of spontaneous improvement is the founding problem of therapeutics — and for most of history we simply didn't.

**Why this chapter exists:** It explains the *baseline illusion* on top of which every other error is built. Chapter 1 explains why we adopt; Chapter 2 explains why we never notice we were wrong.

### 2.1 — The self-limiting majority

Teach the epidemiology of getting better: most acute illness resolves; most symptoms fluctuate; most chronic disease has relapses and remissions. Then the killer inference: **any therapy applied at the peak of symptoms will be followed by improvement in the majority of cases, with a probability approaching certainty.**

Give the reader a number to hold: if 70% of patients with condition X improve within a fortnight regardless, then a clinician who treats 100 such patients will see 70 successes and form an unshakeable conviction after about a dozen.

### 2.2 — Regression to the mean, taught properly

Do not hand-wave this. It is the most under-appreciated concept in clinical reasoning and you should spend 2,500 words on it.

- **The statistical fact:** any measurement with random variation, selected because it was extreme, will on average be less extreme next time.
- **The clinical consequence:** patients present when they are at their worst. That is *definitionally* an extreme value. Their next measurement will usually be better *no matter what you do.*
- **Where it bites hardest:** blood pressure, pain scores, depression scales, liver enzymes, "difficult" behaviour in dementia, symptom flares in autoimmune disease.
- **The vicious version:** it makes *harmful* treatments look neutral and *neutral* treatments look curative.
- **The teaching demonstration:** Kahneman's flight instructors — praise a good landing and the next is worse; scream at a bad landing and the next is better. The instructors concluded that punishment works and praise doesn't. They had discovered nothing but arithmetic.

### 2.3 — Expectation, ritual, and the placebo family

Be precise, because this area is full of overclaiming — and correcting overclaiming *about* placebo is itself a lovely demonstration of the book's method.

Break the "placebo response" into its real components:
1. **Natural history** (would have improved anyway)
2. **Regression to the mean** (measurement artefact)
3. **Response bias / demand characteristics** (patients report improvement to please, or because they expect to)
4. **Genuine psychobiological effect** (real, mediated by expectation and conditioning; robust for pain, nausea, anxiety, Parkinsonian motor signs; weak-to-absent for tumour size, mortality, infection)

Then: Beecher (1955) attributed the whole bundle to component 4. Hróbjartsson & Gøtzsche (*NEJM*, 2001), by comparing placebo arms against *untreated* arms, showed the genuine placebo effect is much smaller than folklore — meaningful for subjective outcomes, essentially absent for objective ones.

**Teaching note:** Emphasise the *gradient* — the more subjective the endpoint, the more your uncontrolled observation is worthless. This is the rule that predicts, in advance, which specialties will accumulate the most reversals. Pain medicine, psychiatry, and elective orthopaedics live at the dangerous end. Say so, and say why.

### 2.4 — The ritual amplifiers

Why some treatments produce larger apparent effects than others, independent of biology:
- **Invasiveness** (injection > pill; surgery > injection)
- **Cost and scarcity**
- **Elaborateness of ritual**
- **Practitioner confidence and warmth**
- **Colour, size, and branding of pills** (yes, really — cite the literature; it delights readers and makes the point unforgettable)
- **The patient's own investment** (effort justification; cognitive dissonance reduction)

**The Deep Why:** These amplifiers are not curiosities. They explain *why the most aggressive and expensive interventions generate the most confident practitioners.* The surgeon is more certain than the physiotherapist, and the reason is not that surgery works better. Land that sentence hard.

### 2.5 — Case File: Bloodletting and the Numerical Method

- **Two thousand years** of the most widespread therapy in Western medicine.
- Why it survived: coherent mechanism (humours), visible immediate "effect" (the patient becomes calm, pale, bradycardic — read as *improvement*), self-limiting disease, no counterfactual, and profound authority.
- **Benjamin Rush**, Philadelphia yellow fever epidemic of 1793: heroic depletion, enormous volumes. He kept records. His records showed his patients doing well — because he counted the survivors and attributed deaths to the disease's severity, i.e., **motivated attribution**, a bias you'll formally name in Chapter 8.
- **Pierre Charles Alexandre Louis**, Paris, 1820s–1830s: the *méthode numérique*. He simply **counted**. He compared pneumonia patients bled early against bled late. The results did not favour bleeding.
- **What happened next is the important part:** Louis was resisted, on the grounds that averages destroy the individuality of the patient and that statistics are the enemy of clinical art. **This objection is still made today, in nearly the same words.** Quote both, centuries apart, side by side.

**Word budget:** 8,000
**Reader exercise:** Design an uncontrolled study that would make an entirely inert treatment for migraine look 80% effective. List every mechanism you exploited.

## CHAPTER 3 — Authority, Eminence, and the Architecture of Deference

**The Argument:** Medicine's transmission of knowledge is structurally hierarchical, which is efficient for teaching skills and catastrophic for correcting errors.

**Why this chapter exists:** It moves the analysis from the individual mind to the social structure — the bridge from psychology to sociology that the book needs before Part II's institutional chapters.

### 3.1 — Why hierarchy exists in medicine, and why it's mostly good

Be fair before you're critical. Apprenticeship is the right model for transmitting tacit skill — you cannot learn to feel a liver edge from a textbook. Hierarchy also allocates responsibility, and in an emergency a clear chain of command saves lives.

### 3.2 — Where it becomes pathological

- **Eminence-based medicine** — the practice pattern of the most senior person becomes the local standard, unexamined, and propagates through their trainees for a generation.
- **The lineage effect** — trace a practice back and find it originates with one charismatic figure. *Halsted's radical mastectomy propagated through Halsted's residents through Halsted's residents' residents for eighty years.*
- **The chilling of dissent** — the junior doctor who has read the trial does not contradict the professor who has not. Quantify the cost with the aviation comparison: crew resource management transformed aviation safety in the 1980s precisely by attacking cockpit gradient. Medicine started thirty years later and is not finished.
- **Credential-as-argument** — the substitution of *who said it* for *what the evidence is*.

### 3.3 — The Semmelweis Reflex

Name it, then earn it with the full case in Chapter 17. Here, define the pattern:

> The reflexive rejection of a finding because accepting it would require accepting responsibility for prior harm.

**The Deep Why — and I want you to write this carefully, because it is subtle and it is the most humane insight in your book:**

Semmelweis's colleagues were not merely arrogant. His finding required them to accept that **they had personally killed their patients** — that the hands they used to heal had carried death from the autopsy room to the delivery bed. That is not an intellectual proposition. That is an unbearable one.

The strength of resistance to a new idea is proportional to the *moral cost of accepting it*, not to the weakness of its evidence.

This principle recurs constantly: the surgeons resisting sham trials, the oncologists resisting the ABMT results, the physicians resisting the WHI. **The better a doctor is, the more patients they have treated, and therefore the more they have to lose by being wrong.** Excellence and volume increase resistance to correction. That is a devastating structural insight — write it as such.

### 3.4 — Planck's Principle, actually tested

The quotation: *"A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents... but rather because its opponents eventually die."*

Then the delight: **it has been empirically tested.** Azoulay, Fons-Rosen & Graff Zivin (*American Economic Review: Insights*, 2019) examined what happens in a scientific subfield after the unexpected death of an eminent researcher. Publications by the star's collaborators decline; publications by *outsiders* to the field rise substantially, and these are disproportionately novel and highly cited.

Science does, measurably, advance one funeral at a time. Let the reader feel how bleak and how useful that finding is.

### 3.5 — Guidelines: authority, industrialised

Preview Chapter 13. Guidelines are how eminence scales. Cover: how panels are constituted, how conflicts of interest are handled (and mishandled), how a weak recommendation becomes a quality metric becomes a legal standard becomes a reimbursement condition — and how, at the end of that chain, a practice becomes *mandatory* on evidence that was never strong enough to justify it being *optional*.

**Word budget:** 7,000
**Reader exercise:** Identify one practice in your own field that you do because you were taught to. Trace it back. Who taught your teacher? What was the original evidence? Most readers cannot get three steps. That is the finding.

## CHAPTER 4 — The Tyranny of the Vivid Case

**The Argument:** Human memory is organised around narrative and salience, not frequency — so clinical experience is a systematically biased sample of reality.

### 4.1 — Why "in my experience" is not evidence

Teach the reader exactly how clinical experience is corrupted, step by step. This is a *mechanism* chapter about the doctor's mind.

1. **You do not see your denominators.** You remember the patient who improved; you never meet the 60 who would have improved anyway, because they are indistinguishable.
2. **You do not see your failures.** Patients who don't improve go elsewhere, or die, or stop coming. **Follow-up is a filter, and it filters in your favour.**
3. **You do not see your harms.** The GI bleed occurs six months later, in another hospital, attributed to something else. The stroke in a patient on the drug you started is "just bad luck." Harms are dispersed in time, space, and attribution; benefits are concentrated at the bedside.
4. **You over-remember the dramatic.** Availability heuristic: the patient who nearly died and recovered after your intervention is worth 200 unremarkable cases in memory weight.
5. **You interpret ambiguous outcomes favourably.** Motivated reasoning is invisible from the inside — that is its definition.

### 4.2 — The n=1 fallacy and the therapeutic illusion

Introduce **the therapeutic illusion**: the clinician's belief that their actions are more effective than they are. It's an old term (Thomas, 1978) and a perfect one. Give it a chapter-defining sentence:

> Every clinician's experience is a case series with no control group, no blinding, no pre-specified endpoint, no complete follow-up, and an assessor with a personal stake in the result. If you submitted it as a study, it would be desk-rejected.

### 4.3 — The uses of the case report

Be fair. Case reports are not worthless — they are the correct tool for a specific job:
- **Detecting the unprecedented** (the first cluster of *Pneumocystis* pneumonia in young gay men, 1981 — the beginning of AIDS)
- **Detecting the rare and dramatic harm** (phocomelia and thalidomide; clear cell adenocarcinoma and DES)
- **Generating hypotheses**

Teach the rule: **case reports are excellent at detecting rare effects with a distinctive signature and short latency; they are useless for common outcomes with a long latency.** That single sentence explains why thalidomide was caught in four years and why hormone replacement therapy took fifty. Make the reader see the symmetry.

### 4.4 — Case File: The Missed Aortic Dissection

Use a composite (or a published) diagnostic error case to show availability, anchoring, and premature closure operating in real time, minute by minute, in one clinician's head. Write it as a thriller. Then rewind and annotate.

**Word budget:** 6,000

## CHAPTER 5 — The Moral Engine: The Need to Act

**The Argument:** Medicine's core ethical drive — *do something for the suffering person in front of you* — is itself a primary generator of error.

**Why this chapter exists:** This is the chapter that makes the book *humane* rather than clever. The errors come from the same place as the virtue.

### 5.1 — Commission bias and the therapeutic imperative

Define **commission bias**: the tendency toward action rather than inaction, particularly under pressure or uncertainty. Contrast with **omission bias** in the general population — laypeople prefer inaction; clinicians are trained out of it.

Then the crucial asymmetry, revisited from 0.2.3: **the harm of acting is invisible; the harm of not acting is on the chart with your name next to it.**

### 5.2 — Desperation as an epistemic solvent

The stronger the suffering, the weaker the evidence required. Chart this explicitly — it's one of the book's best diagrams:

| Clinical situation | Evidence demanded | Historical result |
|---|---|---|
| Mild self-limiting illness | High | Relatively few disasters |
| Chronic disabling disease | Moderate | Many reversals |
| Terminal illness, young patient | Near zero | ABMT for breast cancer; laetrile; unproven stem-cell clinics |
| Public health emergency | Near zero | Hydroxychloroquine 2020 |

**The Deep Why:** This is *rational* at the individual level and *catastrophic* at the population level. A dying patient with nothing to lose should try the unproven thing. But when a whole system adopts that logic, it stops generating the knowledge that would have saved the next cohort. **The tragedy of desperate medicine is that it consumes the patients who could have answered the question.** ABMT is the definitive case: trials couldn't recruit *because women could get the treatment off-trial*, so the question took a decade longer to answer, so tens of thousands more women were harmed. Write that paragraph as the emotional climax of Part I.

### 5.3 — "We had to do something"

Examine this sentence forensically. It is the most common justification in the book, and it contains a hidden and false premise: that action dominates inaction. Teach the reader to hear the missing clause: *"We had to do something — and doing nothing was not an option — even though we had no reason to believe this something would help and several reasons to think it might harm."*

### 5.4 — Hope, marketing, and the exploitation of desperation

Bridge into the commercial chapters. Desperation is a market. Direct-to-consumer stem cell clinics, unproven cancer therapies, right-to-try legislation, accelerated approval. Be nuanced: some of this is genuine compassion badly implemented, some is predation. Distinguish them.

**Word budget:** 7,000

## CHAPTER 6 — Coherence and the Story-Shaped Mind

**The Argument:** Humans accept explanations that are coherent, not explanations that are supported. Medicine's greatest errors were its most beautiful stories.

### 6.1 — Narrative as a truth-substitute

Kahneman's WYSIATI — *what you see is all there is.* The mind builds the best available story from the available fragments and does not represent the fragments it lacks. **Coherence is generated by the absence of contradicting information, so the less you know, the more confident you feel.** Write that sentence and then give it a case.

### 6.2 — The completeness illusion in pathophysiology

Medical education *teaches* coherent stories. Every disease is presented as a chain: aetiology → pathogenesis → pathology → clinical features → treatment. That's excellent pedagogy and terrible epistemology, because it trains students to feel that a mechanism they can *recite* is a mechanism that is *established*. Distinguish, explicitly, between:
- what has been demonstrated in humans,
- what has been demonstrated in animals,
- what has been demonstrated in cells,
- and what is simply a story that connects the above.

Most textbook pathophysiology is category four wearing the clothes of category one.

### 6.3 — Case File: The Amyloid Hypothesis

A live, unresolved, magnificently instructive case — handle it with real care and fairness, because it is contested.

- **The story:** amyloid-beta plaques are found in Alzheimer's brains; rare genetic forms involve amyloid processing genes; therefore amyloid causes Alzheimer's; therefore removing amyloid should treat it.
- **The coherence:** enormous. Genetics, pathology, animal models, and biochemistry all lined up.
- **The investment:** decades, tens of billions of dollars, an entire field's career structure.
- **The record:** a long series of failed anti-amyloid trials. Then accelerated approval of aducanumab in 2021 on the *surrogate* of plaque reduction, over the near-unanimous objection of the FDA's own advisory committee; three committee members resigned. Subsequent agents (lecanemab, donanemab) show plaque clearance and statistically significant but clinically modest slowing on cognitive scales, with meaningful rates of ARIA (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities: brain oedema and microhaemorrhage).
- **The complication:** in 2022, allegations of image manipulation in a highly cited 2006 *Nature* paper on the Aβ*56 oligomer — a paper that had shaped a subfield.
- **The honest verdict:** *not yet a reversal.* This is the chapter where you teach the reader to sit inside an unresolved question without collapsing it into a verdict. Write the strongest case for the hypothesis and the strongest case against, and refuse to resolve it. **That intellectual restraint will teach the reader more than any conclusion could.**

### 6.4 — Why beautiful theories are more dangerous than ugly ones

Close Part I here. A beautiful theory:
- attracts talent and funding,
- generates a research programme that produces confirmatory findings (because you find what you look for),
- makes contradicting data look like technical failure rather than refutation,
- and builds careers that must be defended.

Ugly, partial, messy hypotheses do none of this. **The elegance of an idea is a risk factor, not a credential.** Put that on the last line of Part I.

**Word budget:** 8,000
**Part I capstone exercise:** Take one current, widely accepted treatment. Write two pages: the mechanism story, the coherence sources, the authority lineage, the vividness of its successes, the moral pressure to use it. Then, honestly, list the direct human-outcome evidence. Compare the lengths of the two lists.

# PART II — THE MACHINERY OF ERROR
## A Complete Taxonomy of How Medical Thinking Fails

> *Word budget: ~70,000. Nine chapters. This is the book's reference core — the part readers will return to. Structure it so it can be used as a lookup as well as read straight through.*

**The job of Part II:** Part I explained the *motives* for belief. Part II supplies the *mechanics* of error. By the end, the reader owns a complete diagnostic vocabulary and can name what went wrong in any case they encounter — including cases you never mention.

**Organising principle — the four levels of failure.** Present them as a stack, because errors at each level are invisible from the level below:

```
LEVEL 4  STRUCTURAL   Money, careers, publication, regulation, law     (Ch 14–15)
LEVEL 3  SOCIAL       Groups, hierarchies, institutions, guidelines    (Ch 13)
LEVEL 2  METHODOLOGICAL  Study design, measurement, statistics         (Ch 9–12)
LEVEL 1  COGNITIVE    The individual mind                              (Ch 7–8)
```

**Teaching note:** insist that the levels *interact*. A cognitive bias alone rarely causes a disaster; a cognitive bias amplified by a financial incentive, laundered through a flawed study design, and ratified by a guideline panel — that causes a disaster. Every major case file in Part III should be shown operating on **at least three of the four levels**. Make that an explicit analytical demand.

## CHAPTER 7 — The Two Systems: Cognitive Architecture for Clinicians

**The Argument:** Clinical reasoning runs on two modes with different failure signatures; most diagnostic error is not ignorance but a mode mismatch.

### 7.1 — Dual-process theory, plainly

- **System 1** — fast, pattern-matching, effortless, emotional, always on. In medicine: *illness scripts*, the gestalt, "this child looks septic." Indispensable. An experienced clinician's System 1 is a genuine marvel and is right most of the time.
- **System 2** — slow, analytic, effortful, sequential, easily exhausted. In medicine: differential diagnosis, Bayesian updating, reading a paper properly.

Be careful and honest with the reader: dual-process theory is a **useful model, not a neuroanatomical fact**, and parts of the underlying psychology literature have had their own replication problems. Say so. (Another chance to model the method: a book about medical error that cites psychology uncritically is being hypocritical.)

### 7.2 — Croskerry's clinical framing

Introduce Pat Croskerry's work on cognitive dispositions to respond (CDRs) and cognitive forcing strategies. The key clinical insight: **error is usually not from lack of knowledge but from failure to deploy knowledge already held.** The doctor who missed the pulmonary embolism could have passed an exam on pulmonary embolism that morning.

### 7.3 — Why experts fail differently from novices

An elegant, counterintuitive section:
- Novices fail from **ignorance** — they don't know the pattern.
- Experts fail from **over-fitting** — they know the pattern too well and see it where it isn't; their System 1 is so strong it pre-empts System 2 before the atypical presentation registers.
- Therefore expertise reduces the *frequency* of error and increases the *confidence* of the errors that remain. **The dangerous doctor is not the one who knows nothing; it's the one who knows a great deal and is never uncertain.**

### 7.4 — Cognitive load, fatigue, and the systems view

Error rates rise with hours worked, patients per hour, interruptions, night shifts, and end-of-list fatigue. Cite the literature on decision fatigue in prescribing (antibiotic prescribing rises across a clinic session; so do inappropriate opioid prescriptions). **Consequence: some of what looks like bad thinking is bad scheduling.** This matters enormously for Part VII — the fix is often environmental, not educational.

**Word budget:** 6,000

## CHAPTER 8 — The Bias Atlas
### *A field guide to the cognitive dispositions that harm patients*

**The Argument:** These are not curiosities from a psychology textbook; each has a body count.

**Format instruction:** Write each entry to a fixed template — **Name / One-line definition / How it feels from inside / Clinical case / Historical case / Detection cue / Countermeasure.** Roughly 500–700 words each. The fixed template makes this chapter usable as a reference and prevents you writing 30 essays of wildly varying quality.

### 8.1 — Biases of *acquisition* (what information you gather)

- **Anchoring** — first impression dominates; subsequent data are pulled toward it. *Triage note says "anxiety"; the PE is missed.*
- **Availability** — probability judged by ease of recall. *You over-diagnose the condition you saw last week, or that killed someone memorably.*
- **Search satisficing** — you stop looking after the first finding. *The second fracture on the radiograph.*
- **Framing effect** — the same facts, differently presented, produce different decisions. *"90% survival" vs "10% mortality" changes both patient and physician choices.*
- **Ascertainment bias** — you find what you expect. Includes **stereotyping** and its clinical harms; be concrete about documented disparities in analgesia, cardiac workup, and symptom credibility.

### 8.2 — Biases of *interpretation* (what you do with information)

- **Confirmation bias** — seeking and weighting evidence that supports the working hypothesis. The queen of biases; give it double length.
- **Premature closure** — the diagnosis is accepted before it's verified. Croskerry's line: *"when the diagnosis is made, the thinking stops."*
- **Diagnosis momentum** — a label acquired in triage or in a referral letter hardens as it passes through hands, losing its qualifiers at every step.
- **Base-rate neglect** — ignoring how common the disease actually is. Pair with 0.3.11.
- **Representativeness** — judging by resemblance to the textbook picture; misses atypical presentations, which is where the deaths are.
- **Motivated reasoning** — the conclusion is chosen first; the reasoning is retrofitted. Emphasise it is **invisible from inside**; that's the definition, not an insult.
- **Sunk cost** — persisting because of prior investment. Operates on careers and research programmes, not just on individual patients.
- **Hindsight bias** — see 0.4; here, note its specific poison in morbidity and mortality meetings, where it makes reasonable decisions look negligent and thereby destroys the learning value of the exercise.

### 8.3 — Biases of *action*

- **Commission bias** — bias toward doing. Revisit from Chapter 5 with data.
- **Omission bias** — in patients and in policy; a harm caused by action feels worse than an equal harm caused by inaction. Explains vaccine hesitancy's psychology better than ignorance does. Be respectful, be precise; you will need this in Chapter 45.
- **The technological imperative** — if the capability exists, it will be used. Once the scanner is installed, it must be filled. Once the robot is bought, the robot must operate.
- **Therapeutic illusion** — Chapter 4.2.
- **Illusion of control** — the conviction that monitoring and titrating produces better outcomes than a simple protocol. Pair with the Swan-Ganz catheter case (Ch 21).

### 8.4 — Biases of *self-assessment*

- **Overconfidence** — the calibration literature: clinicians' confidence and accuracy correlate weakly. Cite the studies comparing stated diagnostic confidence with autopsy findings.
- **Blind spot bias** — everyone believes they are less biased than their colleagues. Include this explicitly, because your reader is currently thinking they'd have spotted it. Say so, by name, on the page. It's the most effective single paragraph you can write in this chapter.
- A note of caution on Dunning–Kruger: the popular version overstates and partly misrepresents the original finding, and the effect is partly a statistical artefact. **Don't use it lazily** — using a shaky psychology result to criticise shaky medical results would undermine your whole project.

### 8.5 — Why debiasing mostly doesn't work
### *(and what does)*

An honest and important section. Teaching people about biases produces small and inconsistent improvements in real-world performance. Knowing about anchoring does not stop you anchoring.

What has better support:
- **Changing the environment**, not the mind: checklists, forced fields, default settings, order-set design, removing an option.
- **Structural redundancy**: second reads, independent double-checks, radiology double-reporting.
- **Feedback loops**: clinicians almost never learn their diagnostic accuracy because they never learn the outcome. Fix the loop and calibration improves.
- **Slowing down at pre-identified points** (cognitive forcing at handover, at discharge, at "I'm about to discharge someone with chest pain").

**The Deep Why:** This section is the intellectual bridge to Part VII and it must be here, in Part II, or your book becomes a self-help manual. **The lesson of the last thirty years of safety science is that individual virtue is a weak lever and system design is a strong one.** Medicine's instinct — to respond to error by exhorting doctors to try harder — is itself a cognitive bias (the fundamental attribution error, applied institutionally).

**Word budget:** 12,000
**Reader exercise:** Take a diagnostic error from your own experience (or a published case). Identify three biases operating simultaneously and specify, for each, the *earliest* point at which a system-level intervention could have caught it.

## CHAPTER 9 — Illusions of Number: How Statistics Deceives Honest People

**The Argument:** Statistical methods were built to protect us from chance, and they have been repurposed — mostly unintentionally — into machinery for manufacturing false certainty.

**Tone instruction:** Zero equations. Every idea gets a physical analogy. If the reader can't explain it to a friend at dinner, you failed.

### 9.1 — The p-value: origin, meaning, and corruption

- What it actually says (see 0.3.10), stated three ways until it sticks.
- **The five things people think it means, all wrong.** Write them as a list and demolish each. This is one of the most valuable pages you can produce.
- Fisher's 0.05: a convenience, offered as a rough guide, hardened into a wall by the needs of journals and regulators.
- **Why the wall is where the damage happens:** it converts a continuous measure of surprise into a binary verdict, and binary verdicts create incentives to cross a line.

### 9.2 — Power, and the underappreciated tragedy of the small study

- Define power in plain terms: the probability of detecting an effect that is really there.
- **Most medical studies are underpowered.** Consequence one (well known): they miss real effects. Consequence two (poorly known and far more damaging): **among underpowered studies, the ones that reach significance systematically overestimate the effect size.** Teach this with the "winner's curse" analogy. To cross the significance line with a small sample, you need a lucky, exaggerated result. So the published literature of small studies is a museum of overestimates.
- This single point explains: why early trials look great and later trials disappoint; why meta-analyses shrink effects; why preclinical results don't translate. Give it space.

### 9.3 — Multiplicity: the garden of forking paths

- Twenty tests at p<0.05 yields one "finding" from pure noise.
- **Subgroups.** The definitive teaching case: **ISIS-2** (1988), a landmark trial showing aspirin and streptokinase reduce mortality after myocardial infarction. The investigators, to make the point about subgroup analysis, reported the results split by *astrological birth sign* — and found aspirin appeared not to work for those born under Gemini and Libra. Nobody believes the stars affect aspirin. **Ask the reader why they believe the diabetic subgroup.**
- **Multiple endpoints, multiple time points, multiple analytic choices.** Introduce "researcher degrees of freedom" (Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, 2011) and the *garden of forking paths* (Gelman & Loken): you don't need to cheat; you just need to make reasonable choices, sequentially, in the light of the data.
- **The countermeasure:** pre-registration and pre-specified analysis plans. Explain *why* they work — they close the garden's gates before you can see where the paths lead.

### 9.4 — Composite endpoints and the arithmetic of hype

How "reduces major adverse cardiovascular events" can mean "reduces hospitalisation for chest pain, with no effect whatsoever on death or infarction." Teach the reader to always decompose a composite, and to check whether the components are of comparable importance and moving in the same direction. **The rule: a composite is only honest if its components are of similar severity and similar frequency.** Most are not.

### 9.5 — Early stopping, fragility, and other ways to break a good trial

- **Truncation for benefit.** Trials stopped early because the interim result looks good systematically overestimate effects (Montori et al., *JAMA*, 2005). Why: you're most likely to hit the stopping boundary during a random upward fluctuation. Same logic as 9.2 — teach it as a variation on one theme, not as new material.
- **The fragility index.** How many patients would have to switch outcome to erase significance? Often two or three, in trials that changed practice worldwide. A wonderfully concrete way to make statistical uncertainty tangible.
- **Post-hoc reclassification, endpoint switching, per-protocol substitution.** Introduce the COMPare project's findings on outcome-switching in published trials.

### 9.6 — Intention-to-treat, per-protocol, and the honest denominator

Why analysing "everyone as randomised" is the conservative and correct default; why per-protocol analysis re-introduces exactly the confounding randomisation removed; the healthy-adherer evidence from placebo arms as the killer demonstration.

### 9.7 — Ioannidis's argument, explained for a beginner

Walk through *"Why Most Published Research Findings Are False"* (2005) without algebra. The logic chain:
1. In any field, only some hypotheses tested are true.
2. Type I error, low power, bias, and multiplicity all inflate false positives.
3. Journals publish positive results preferentially.
4. Therefore the **positive predictive value** of a published finding depends on the field's prior plausibility, its typical power, and its level of bias and competition.
5. In fields with low priors, small studies, flexible designs, and many competing teams — most published positives are false.

Then, essential for fairness: note the critiques, and note that this is an argument about *expected proportions under assumptions*, not a measurement. **Show the reader you can apply the book's scepticism to the book's own heroes.**

**Word budget:** 11,000
**Reader exercise:** Given a reported trial, compute: absolute risk reduction, NNT, and the fragility index. Then rewrite the press release honestly.

## CHAPTER 10 — Bias in Design: Why Studies Lie Even When Nobody Does

**The Argument:** Systematic bias, not chance, is responsible for the great reversals. Randomisation is not a bureaucratic formality; it is the only reliable defence against a specific and very powerful class of illusion.

### 10.1 — The hierarchy of evidence, taught properly (and then criticised)

Present the ladder — expert opinion → case report → case series → ecological → cross-sectional → case-control → cohort → RCT → systematic review of RCTs — and explain **what each design controls for and what it cannot**.

Then be sophisticated: the hierarchy is a heuristic, not a law. A well-conducted cohort beats a badly conducted RCT. Some questions cannot be randomised (parachutes; smoking; rare harms). GRADE exists precisely to let evidence be rated up and down on its merits. **A reader who worships the hierarchy has learned dogma, not thinking.**

### 10.2 — Confounding, in full

- The general form.
- **Confounding by indication** — the great destroyer of observational treatment research. Worked example: in observational data, patients receiving mechanical ventilation die more often than those who don't. Ventilators do not kill people; being unable to breathe does.
- **Confounding by contraindication** — the mirror image; people too sick to receive the treatment are excluded, making the treated group look healthy.
- **Healthy user / healthy adherer** — the full anatomy, because it is the whole HRT story.
- **Residual confounding** — why "we adjusted for it" is a promise, not a solution; you can only adjust for what you measured, at the precision you measured it. Introduce measurement error in confounders as the reason adjustment systematically under-corrects.

### 10.3 — Time-related biases (the subtle killers)

These deserve their own section because they are the ones that fool sophisticated researchers.

- **Immortal time bias** — patients must survive long enough to receive the treatment, so the treated group has guaranteed survival time built into its definition. This is the technical explanation for the famous "Oscar winners live longer" result and for a long series of spurious drug benefits in database studies.
- **Lead-time bias** — earlier diagnosis lengthens measured survival without postponing death by a single day. Absolutely essential for Chapter 12; introduce here with a clock diagram.
- **Length-time bias** — screening preferentially catches slow-growing disease, because fast-growing disease appears and kills between screens. Screen-detected cancers are therefore a *biased sample of nicer cancers*.
- **Prevalent user bias** — studying people already established on a drug excludes everyone who stopped because of harm.

### 10.4 — Selection and information bias

- Volunteer/healthy-volunteer effects; the "worried well."
- **Berkson's bias** and collider stratification, taught with a diagram, because it's the most counterintuitive: *conditioning on a common effect creates a spurious association between its causes.* Give the modern, live example — the "obesity paradox," and the collider-bias explanations offered for early COVID risk-factor findings drawn from hospitalised samples.
- **Differential misclassification**, recall bias, interviewer bias.
- **Loss to follow-up** as a silent randomisation-destroyer.

### 10.5 — Blinding: who must not know, and why

Enumerate the five parties that can break a trial: patient, treating clinician, outcome assessor, data analyst, and the committee adjudicating events. Explain what each contributes. Explain **why blinding matters most for subjective outcomes and least for all-cause mortality** — and therefore why all-cause mortality is the endpoint you can trust from an imperfect trial. Teach the rule: *the softer the endpoint, the more the blinding matters.*

### 10.6 — The unblinding problem in practice

Drugs with obvious side effects unblind themselves. Ask how many "double-blind" trials of beta-blockers, opioids, or SSRIs were functionally unblinded by their own pharmacology, and what the active-placebo literature suggests about this. Honest, unresolved, and important.

### 10.7 — Simpson's paradox and the ecological fallacy

Two short, vivid sections with worked numbers. Simpson's paradox: an effect that reverses direction when groups are combined (use the Berkeley admissions example, then a clinical one). Ecological fallacy: population-level correlations do not license individual-level inference — the historical engine behind the dietary fat wars.

**Word budget:** 11,000

## CHAPTER 11 — Measuring the Wrong Thing
### *Surrogates, composites, and the outcomes that matter to nobody*

**The Argument:** More people have been harmed by measuring the wrong outcome well than by measuring the right outcome badly.

**Why this chapter exists:** It is the single most predictive chapter in the book. If your reader learns to ask *"is that a surrogate?"* automatically, they will anticipate reversals before they happen.

### 11.1 — What a surrogate is and why we use them

Honest first: surrogates are not a scam. They are used because they are **faster, cheaper, smaller, and earlier**. Waiting for mortality endpoints in a prevention trial can take a decade and 20,000 patients. Nobody adopted surrogates out of laziness; they adopted them out of arithmetic.

### 11.2 — The three conditions for a valid surrogate

Teach Prentice's criteria in plain English, and stress the third:
1. The surrogate must correlate with the outcome. *(Easy, and nearly worthless on its own.)*
2. The treatment must affect the surrogate. *(Easy.)*
3. **The treatment's entire effect on the outcome must be mediated through the surrogate.** *(Almost never verifiable in advance, and this is where every disaster lives.)*

**The Deep Why — write this as the chapter's centrepiece:** Condition 3 fails whenever a drug does *anything else*. And every drug does something else. Torcetrapib raised HDL beautifully and also raised blood pressure and aldosterone; the ILLUMINATE trial was stopped in December 2006 for **increased mortality**. Encainide and flecainide suppressed PVCs beautifully and were proarrhythmic. Fluoride increased bone density and increased fractures, because the new bone was brittle. Erythropoietin corrected anaemia and increased strokes and deaths at higher haemoglobin targets.

Then state the law of the chapter:

> **A surrogate captures the effect you were hoping for. It is structurally blind to the effect you weren't.** That asymmetry is not a bug in a particular surrogate; it is the nature of surrogates.

### 11.3 — The surrogate hall of infamy

Build this as a table — it will be one of the most photographed pages in your book.

| Surrogate | Drug/intervention | Surrogate result | Patient outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| PVC suppression | Encainide, flecainide | Excellent | ↑ mortality (CAST, 1989) |
| HDL cholesterol | Torcetrapib | ↑ 72% | ↑ mortality (ILLUMINATE, 2006) |
| Bone mineral density | Fluoride | ↑ | ↑ non-vertebral fractures |
| Haemoglobin | Erythropoietin, high targets | Normalised | ↑ death/stroke (CHOIR, CREATE, TREAT) |
| Blood glucose | Intensive control in T2DM | ↓ HbA1c | ↑ mortality (ACCORD, 2008) |
| Carotid intima-media thickness | Ezetimibe (ENHANCE) | No change | *Later* modest benefit (IMPROVE-IT) |
| Tumour shrinkage / PFS | Bevacizumab in breast cancer | ↑ PFS | No OS benefit; approval revoked 2011 |
| Amyloid plaque | Aducanumab | ↓ plaque | Contested; ARIA harms |
| Oxygen saturation | Liberal O₂ in MI | Normalised | No benefit, possible harm (AVOID, DETO2X) |

**Then, for fairness, the surrogates that worked:** HIV viral load, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol (largely), HbA1c *for microvascular* outcomes, INR. Ask the reader the hard question: **could you have told them apart in advance?** Make them try. Then reveal: not reliably. Which is the whole point.

### 11.4 — Composite endpoints, patient-reported outcomes, and the MCID

- Composites: revisit 9.4 with the emphasis here on *importance-weighting*.
- **Patient-reported outcomes**: good in principle, easily gamed in practice — instrument selection, scale manipulation, and the problem that unblinded subjective outcomes are barely evidence at all.
- **The minimal clinically important difference.** Statistically significant ≠ noticeable to a human. A 2-point change on a 100-point scale can have p<0.001 in a large trial and be undetectable in a life. Teach the reader to always ask: *would the patient notice?*

### 11.5 — Who chooses the outcome, and why that's a political act

Bridge into Chapter 14. The choice of endpoint determines the answer. Sponsors choose endpoints. Introduce **core outcome sets** (COMET initiative) as the reform, and patient involvement in endpoint selection as the deeper one.

**Word budget:** 9,000

## CHAPTER 12 — The Logic of Screening
### *Why finding disease early can hurt you*

**The Argument:** Screening is where good intentions meet the most counterintuitive mathematics in medicine, and it is the largest domain of ongoing, unrecognised iatrogenic harm.

**Why this chapter exists:** It is the hardest single idea in the book for a lay reader and the one with the greatest present-day relevance. Give it a full chapter and teach it slowly.

### 12.1 — The intuition that must be dismantled

State the reader's belief plainly and sympathetically: *finding cancer early saves lives; therefore more screening is better.* Then say: the first clause is sometimes true; the second does not follow; and the reasons why are the substance of this chapter.

### 12.2 — Lead time: the illusion of longer survival

Diagram it. A person destined to die at 70 whose cancer is found at 60 instead of 67 now "survives 10 years instead of 3." **Nothing has changed except the start of the clock.** Teach the corollary: **five-year survival is a corrupted metric for screened diseases, and comparisons of survival across countries with different screening intensities are nearly meaningless.** The honest metric is mortality per population, not survival per diagnosed case.

### 12.3 — Length time: the biased sample

Fast, lethal cancers spend little time in the detectable-but-asymptomatic window; slow, indolent ones spend years there. **Screening therefore preferentially catches the cancers least likely to kill you.** Make the reader see that this makes screening look good on every metric except the one that matters.

### 12.4 — Overdiagnosis: the extreme case of length time

The hardest idea. Define it precisely:

> **Overdiagnosis:** the detection of a genuine abnormality — histologically real cancer — that would never have caused symptoms or death in that person's lifetime.

Stress: this is *not* a false positive. The pathology is correct. The person is nonetheless harmed, because they receive treatment for a disease that was never going to hurt them, and they become a "survivor" who credits the screening that harmed them. **Overdiagnosis manufactures its own advocates.** That sentence explains why overdiagnosis is politically almost impossible to correct.

Cases to build it on:
- **South Korea's thyroid cancer epidemic.** Ultrasound screening offered opportunistically alongside other health checks from the late 1990s; thyroid cancer incidence rose roughly fifteen-fold; **mortality remained flat.** Thousands of thyroidectomies, with permanent hypoparathyroidism and vocal cord palsy among the complications. (Ahn, Kim & Welch, *NEJM*, 2014.)
- **Neuroblastoma screening in infants.** Mass screening in Japan; controlled evaluations in Quebec and Germany showed incidence roughly doubled or tripled with **no reduction in mortality** — the extra tumours were ones that regress spontaneously.
- **Prostate cancer and PSA.** ERSPC vs PLCO; contamination in PLCO; the long, slow renegotiation toward active surveillance.
- **Mammography.** Handle with extreme care and balance — it is the most contested area in this chapter. Present the range of estimates honestly, including the Canadian National Breast Screening Study, the Cochrane review, and the mainstream guideline positions that disagree with them. **Do not resolve it. Explain why reasonable experts disagree, and what evidence would settle it.**

### 12.5 — The popularity paradox

Teach this elegant, cruel dynamic explicitly, because it explains the politics of every screening controversy:

> The more overdiagnosis a screening programme causes, the more "survivors" it creates, the more grateful advocates it produces, the more political support it receives, and the harder it becomes to evaluate or scale back.

**Harm is silent; false benefit is loud.** This is the same asymmetry from 0.2.3, wearing a different costume. Point that out — the reader should be starting to see the same skeleton under different cases. That recognition *is* expertise.

### 12.6 — What good screening looks like

End constructively. The criteria (Wilson & Jungner, 1968, and their modern updates): important condition, detectable pre-clinical phase, effective earlier treatment, acceptable test, and — the one always forgotten — **evidence from randomised trials of reduced disease-specific and ideally all-cause mortality.** Examples that meet the bar reasonably well: colorectal cancer screening, cervical cytology/HPV. Examples that don't: whole-body MRI, most "executive health screens," and most consumer diagnostics.

**Word budget:** 9,000
**Reader exercise:** A country announces a new national screening programme and reports that five-year survival among screen-detected cases is 92% versus 61% in unscreened. Write 300 words explaining why this tells you almost nothing, and state exactly what number you would need instead.

## CHAPTER 13 — The Social Machinery: Groups, Guidelines, and Institutions

**The Argument:** Individual clinicians are corrected by their colleagues; but colleagues in a group can be wrong together in ways no individual would be alone.

### 13.1 — Groupthink and consensus formation

Janis's conditions, applied to medicine: cohesive expert group, insulated from outside challenge, directive leadership, high stress, low perceived alternatives. Then the medical form: the guideline panel, the tumour board, the specialty society.

### 13.2 — Normalization of deviance

Diane Vaughan's concept from the *Challenger* analysis: a deviation from standard, repeated without immediate catastrophe, becomes the new standard. Medical versions: skipped timeouts, alarm fatigue, "we always do it this way here," progressive expansion of an indication one small step at a time. **Nobody ever decided to do the wrong thing; each step was a small extension of the last.**

Link explicitly to indication creep — the mechanism by which opioids went from terminal cancer pain to chronic back pain over fifteen years without any single decision to make that leap.

### 13.3 — How a guideline is made, and where it goes wrong

Walk through it forensically, because the reader has no idea:
1. Society appoints a panel — often the field's most enthusiastic proponents, because those are the recognised experts.
2. Evidence reviewed, often by the panel itself, sometimes with variable rigour.
3. Recommendation graded — and **weak recommendations based on low-quality evidence appear in the same document, in the same typeface, as strong ones.**
4. Guideline published; media reports the recommendation, never the grade.
5. Recommendation becomes a **quality metric**.
6. Quality metric becomes a **payment condition** or a **public league table**.
7. Metric becomes the **legal standard of care** in malpractice litigation.

**By step 7, an expert opinion has become mandatory.** Trace one real practice through all seven steps — the sepsis bundle time-to-antibiotics mandates are a good, live, genuinely contested example; so is the history of "pain as the fifth vital sign."

### 13.4 — Conflicts of interest on panels

Report the empirical literature on the proportion of guideline authors with financial ties, the relationship between ties and recommendations, and the reform efforts (Institute of Medicine standards, chair-must-be-conflict-free rules). Be fair: expertise and industry contact are correlated for real reasons — the people who ran the trials know the most. **State the dilemma honestly rather than resolving it cheaply.**

### 13.5 — Specialty identity and the ownership of procedures

A procedure is not just a treatment; it is an income, a training pathway, a professional identity, a departmental budget, and a source of status. **You are not asking a spine surgeon to change their mind. You are asking them to dissolve a part of themselves.** Write it that way. It will land.

### 13.6 — Small area variation: the fingerprint of non-evidence

Introduce **Glover (1938)** on tonsillectomy rates between English school districts and **Wennberg (1973, *Science*)** on Vermont. Rates of tonsillectomy, hysterectomy, prostatectomy, and spinal fusion vary several-fold between neighbouring areas with similar populations.

Teach the inference, which is beautiful and damning: **where practice varies wildly and outcomes don't, the practice is not being driven by patient need. It is being driven by local supply and local habit.** Variation is a *diagnostic test for the absence of evidence*, and it can be run on any health system's data today.

**Word budget:** 8,000

## CHAPTER 14 — Follow the Money, Follow the Career
### *Structural incentives and the shaping of knowledge*

**The Argument:** The scientific literature is not a neutral record of nature; it is the output of a system with funders, gatekeepers, and career ladders, and its shape reflects those forces as much as it reflects biology.

**Tone warning:** This is the chapter where a lesser book becomes a conspiracy tract. Do not let it. The mechanisms here are almost all *legal, disclosed, and structural*. That is what makes them dangerous — and it means you never need to allege a plot.

### 14.1 — Publication bias: the missing literature

- Positive results are more likely to be written up, submitted, accepted, published quickly, published in English, and cited.
- **The definitive demonstration:** Turner et al., *NEJM*, 2008. FDA registration data for antidepressant trials compared to the published literature. Roughly half the trials were positive by FDA assessment; almost all *published* trials appeared positive, because negative trials were either unpublished or written up as though positive. The published effect size was inflated by roughly a third.
- **Reboxetine:** Eyding et al., *BMJ*, 2010 — the large majority of patient data was unpublished, and its inclusion changed the drug's apparent efficacy and harm profile substantially.
- Funnel plots and how to read one, taught visually.

### 14.2 — Outcome reporting bias and spin

Within a published paper: pre-specified primary endpoint disappoints, a secondary endpoint is promoted, the abstract emphasises the favourable subgroup. Cite the systematic literature on "spin" in abstracts of negative trials, and the COMPare project's tracking of outcome switching. **Teach the reader the single most powerful check available to them: read the registry entry, then read the paper, and compare the outcomes.**

### 14.3 — Ghostwriting and the industrial production of literature

Explain the mechanics: a communications agency drafts the manuscript, an academic is recruited as "author," the article appears under their name in a respected journal. Cover the documented cases exposed through litigation discovery — including the hormone-therapy manuscript programmes revealed in the Wyeth litigation, and the gabapentin marketing documents. Distinguish carefully between professional medical writing with disclosure (legitimate) and concealed authorship (not).

### 14.4 — Seeding trials, KOLs, and the marketing–science boundary

- **Seeding trials:** studies whose real purpose is to familiarise prescribers with a drug rather than to answer a question. Explain how to recognise one (huge site count, tiny per-site enrolment, weak endpoints, no plausible power calculation).
- **Key opinion leaders** as a formal, budgeted marketing category.
- **CME funding**, speaker bureaus, and the advisory-board economy.

### 14.5 — Regulatory capture and the approval pathway

- User fees and the funding structure of regulators.
- **Accelerated approval**: designed for AIDS-era urgency, now a substantial share of oncology approvals, granted on surrogates, with confirmatory trials frequently delayed, negative, or never completed. Give the numbers.
- **Withdrawal is slow and rare**: bevacizumab for breast cancer took three years from approval to revocation and required a formal hearing; aducanumab's story ran through advisory-committee dissent, resignations, coverage restriction, and eventual commercial discontinuation.
- Device regulation and the 510(k) substantial-equivalence pathway: how a device can reach the market via a chain of predicates without direct clinical evidence, and what happened with metal-on-metal hips and surgical mesh.

### 14.6 — Academic incentives: the other money

Don't let universities off the hook. Publish-or-perish, impact-factor chasing, grant renewal cycles, the premium on novelty over replication, the near-total absence of career reward for reproducing someone else's work or for publishing a negative result. **The reproducibility crisis is not a failure of scientists' character; it is the predictable equilibrium of the incentive structure they work in.** Cite Begley & Ellis (*Nature*, 2012 — 6 of 53 landmark preclinical oncology studies reproduced) and Prinz et al. (2011, Bayer's internal replication rates) and be careful about their methodological limitations, which have been criticised.

### 14.7 — Media, hype, and the amplification chain

Trace one finding from lab to headline: press release → wire service → headline → morning television → patient in clinic asking for it. Cite the research showing that exaggeration in health news usually originates in the **university press release**, not the journalist. This is a genuinely surprising and useful finding for readers who blame the media.

**Word budget:** 10,000

## CHAPTER 15 — When It Isn't Error: Fraud, Misconduct, and the Immune Response

**The Argument:** Outright fabrication is rare compared to bias, but it is uniquely instructive — because how a field detects fraud reveals what it was failing to check all along.

**Why this chapter exists:** Partly to separate fraud from error (so the reader doesn't collapse everything into villainy), partly because fraud cases are natural experiments on the strength of medicine's error-detection systems.

### 15.1 — The taxonomy: fabrication, falsification, plagiarism — and the vast grey zone

Most damage lives in the grey zone: selective reporting, image "beautification," data exclusion by post-hoc rule, salami-slicing, honorary authorship, undisclosed conflicts. Name the grey zone as such.

### 15.2 — The case files

For each: what was fabricated, how long it stood, what it changed, how it was caught, and **what should have caught it sooner.**

- **Werner Bezwoda** — the single positive high-dose chemotherapy trial for breast cancer; unravelled when a site visit in 2000 found records did not exist. **Caught by audit, not by peer review.**
- **Yoshitaka Fujii** (anaesthesia) — the largest retraction count in medical history (183 papers); exposed substantially through *statistical* analysis of implausibly uniform data distributions (Carlisle's method). **Caught by arithmetic, not by reviewers.**
- **Joachim Boldt** — hydroxyethyl starch research; retractions on a massive scale; the affected literature had supported a fluid later shown in independent trials (6S, CHEST) to increase renal failure and mortality in sepsis.
- **Andrew Wakefield** — the 1998 *Lancet* paper; twelve children; retracted in 2010; consequences measured in measles outbreaks decades later. Use it to teach how a *tiny, methodologically trivial* study can outrun mountains of contrary evidence when it supplies a narrative people need.
- **Anil Potti** (Duke) — genomic signatures directing cancer chemotherapy; patients were enrolled in trials guided by the signatures. Caught by two biostatisticians (Baggerly and Coombes) attempting to reproduce the analysis. **Caught by people asking for the data.**
- **Paolo Macchiarini** — synthetic trachea transplants; patients died; institutional failure at a world-leading centre; the whistleblowers were initially punished.
- **Surgisphere (2020)** — a fabricated database underlying high-profile papers in *The Lancet* and *NEJM* on hydroxychloroquine, retracted within about two weeks. **Caught in days by open scrutiny — the fastest correction in this list, and a genuine sign of a system improving.**

### 15.3 — What the pattern reveals

Draw the conclusions explicitly:
1. **Peer review almost never catches fraud.** It was never designed to; it assumes good faith and has no access to raw data.
2. **Fraud is usually caught by people trying to reuse the data** — replicators, statisticians, and auditors. Therefore *data sharing is an anti-fraud technology*, not just an efficiency measure.
3. **Whistleblowers are punished with striking regularity.** Document it. Institutional reputational interest is the mechanism.
4. **Retraction is slow and incomplete**, and retracted papers continue to be cited, often approvingly, for years afterward. Cite the Retraction Watch database and the citation-after-retraction literature.

### 15.4 — Why fraud is not the main problem

Close with proportionality. If every fraudulent paper vanished tomorrow, the reversals in Part III would be almost entirely unchanged. **CAST, WHI, vertebroplasty, HRT, the Swan-Ganz catheter, EGDT, tight glycaemic control — not one of these involved fraud.** They involved honest people, honest data, and bad thinking.

That is the sentence that carries the reader into Part III. Put it at the end of Part II, alone on the page.

**Word budget:** 7,000

## CHAPTER 16 — The Ancient Regime
### *Flaw: a complete, coherent, and entirely false explanatory system*

**The Argument:** A wrong framework does not produce hesitant, sloppy medicine. It produces confident, systematic, industrial-scale medicine that harms with precision.

### 16.1 — The flaw: system-level error

When the *axioms* are wrong, every downstream deduction is impeccably reasoned and wrong. Rigour amplifies the harm rather than limiting it. This is the most frightening idea in the chapter and the one that transfers most directly to the present.

### 16.2 — Case: Humoral medicine and the 1,700-year run

- The system: four humours, four qualities, imbalance as disease, evacuation as therapy.
- **Why it survived so long — this is the real content, and I want you to give it 4,000 words.** Not "they were ignorant." The reasons are structural and every one of them recurs later in the book:
  - It **explained everything** (unfalsifiable — see 0.2.1, species 9).
  - It **matched observation superficially** (fevers do produce sweating; illness does resolve after treatment).
  - It produced **immediate, visible effects** — bleeding causes pallor, bradycardia, and calm, which in an agitated febrile patient reads unmistakably as improvement.
  - It was **institutionally embedded** — universities, licensing, texts, guild structures.
  - It had **no competitor** — you cannot abandon your only theory.
  - There was **no counterfactual** — no controls, no records, no denominators.
  - Its failures were **attributable to the disease**, its successes to the treatment. (Name this: *asymmetric attribution*. It's a recurring device.)

### 16.3 — Case: Benjamin Rush and the Philadelphia yellow fever epidemic (1793)

The most instructive individual in the chapter, because he was in every respect *admirable*: signer of the Declaration of Independence, abolitionist, reformer of the treatment of the mentally ill, and a physician who stayed in a plague city when others fled, treating the poor for free until he collapsed.

And he bled his patients enormously, purged them with calomel, and almost certainly killed a great many of them.

**Run the fairness test (0.4.3) explicitly on Rush.** He kept records; he believed the records vindicated him; he defended his method in public, sued a critic, and never wavered. **Rush is the book's proof that personal virtue, courage, intelligence, and even data collection are insufficient protection against a wrong framework.** If he could not save himself, neither can you. Say that to the reader directly.

### 16.4 — Case: Pierre Louis and the birth of counting

- The *méthode numérique*; the 1830s study of bloodletting in pneumonia.
- **The critical insight to convey:** Louis needed no new technology, no microscope, no laboratory. He needed only to *count*, *compare*, and *be willing to be wrong*. The tools to overturn 1,700 years of practice had been available for 1,700 years. Nobody had picked them up.
- **The resistance:** that averages efface the individual; that medicine is an art; that the physician's judgement about *this* patient cannot be superseded by numbers about *other* patients.
- **Then the twist that makes this chapter modern:** print that objection, and then print a near-identical objection made by a contemporary clinician about evidence-based medicine or clinical decision support. Unlabelled. Let the reader place them.

### 16.5 — The pattern

Coherence without correspondence. Rigour without validity. Then the transfer question, asked seriously: **what are our humours?** Candidates worth exploring honestly — inflammation as a universal explanation, the serotonin hypothesis of depression, "chemical imbalance" language, oxidative stress, the pre-2020 assumptions about a great many things. Present them as candidates, not verdicts.

**Word budget:** 9,000

## CHAPTER 17 — The Invisible Cause
### *Flaw: rejecting evidence because the mechanism is unimaginable*

**The Argument:** Medicine repeatedly demands a mechanism before accepting a well-demonstrated effect — a demand that is epistemically backwards and has cost enormous numbers of lives.

### 17.1 — The flaw: mechanism as a precondition for belief

Chapter 1 showed mechanism creating false belief. This chapter shows the mirror image: **the absence of a mechanism destroying true belief.** Make the symmetry explicit — it's the same error (over-weighting mechanistic plausibility relative to empirical data) pointing in opposite directions. That symmetry is a genuinely deep observation and it will impress your best readers.

### 17.2 — Case: Ignaz Semmelweis (Vienna, 1847)

Tell it properly and slowly; it is the book's central parable.

- **The setting:** Vienna General Hospital, two maternity clinics. First Clinic staffed by medical students and physicians; Second Clinic by midwives. Maternal mortality from puerperal fever roughly 10% in the First, roughly 4% in the Second.
- **The public knew.** Women begged to be admitted to the Second Clinic; some gave birth in the street rather than enter the First. **The patients had already made the correct inference from the data.** That detail is devastating; use it.
- **The clue:** the death of Semmelweis's colleague Jakob Kolletschka in 1847, after a scalpel cut during an autopsy, with a post-mortem picture resembling puerperal fever.
- **The inference:** "cadaverous particles" carried on the hands of doctors and students who came from the dissection room to the delivery bed. Midwives did no autopsies.
- **The intervention:** handwashing in chlorinated lime. Mortality in the First Clinic fell dramatically.
- **The rejection.** And here you must be scrupulously fair, because the popular version of this story is a caricature:
  - **Germ theory did not exist.** His mechanism ("cadaverous particles") was vague and, to trained physicians, unpersuasive.
  - **He published badly and late** — the major book came in 1861, fourteen years after the finding, and it was long, disorganised, and increasingly furious.
  - **He accused his colleagues of murder**, in print, by name. This is not an effective persuasion strategy, but — and this is the humane point — **he was correct**, and the correctness is precisely what made it unbearable.
  - **The finding threatened professional identity.** Gentlemen's hands were clean by definition. Cleanliness was a matter of social class, not microbiology.
  - **Contemporary competing explanations existed** (miasma, ventilation, overcrowding) and were not obviously worse to someone without the germ concept.
- **The end:** committed to an asylum; died in 1865, aged 47, of sepsis — probably from an infected wound.
- **The lag:** roughly two decades until Lister and Pasteur made the mechanism thinkable, and longer for practice to change fully.

**The Deep Why — write this as the chapter's thesis:** The evidence Semmelweis had was, by any modern standard, overwhelming: a large mortality difference, a plausible exposure, a clean natural experiment, and a dramatic intervention effect. It was rejected because it **lacked a mechanism the profession could accept** and because accepting it required accepting guilt. Those two forces, together, defeated an effect size that would today stop a trial early.

Then turn to the reader: *what effect are you currently disbelieving because you can't imagine how it would work?*

### 17.3 — Case: Oliver Wendell Holmes (Boston, 1843)

Four years *before* Semmelweis, Holmes published "The Contagiousness of Puerperal Fever," reaching a similar conclusion from a different route. He was also dismissed — notably by the eminent obstetrician Charles Meigs, whose response included the sentiment that doctors are gentlemen and gentlemen's hands are clean.

**Why include Holmes:** it destroys the "lone genius who communicated badly" excuse. Holmes was eloquent, well-connected, socially adept, and American. **He was ignored too.** Therefore the problem was not Semmelweis's temperament. The problem was structural. This is a crucial analytic move — make it explicitly.

### 17.4 — Case: John Snow and the Broad Street pump (1854)

- The Broad Street outbreak; the mapping; the removal of the pump handle; the natural experiment of the Southwark & Vauxhall versus Lambeth water companies serving intermingled houses from different intakes on the Thames.
- **The rejection:** miasma theory was dominant, coherent, and endorsed by the leading sanitary reformers, including Edwin Chadwick and Florence Nightingale. Snow's committee response was that his theory was insufficiently supported.
- Yet Snow's water-company comparison is, in retrospect, one of the finest observational studies ever conducted.
- **The nuance to include:** miasma theory, though wrong, motivated sanitation reforms that saved enormous numbers of lives. **A wrong theory can produce right actions.** Do not let the reader think wrongness always means harm — that oversimplification is its own error, and confronting it here makes your book smarter than its competitors.

### 17.5 — Case: *Helicobacter pylori* (the modern Semmelweis)

Full treatment belongs in Chapter 25; here, place it as the proof that this flaw is not historical. Warren and Warren's observation and Marshall's culture in the early 1980s were rejected for years partly because "bacteria cannot live in the stomach" — a mechanistic conviction that overrode direct observation of the bacteria. Nobel Prize, 2005.

### 17.6 — The pattern and the live version

Formalise the rule:

> When a well-measured effect conflicts with an accepted mechanism, the mechanism is more likely to be wrong than the measurement. Yet medicine reliably discards the measurement.

Live candidates worth discussing carefully and non-dogmatically: the long lag on the microbiome's clinical significance; the historically dismissive treatment of post-infectious fatigue syndromes and now long COVID; the credibility deficit applied to patient-reported symptoms without a visible lesion — where "no mechanism" has repeatedly been translated into "no disease." Handle this with real care; it is where the book can do the most present-day good.

**Word budget:** 11,000

## CHAPTER 18 — Killed by the Surrogate
### *Flaw: treating the number instead of the person*

**The Argument:** This is the deadliest flaw in modern medicine, and unlike most of the others it operates *because* of good science rather than in the absence of it.

### 18.1 — Case: CAST — the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial (1989)

**Make this the single most detailed autopsy in the book.** It is the perfect case: modern, rigorous, well-intentioned, and catastrophic.

- **The claim:** Suppressing premature ventricular contractions after myocardial infarction prevents sudden cardiac death.
- **The mechanism story — and it is genuinely excellent:** patients who die suddenly after an MI usually die of ventricular fibrillation. Ventricular fibrillation is often preceded by ventricular ectopy. More frequent PVCs after MI predict higher mortality (this was solid, replicated observational epidemiology — the Lown grading system). Class I antiarrhythmic drugs suppress PVCs, measurably and impressively, on Holter monitoring. **Therefore suppress the PVCs and prevent the deaths.**
- **Force the reader to agree.** Ask: *would you have prescribed it?* The honest answer is yes. Every step is supported. Make them say yes before you show them the result — otherwise the lesson doesn't land.
- **Adoption:** flecainide and encainide widely used. Estimates of American patients on class I antiarrhythmics in the late 1980s run into the hundreds of thousands.
- **The trial:** CAST was designed by people who *believed* the drugs worked; the ethical debate at the time was whether it was acceptable to randomise patients to placebo and deny them a beneficial drug. Note that. It matters enormously.
- **The result (NEJM, 1989):** the encainide and flecainide arms were stopped early. Mortality in the treated groups was roughly **two to three times higher** than placebo. The drugs suppressed the arrhythmias and killed the patients.
- **Why:** the ectopy was a *marker* of a diseased, scarred myocardium, not the causal pathway. And the drugs, in an ischaemic ventricle, were themselves proarrhythmic.
- **The body count:** Thomas Moore's analysis in *Deadly Medicine* argued the death toll exceeded American losses in the Vietnam War. Treat the estimate with appropriate caveats — it is an extrapolation — but the order of magnitude is defensible and it belongs in the book.
- **The fair verdict:** largely tier 2 (negligent-structural rather than culpable). Nobody lied. The reasoning was excellent. The reasoning was the problem.
- **The transfer:** *every* surrogate-based approval since.

### 18.2 — Case: Torcetrapib and the HDL hypothesis (2006)

- HDL is "good cholesterol" — decades of consistent observational epidemiology showed higher HDL, lower cardiovascular risk.
- CETP inhibition raised HDL dramatically (~70%).
- **ILLUMINATE**, over 15,000 patients, halted December 2006 for **excess mortality**.
- **The subsequent, deeper lesson — and this is the part most accounts miss:** Mendelian randomisation studies (Voight et al., *Lancet*, 2012) showed that genetic variants raising HDL were **not** associated with reduced myocardial infarction. The association between HDL and heart disease was largely non-causal from the start. **The surrogate wasn't merely an incomplete mediator; it was a bystander.**
- Teach the reader that this changes the whole picture: even a surrogate with forty years of consistent epidemiology behind it can be causally inert.

### 18.3 — Case: Erythropoietin and the haemoglobin target

Anaemia in chronic kidney disease correlates with mortality; ESAs correct anaemia; therefore normalise haemoglobin. CHOIR, CREATE and TREAT showed higher targets increased death, stroke, and thrombotic events. Add the commercial layer: reimbursement structures rewarded higher dosing. **A financial incentive and a surrogate pointing the same direction is the most dangerous configuration in this book.** Name it as a general principle.

### 18.4 — Case: Intensive glycaemic control (ACCORD, NICE-SUGAR)

- The surrogate: HbA1c and blood glucose.
- The mechanism: glucose damages vessels; lower is better; "normalise the physiology."
- The seductive precedent: Van den Berghe's 2001 single-centre surgical ICU trial showed striking benefit from tight glucose control, and it was adopted worldwide with remarkable speed.
- **ACCORD (2008):** intensive glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes — the intensive arm was stopped for **increased all-cause mortality.**
- **NICE-SUGAR (2009):** over 6,000 ICU patients — intensive control **increased 90-day mortality**, with far more severe hypoglycaemia.
- **The refinement, not reversal, distinction:** glucose control does reduce *microvascular* complications over long horizons. The error was extrapolating to macrovascular outcomes, to acute illness, and to aggressive targets. Use this to teach species 3 from 0.2.1 — **the Overextended** — with precision.
- **Extra lesson: single-centre miracles.** Van den Berghe's result was real in her unit. It did not generalise. Teach the reader to distrust dramatic single-centre results as a *class*, and explain why (centre effects, protocol fidelity, patient mix, and the winner's curse from 9.2).

### 18.5 — Case: Oxygen

A lovely, under-told story. Oxygen was given routinely in myocardial infarction for decades — the mechanism is irresistible (ischaemia means insufficient oxygen; give oxygen). **AVOID (2015)** and **DETO2X-AMI (2017)** found no benefit in normoxic patients, with signals of possible harm from hyperoxia. Similar re-evaluations occurred in stroke, resuscitation, and critical care. Connect forward to Chapter 23 and retinopathy of prematurity — **the same molecule, the same reasoning error, seventy years apart, in a different specialty, and nobody made the connection.** That failure of institutional memory is itself a finding.

### 18.6 — The pattern, and the rule you will give the reader

Formalise:
> **Before accepting any claim of benefit, identify the endpoint. If it is not something a patient could feel, notice, or die of, treat the claim as a hypothesis about a hypothesis.**

### 18.7 — The live version

Accelerated approvals on progression-free survival; amyloid clearance; a great deal of what is measured in "biological age," continuous glucose monitoring in non-diabetics, and consumer wearables. Be balanced but unflinching.

**Word budget:** 12,000

## CHAPTER 19 — The Observational Mirage
### *Flaw: mistaking the kind of person for the effect of the treatment*

**The Argument:** The largest, longest, most carefully adjusted observational studies in the history of medicine got a central question exactly backwards — and the reason is a bias that no amount of statistical adjustment can remove.

### 19.1 — Case: Hormone replacement therapy and the Women's Health Initiative

The definitive case. Give it 6,000 words.

- **The observational evidence:** decades of cohort data, above all the Nurses' Health Study — one of the best-run cohorts ever assembled — showing that women on postmenopausal hormone therapy had roughly 40–50% less coronary heart disease.
- **The mechanism:** oestrogen improves lipid profiles, endothelial function, and vascular reactivity. Coherent, replicated, biologically satisfying.
- **The consensus:** hormone therapy for cardiovascular prevention became mainstream advice. Millions of prescriptions. Guidelines. Textbooks.
- **The WHI (2002):** the oestrogen-plus-progestin arm halted early. **No cardiovascular protection; increased breast cancer, stroke, and venous thromboembolism.** In absolute terms the harms were small per woman, but the treated population was enormous.
- **Why the observational studies were wrong — this is the teaching payload:**
  - **Healthy user bias.** Women prescribed and continuing HRT in that era were disproportionately wealthier, thinner, more educated, more physically active, more likely to have private healthcare, and more engaged with preventive medicine. They were going to have less heart disease *whatever they took*.
  - **Adherence effects.** Continuing any long-term therapy is itself a marker of health behaviour.
  - **Prevalent user bias.** Women who had bad experiences stopped and were no longer counted among users.
  - **Residual confounding.** Socioeconomic status was adjusted for — imperfectly, with crude proxies. **Adjustment for a mismeasured confounder removes only part of the confounding, and the remainder is invisible.** Make this point precisely; it's the technical heart of the chapter.
- **The aftermath, and the necessary nuance:** the **timing hypothesis** — that oestrogen may be beneficial or neutral when started near menopause and harmful when started years later in women with established atherosclerosis. Evidence from WHI age subgroups, ELITE, and KEEPS is consistent with this but not definitive. Meanwhile, the pendulum swung so hard that women with severe menopausal symptoms were denied effective treatment for a decade. **The overcorrection was its own harm.** Include it. A book that only teaches "be sceptical" produces a different kind of bad doctor.

### 19.2 — Case: Antioxidant vitamins

- Observational data: high dietary antioxidant intake associated with less cancer and heart disease. Consistent, large, biologically plausible (oxidative stress → DNA damage → cancer).
- **ATBC (1994):** beta-carotene and alpha-tocopherol in Finnish male smokers — beta-carotene was associated with **increased lung cancer incidence and mortality**.
- **CARET (1996):** beta-carotene plus retinol, stopped early — again, **increased lung cancer**.
- Vitamin E trials for cardiovascular prevention: repeatedly null; meta-analyses raised concerns about higher doses.
- **The lesson to draw out:** eating fruit and vegetables is associated with health. **The nutrient is not the food, and the food is not the person.** Isolating a molecule from a dietary pattern from a lifestyle from a socioeconomic position — each step is an inferential leap and all four were made at once.
- Add the vitamin D story as the current, live iteration: strong observational associations across an extraordinary range of conditions; VITAL and other large RCTs largely null; Mendelian randomisation largely null. **The likely explanation: low vitamin D is a marker of being ill, indoors, and inactive — a consequence, not a cause.** This is one of the most transferable insights in the book: *reverse causation dressed as prevention.*

### 19.3 — Case: The dietary fat wars

Handle with maximum care, minimum polemic — this is a field full of loud partisans.

- Ancel Keys, the Seven Countries Study, the diet–heart hypothesis; the strengths and the real methodological criticisms (country selection, ecological design).
- The **ecological fallacy** at work (10.7).
- The sugar industry's documented funding of reviews shifting attention from sugar to fat.
- The subsequent evidence: mixed, with saturated-fat replacement effects depending heavily on the replacement nutrient.
- **The honest conclusion:** we still do not have clean answers to basic nutritional questions, because the field's methods (food-frequency questionnaires, uncontrollable confounding, impossibly long horizons, unblindable interventions) are structurally weak. **Say so plainly. Nutritional epidemiology is the hardest science in this book and the one most confidently reported.**

### 19.4 — The pattern and the rescue

State the diagnostic rule: **whenever the exposure is something a health-conscious person chooses, suspect the person, not the exposure.**

Then give the reader the escape routes (previewing Chapter 35): Mendelian randomisation, negative control outcomes, active comparator new-user designs, target trial emulation, and triangulation across designs with different biases. **Observational research is not worthless; naive observational research is.** Make that distinction cleanly or you'll teach nihilism.

**Word budget:** 11,000

## CHAPTER 20 — The Unexamined Knife
### *Flaw: exempting procedures from the evidence standards applied to pills*

**The Argument:** Surgery has been held to a systematically lower evidentiary standard than pharmacology for a century, for reasons that are historically explicable and no longer defensible.

### 20.1 — The flaw: why procedures escape

Enumerate honestly:
- No regulator approves an operation the way one approves a drug.
- Blinding seems impossible (it isn't — see Chapter 33).
- Sham surgery raises real ethical objections that require real answers.
- Techniques evolve continuously, so "the operation" is a moving target.
- Surgeon skill varies, creating a genuine methodological problem (learning curves, expertise-based designs).
- **And: the visible, physical, mechanical logic of surgery makes it feel self-evidently effective.** You can *see* the torn tissue. You removed it. How could that not help?

That last point is Chapter 1's flaw in its purest form. **Surgery is mechanism you can hold in your hand.**

### 20.2 — Case: Internal mammary ligation (revisit from 1.5, now in depth)

Full autopsy. Emphasise that **the definitive method — sham-controlled surgical trials — was demonstrated in 1959 and then essentially ignored for fifty years.** Ask why, and answer it: no regulator required it, no funder demanded it, no journal insisted, and the people who would have had to do it were the people it would have cost.

### 20.3 — Case: Arthroscopic surgery for knee osteoarthritis and degenerative meniscal tear

- **Moseley et al., NEJM 2002:** arthroscopic debridement or lavage versus **sham surgery** (skin incisions, simulated procedure) for knee osteoarthritis. No benefit over sham at any point over two years.
- **Reaction:** widespread rejection; criticisms of the VA population, of surgeon selection, of patient selection. Some legitimate. Volumes continued.
- **Sihvonen et al. (FIDELITY), NEJM 2013:** arthroscopic partial meniscectomy versus sham for degenerative meniscal tear in patients without osteoarthritis — precisely the "correct" population the critics had specified. **No difference.**
- **The lag:** hundreds of thousands of procedures annually worldwide, for years afterward. Rates fell in some countries, and remained high in others — **and the international variation is itself the natural experiment that proves it was never about the evidence.**
- **The subtle trap to teach here:** an MRI-visible meniscal tear is extremely common in asymptomatic middle-aged knees. **Finding an abnormality is not the same as finding the cause of the symptom.** Generalise it: *incidental structural findings + a symptomatic patient + an available procedure = a reliable engine of unnecessary surgery.* This is one of the highest-yield sentences you will write, because it applies to spines, shoulders, hips, and sinuses.

### 20.4 — Case: Vertebroplasty

Cement injected into osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures. Enthusiastic uncontrolled series; dramatic reported pain relief; rapid adoption. Then **two independent sham-controlled trials published simultaneously in NEJM in 2009** (Buchbinder et al.; Kallmes et al. — INVEST) showed no benefit over sham. Cover the vigorous methodological counter-arguments from the interventional community, the subsequent trials, and the continuing disagreement. **Model how to write about a contested reversal without pretending it's settled.**

### 20.5 — Case: Stents for stable angina

- **COURAGE (2007):** PCI plus optimal medical therapy versus optimal medical therapy alone in stable coronary disease — no reduction in death or MI.
- The defence: "but it relieves symptoms."
- **ORBITA (2018):** the sham-controlled trial of PCI for stable single-vessel angina. Small, but the first of its kind. The difference in exercise time versus placebo procedure was not statistically significant. Reaction was extraordinary and worth documenting as a sociological event in its own right.
- **ISCHEMIA (2020):** large, moderate-to-severe ischaemia, invasive versus conservative strategy — no significant reduction in the primary composite, with symptom benefit in those who had angina at baseline.
- **The nuance that must be preserved:** none of this applies to acute coronary syndromes or STEMI, where PCI is one of the great achievements of modern medicine. **Precision about indication is what separates a scholar from a contrarian.** Say that to your reader explicitly, because the internet is full of people who read the headline of COURAGE and concluded that cardiology is fraudulent.

### 20.6 — Shorter cases (2,000 words each)

- **Gastric freezing** (from 1.4, cross-reference not repeat)
- **Routine episiotomy** — decades of routine use on the theory that a clean surgical cut heals better than a ragged tear and prevents pelvic floor damage. Systematic review evidence (Hartmann et al., *JAMA*, 2005) did not support routine use; harms included increased severe perineal trauma. Rates fell dramatically, at very different speeds in different countries.
- **Tonsillectomy** — Glover's 1938 variation data; the long slow narrowing of indications.
- **Spinal fusion for non-specific low back pain** — the live case; enormous international variation, weak evidence, high cost, considerable harm.

### 20.7 — The pattern and the live version

The rule: **an intervention's physical vividness is inversely related to the scrutiny it receives.** Live versions: robotic surgery's diffusion ahead of comparative outcome data; a large proportion of interventional pain procedures; many endoscopic and structural cardiac interventions; cosmetic and "regenerative" injectables.

**Word budget:** 12,000

## CHAPTER 21 — Sicker Patients, Bigger Guns
### *Flaw: assuming that more monitoring, more titration, and more physiological control produce better outcomes*

**The Argument:** Critical care is the natural habitat of mechanistic reasoning, and it has produced a remarkable series of reversals — each one a device or protocol that made the numbers better and the patients no better or worse.

### 21.1 — The flaw: the illusion of control

In an ICU, you can measure and manipulate almost everything. The temptation to *normalise every derangement* is overwhelming, and it rests on an unexamined assumption: **that the abnormal number is the problem rather than the body's response to the problem.** Fever, tachycardia, hyperglycaemia in stress, low blood pressure in sepsis — each is partly adaptive. Normalising an adaptation can be lethal.

Also introduce here: the ICU is where the harm of *action* is least visible, because everyone is critically ill and every death is attributable to the disease. **Asymmetric attribution (16.2) at maximum strength.**

### 21.2 — Case: The pulmonary artery (Swan-Ganz) catheter

- Introduced in 1970; became near-ubiquitous in critical care and cardiology.
- The logic: you cannot manage haemodynamics you cannot measure. Cardiac output, filling pressures, mixed venous saturation — real-time physiology at the bedside.
- **Connors et al., JAMA 1996:** a large propensity-matched observational study suggesting **increased mortality** with PAC use. Enormous controversy; the obvious objection was confounding by indication (sicker patients get catheters).
- **The RCTs that followed** — PAC-Man (2005), ESCAPE (2005), and others in various populations — showed **no benefit.**
- **Why no benefit despite excellent data?** Two reasons, and both are generalisable: (1) the measurements were frequently *misinterpreted* — studies of clinicians' ability to interpret PAC tracings showed poor performance; (2) even correctly interpreted, **better information does not help unless the treatments you choose in response are themselves effective.** Say that clearly. *A diagnostic that improves knowledge without improving action is not a benefit; it is a cost with a complication rate.*
- **The transfer:** every new monitoring technology, every wearable, every continuous sensor, every AI early-warning score. **Ask of each: what action changes, and is that action known to help?**

### 21.3 — Case: Early Goal-Directed Therapy in sepsis

- **Rivers et al., NEJM 2001:** single-centre trial of a protocolised resuscitation bundle in severe sepsis — an extraordinary reported absolute mortality reduction (~16 percentage points).
- Adoption: enormous. Bundles, quality metrics, international guidelines, hospital mandates.
- **The re-test:** ProCESS (2014, US), ARISE (2014, Australasia), ProMISe (2015, UK) — three large multicentre RCTs — found **no benefit** of the protocol over usual care.
- **Why the difference? Teach all the candidate explanations, because the reasoning is the lesson:**
  - Single-centre effect and the winner's curse (9.2).
  - **Usual care improved** in the intervening decade, partly *because of* Rivers — so the control arm caught up. This is a genuinely important idea: **a trial can be right when done and wrong later, because it changed the world it was measuring.**
  - The bundle's active ingredient may have been early recognition and early antibiotics, not the central venous oxygen saturation targets and transfusion triggers.
- **The honest verdict:** not a simple reversal. A decomposition. Some of the bundle was gold; some was ritual. Teach the reader to *decompose* complex interventions rather than accept or reject them wholesale.

### 21.4 — Case: Activated protein C (drotrecogin alfa / Xigris)

- Mechanism: sepsis involves diffuse microvascular thrombosis and dysregulated coagulation; activated protein C is anticoagulant, profibrinolytic, and anti-inflammatory. Superb story.
- **PROWESS (2001):** significant mortality reduction. Approved in 2001.
- Controversy from the start: the trial had a mid-course protocol amendment and manufacturing change; critics questioned the result; the guidelines that recommended it had substantial industry involvement.
- **PROWESS-SHOCK (2012):** no benefit. The drug was **withdrawn from the market in October 2011.**
- **The lesson:** a single positive trial, however large, is a hypothesis about the world — and when there is a commercial and guideline apparatus ready to convert that hypothesis into standard of care overnight, the correction takes a decade.

### 21.5 — Case: Corticosteroids in traumatic brain injury (CRASH)

- Mechanism: brain injury causes swelling; steroids reduce swelling; therefore steroids help. Used for roughly thirty years.
- **CRASH (Lancet, 2004):** over 10,000 patients randomised. **Increased mortality** in the steroid arm. Trial stopped.
- **The important structural point:** it took a very large, simple, international, pragmatic trial to detect a moderate harm in a heterogeneous condition. **Small trials could never have found this**, and dozens of small trials had been done. Use this to teach the necessity of large simple trials, and to introduce the idea that *the absence of evidence had been read as evidence of safety for three decades.*

### 21.6 — Shorter cases

- **Intra-aortic balloon pump in cardiogenic shock** (IABP-SHOCK II, 2012) — decades of use, elegant physiology, no mortality benefit.
- **Hydroxyethyl starch** — plausible colloid physiology; 6S and CHEST showed increased renal replacement therapy and harm in sepsis; compounded by the Boldt fraud having polluted the prior literature.
- **Albumin, and the SAFE trial** — a case where a widely feared harm turned out to be absent; include it for balance so the chapter isn't monotonous.
- **Prolonged bed rest** — after myocardial infarction (weeks, mid-century), for back pain, for threatened preterm labour. Almost always harmful. **The most consistently wrong medical instruction of the twentieth century, and the cheapest, which tells you something about why it survived.**

### 21.7 — The pattern

> **Physiological plausibility plus measurability plus severity of illness equals the highest-risk configuration in medicine.**

**Word budget:** 11,000

## CHAPTER 22 — More Is Better
### *Flaw: the dose-intensity heuristic, applied to surgery and chemotherapy*

**The Argument:** The intuition that a more aggressive version of a working treatment must work better is false often enough — and seductive enough — to have shaped two of medicine's largest tragedies.

### 22.1 — The flaw: linear extrapolation in a non-linear system

If some is good, more is better. This holds for very few things in biology and for almost nothing with a toxicity profile. Teach the U-shaped curve as a default expectation rather than an exception.

### 22.2 — Case: The Halsted radical mastectomy

- **The theory (1890s):** breast cancer spreads in an orderly, centrifugal fashion — outward from the tumour, through the lymphatics, then systemically. Therefore cure requires removing everything in the path: breast, pectoralis major and minor, axillary contents. Some surgeons extended further — supraclavicular nodes, internal mammary chain, "super-radical" and "extended radical" operations, occasionally the arm.
- **Why it was believed:** the theory was coherent; Halsted was the most influential surgeon in America; his local recurrence rates genuinely improved on what came before; and he trained a generation who trained a generation.
- **The cost:** disfigurement, lymphoedema, frozen shoulder, chronic pain, in a great many women.
- **The challenge:** Bernard Fisher and the NSABP trials. Fisher's alternative hypothesis was that breast cancer is often **systemic at diagnosis** — micrometastases already present — so the extent of local surgery cannot determine survival. **NSABP B-04** compared radical mastectomy with less extensive surgery ± radiation: no survival difference. **NSABP B-06** established lumpectomy plus radiation as equivalent to mastectomy for suitable tumours.
- **The resistance:** decades. Fisher was attacked. The surgical establishment's response was that the trials were flawed, the surgeons insufficiently skilled, the patients wrongly selected.
- **The deep lesson:** Halsted's error was **anatomical thinking applied to a biological problem.** He thought about cancer the way a surgeon thinks — spatially. Fisher thought about it the way a biologist does — as a systemic disease. **The specialty you belong to determines the kind of hypothesis you can imagine.** That is a profound and generalisable observation; give it space.

### 22.3 — Case: High-dose chemotherapy with autologous bone marrow transplant for breast cancer

Return to the cold open of Chapter 0.1 and complete it. This case deserves 6,000 words because **every level of the four-level stack is visible at once.**

- **Level 1 (cognitive):** dose-intensity logic; desperation; commission bias; the vividness of dramatic responses.
- **Level 2 (methodological):** uncontrolled phase II series with **selected** patients (fit enough to survive the transplant — a spectacular selection bias); comparison against historical controls; response rate as a surrogate.
- **Level 3 (social):** oncology's identity as an aggressive specialty; transplant units as prestige centres; patient advocacy demanding access; **litigation and state legislation forcing insurers to cover it.**
- **Level 4 (structural):** transplant programmes as major revenue centres; the market for the procedure.
- **The trials:** hampered for years because women could get the treatment outside a trial, so why enrol and risk randomisation to standard care? **This is the most important single dynamic in the chapter: off-trial access to an unproven treatment destroys the ability to learn whether it works.** Trace the consequence precisely — the delay is measured in years and the harm in tens of thousands of women.
- **1999, ASCO:** the randomised trials reported. No survival benefit. The one strongly positive trial, Bezwoda's, was investigated and found to be fraudulent in 2000.
- **The aftermath:** rapid collapse of the practice. Note that when the evidence finally arrived, **de-adoption here was fast** — an important counterexample to Part V's thesis. Ask why. Plausible answer: no ongoing commercial champion, an unambiguous set of concurrent trials, a fraud scandal delegitimising the remaining defence, and a treatment so brutal that nobody wanted to do it without justification. **Extract the general conditions for fast de-adoption from this case — they are the blueprint for Chapter 39.**

### 22.4 — Shorter cases

- **Extended lymphadenectomy** in various cancers.
- **Dose-dense and high-dose regimens** in other tumours, where they sometimes *do* help — include the counterexample (testicular cancer, some lymphomas, paediatric leukaemia) so the reader learns the heuristic is not always wrong, merely not automatic.
- **Radical prostatectomy for low-risk disease**, and the move to active surveillance.

### 22.5 — The pattern and the live version

Live: intensification in oncology generally; polypharmacy in the elderly and the near-total absence of deprescribing infrastructure; aggressive multi-target treatment in metabolic disease; ever-lower treatment thresholds.

**Word budget:** 11,000

## CHAPTER 23 — The Smallest Patients
### *Flaw: extrapolating adult physiology to infants, and treating "natural" as "safe"*

**The Argument:** Neonatology and obstetrics have produced the most emotionally devastating reversals in medicine, for a structural reason: the patients cannot report harm, the harms often appear years later, and the interventions are administered universally.

**Tone instruction:** This will be the hardest chapter to write and the hardest to read. Do not soften it, and do not exploit it. Write with restraint; the facts supply all the emotion required.

### 23.1 — Case: Oxygen and retinopathy of prematurity

- **The 1940s:** supplemental oxygen for premature infants — plausible, humane, and in part genuinely beneficial.
- **The epidemic:** retrolental fibroplasia (now retinopathy of prematurity) emerged as a leading cause of childhood blindness in the developed world, essentially unknown before. Estimates run to roughly **10,000 infants blinded** worldwide.
- **The delay:** the association with oxygen was suspected for years, resisted, and required a large cooperative randomised study (reported 1956) to settle.
- **The cruel epilogue — and you must include it:** oxygen restriction, once adopted, was itself associated with increased mortality and brain injury. The field then spent decades searching for the right target, through the SUPPORT trial and the NeOProM meta-analyses, which found a narrow and consequential trade-off between mortality and retinopathy across saturation targets.
- **The lesson: there was no safe default.** Both action and inaction harmed. **This is the chapter's most important idea and it must be stated plainly: the demand for a "safe, natural, conservative" option is often a fantasy. Every choice is an intervention, including the choice to do less.**

### 23.2 — Case: Prone infant sleeping and SIDS

The case with the clearest arithmetic of harm-inside-the-lag in the entire book.

- **The advice:** place infants face-down to sleep. Rationale: reduced aspiration risk if the baby vomits; observed better sleep and less crying; developmental arguments.
- **The authority:** Benjamin Spock's *Baby and Child Care*, one of the best-selling books in history, recommended prone sleeping from its 1956 edition onward. Paediatric textbooks and hospital nurseries followed.
- **The evidence:** case-control studies from the 1960s and 1970s repeatedly found associations between prone position and sudden infant death.
- **The cumulative meta-analysis:** Gilbert et al. (*International Journal of Epidemiology*, 2005) reconstructed the evidence chronologically and concluded that the accumulated data would have shown the risk **by about 1970**.
- **The action:** Dutch and other European campaigns from the late 1980s; the UK's "Back to Sleep" in 1991; the US AAP recommendation in 1992 and the national campaign in 1994. SIDS rates fell by roughly half or more where campaigns were implemented.
- **The estimate:** the authors calculated the delay cost on the order of **50,000 infant deaths** in the developed world.
- **The autopsy — why this happened. Give each reason its own subsection:**
  - **The advice was mechanism-based** (aspiration) and never tested.
  - **The endpoint was rare**, so no individual clinician could see it. **A 2-per-1,000 event is invisible to a paediatrician with 2,000 patients over a career.**
  - **The evidence was distributed** across small studies in different countries in different languages — **nobody had synthesised it.** Systematic review as a discipline barely existed. *This is the single strongest argument for the existence of Cochrane that anyone has ever assembled.*
  - **The advice came from a trusted, benevolent authority** — and Spock's benevolence was real, which made him harder to contradict.
  - **The harm was invisible and unattributed:** SIDS was, by definition, unexplained. **You cannot count deaths you have classified as inexplicable.** That is a chilling and generalisable point about how diagnostic categories can hide iatrogenic harm.
- **Fair verdict:** blameless for Spock in 1956; increasingly negligent as a system after 1970.

### 23.3 — Case: Diethylstilbestrol (DES)

- Synthetic oestrogen given to millions of pregnant women from the 1940s into the 1970s to prevent miscarriage.
- **The evidence against it existed early:** Dieckmann's randomised controlled trial (1953) found **no benefit** in preventing miscarriage. It was largely ignored; prescribing continued and in some places expanded.
- **1971:** Herbst et al. reported clear cell adenocarcinoma of the vagina in young women — an essentially unprecedented finding in that age group — linked to in-utero DES exposure.
- **Then:** structural genital tract abnormalities, infertility, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and effects investigated across further generations.
- **Two distinct lessons, and they are both essential:**
  1. **A negative RCT that nobody acts on is worthless.** Evidence is not self-executing. This foreshadows Part V.
  2. **Transplacental and delayed harms are invisible to every routine safety system we have.** The latency was two decades. **No pharmacovigilance system built on clinician reporting can detect a harm that appears in a different person, twenty years later, as a rare tumour.** It was caught only because the tumour was so unusual that a single alert clinician noticed a cluster (Chapter 4.3's rule in action).

### 23.4 — Case: Thalidomide

- Marketed from 1957 as a safe sedative and antiemetic, including for morning sickness.
- **The prevailing assumption:** the placenta was a barrier. This was **an assumption, never a finding** — and it functioned as an axiom. Name it as such; it's a beautiful example of an unexamined premise doing enormous work.
- Roughly 10,000 affected infants worldwide with phocomelia and other malformations before withdrawal in 1961.
- The detection: McBride and Lenz, from clinical observation of a rare and unmistakable malformation pattern — again, Chapter 4.3's rule.
- **Frances Oldham Kelsey** at the FDA withheld US approval over concerns about the adequacy of the safety data, sparing the US the worst of it.
- **The consequence:** the 1962 Kefauver–Harris Amendments, which for the first time required manufacturers to demonstrate **efficacy**, not merely safety, before marketing in the US. **A regulatory regime born from a disaster — the pattern for almost every major reform in this book.** Note it, and ask the uncomfortable question: does medicine only reform after catastrophe? Examine the evidence for and against.

### 23.5 — Shorter cases

- **Neonatal chloramphenicol and grey baby syndrome** — adult dosing extrapolated to immature hepatic conjugation.
- **Hexachlorophene bathing** in newborn nurseries.
- **Routine electronic fetal monitoring** — near-universal adoption; the evidence indicates a substantial rise in caesarean and instrumental delivery with, at best, a small effect on neonatal seizures and no clear effect on cerebral palsy. A live, enormous, ongoing example.
- **Bed rest for threatened preterm labour** — ineffective and harmful (thromboembolism, deconditioning, economic and psychological cost).

### 23.6 — The pattern

> **Universal interventions on healthy populations demand the highest evidence standard in medicine, because the harmed are numerous, the harm is diluted, and the beneficiaries are invisible.** Medicine has consistently applied the *lowest* standard to exactly these interventions, because they seem gentle.

**Word budget:** 11,000

## CHAPTER 24 — The Mind
### *Flaw: unfalsifiable outcomes, powerless patients, and desperate families*

**The Argument:** Psychiatry and neurosurgery for mental illness constitute the domain where every protective mechanism in medicine failed simultaneously — and the reasons are instructive rather than exceptional.

**Tone instruction:** Avoid anti-psychiatry polemic. It is lazy, it is unfair to a field treating genuine and severe suffering with inadequate tools, and it will cost you readers you need. The critique must be methodological, not ideological.

### 24.1 — Why psychiatry is structurally vulnerable

Lay this out first, as the chapter's analytic frame:
- **Outcomes are subjective and rater-dependent.** No biomarker, no imaging endpoint, no autopsy confirmation.
- **Placebo response is large and rising** in psychiatric trials — a documented phenomenon that makes drug–placebo separation increasingly hard to demonstrate.
- **Diagnostic categories are constructed**, revised by committee, and their boundaries move.
- **Patients have historically had reduced legal and social power to refuse or complain**, especially those detained.
- **Families are desperate** and often the ones consenting.
- **Natural history is episodic and relapsing** — maximum exposure to regression to the mean (2.2).

**Every single one of these is an amplifier of the flaws in Part II.** Say so; that framing turns a grim chapter into a rigorous one.

### 24.2 — Case: Prefrontal lobotomy

- **Egas Moniz**, leucotomy from 1935, based on limited human observation and a thin animal rationale.
- **The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 1949.** Emphasise this. The highest honour in science, awarded for a procedure now regarded as one of medicine's worst mistakes. **The Nobel Committee is not a stupid institution. That is the point.**
- **Walter Freeman** and the transorbital "ice pick" leucotomy: performed rapidly, sometimes in non-surgical settings, in some cases without an operating theatre; performed on adolescents; performed on people whose primary problem was disruptive behaviour rather than psychosis.
- **Scale:** tens of thousands in the US alone, and comparable programmes elsewhere.
- **Why it was accepted:**
  - Asylums were overcrowded, and there was almost nothing else. **This is the real reason, and it must be stated with compassion.** The alternative was lifelong institutional confinement.
  - The outcome measure was **institutional manageability** — a patient who no longer screamed, no longer resisted, no longer required restraint was recorded as improved. **Whose outcome was being measured? The institution's.** This is the chapter's central question and it generalises far beyond psychiatry: *whenever an outcome measure serves the provider rather than the patient, expect systematic error.*
  - Follow-up was conducted by the operator, unblinded, without controls, with no standardised instrument.
  - Devastating harms — apathy, disinhibition, loss of initiative, personality obliteration — were the *expected effects of the procedure* and so were normalised rather than counted as complications.
- **What ended it:** partly chlorpromazine from the mid-1950s (a replacement, not a refutation — an important distinction; the practice was displaced, not disproven), partly public revulsion, partly the deinstitutionalisation movement.
- **The uncomfortable question to leave with the reader:** it was never subjected to a randomised trial. It was abandoned for reasons that were largely *not* epistemic. **We got the right answer for the wrong reasons — which means the system that produced it is not fixed.**

### 24.3 — Case: Study 329 and paroxetine in adolescents

- The 2001 publication in a leading child psychiatry journal reporting paroxetine as effective and well tolerated for adolescent major depression.
- **The RIAT reanalysis (*BMJ*, 2015):** independent researchers, working from the clinical study report and appendices under the Restoring Invisible and Abandoned Trials initiative, concluded that paroxetine was **not** effective on the pre-specified primary outcomes and that harms — including suicidal ideation and behaviour — had been under-represented in the original publication.
- **Teach the mechanics:** primary outcomes were not met; secondary outcomes were emphasised; adverse events were coded in ways that obscured the signal. **No fabrication was required.** Every step was a defensible-looking choice. That is the point (revisit 15.1's grey zone).
- **The reform this case powers:** access to clinical study reports and individual patient data. Connect directly to Chapter 37.

### 24.4 — Case: Antidepressant efficacy and the publication record

- **Turner et al. (*NEJM*, 2008)** — the FDA registry comparison (see 14.1).
- **Kirsch's meta-analyses** and the drug–placebo difference varying with baseline severity; the vigorous and legitimate methodological debate that followed.
- **The mature position you should model:** antidepressants have real effects, most clearly in severe depression; the *published* literature substantially overstated the average benefit; both statements are true; and holding both simultaneously is what competent reading looks like. **Refuse the two available cheap positions ("antidepressants are a scam" / "the critics are anti-science"). Show your reader what genuine intellectual balance costs and why it's worth paying.**

### 24.5 — Case: The chemical imbalance narrative

- Trace how a research hypothesis (monoamine deficiency) became a public explanatory story, promoted in advertising and in clinical conversation, long after the research literature had moved on.
- The 2022 umbrella review of the serotonin hypothesis and the fierce, largely productive debate it triggered.
- **The key distinction, and be careful here:** a drug can work without the mechanism story being true. Aspirin worked for decades before anyone understood prostaglandins. **The failure of the serotonin story is a failure of *explanation*, not automatically of *efficacy*.** Many commentators conflated the two, in both directions. Teach the reader to keep them separate. This section is a superb exercise in the discipline the whole book teaches.

### 24.6 — The pattern and the live version

Live candidates: the rapid expansion of psychedelic therapy on trials with essentially unblindable interventions and highly enthusiastic investigators (**a textbook configuration for over-estimation** — say so, while acknowledging genuine promise); digital mental health applications with almost no outcome evidence; expanding diagnostic boundaries in child psychiatry; ketamine clinics.

**Word budget:** 10,000

## CHAPTER 25 — Being Wrong in the Right Direction
### *The ulcer story, and what a successful reversal looks like*

**The Argument:** Not all reversals remove a treatment. Some install one. This chapter is the book's structural relief valve, and it teaches the correction machinery working — slowly, painfully, but working.

**Why this chapter exists:** Placed at the mid-point of Part III on purpose. The reader has just endured nine chapters of harm. If you don't give them a working system now, you lose them to despair — and despair is the wrong lesson.

### 25.1 — The old model

Peptic ulcer disease as a disorder of acid, stress, personality, diet, and lifestyle. Treatment: antacids, bland diets, rest, anticholinergics, then H2 blockers and proton pump inhibitors, and — for the severely affected — **surgery**: vagotomy, pyloroplasty, partial gastrectomy, with their own considerable long-term morbidity. Ulcers relapsed endlessly, which fitted the model perfectly: a chronic constitutional disorder requiring lifelong management. **A wrong theory that accommodates its own failures is the most durable kind (0.2.1, species 9).**

### 25.2 — The observation

Robin Warren, a pathologist in Perth, repeatedly saw curved bacteria in gastric biopsies from patients with gastritis. The prevailing conviction was that the stomach was sterile — **that no bacterium could survive gastric acid.** So the organisms had been seen before, over decades, by many pathologists, and dismissed as contaminants.

**Stop and make the reader feel this.** The evidence was literally under the microscope, in plain sight, for a century. It was not *absent*. It was **unseeable**, because a theory said it could not be there. This is the purest demonstration of theory-laden observation in the book and it belongs in the reader's memory forever.

### 25.3 — The proof and the resistance

Barry Marshall's culture success (aided by an accidentally prolonged incubation over Easter — include it; the role of luck in discovery is real and under-reported). The self-experiment: Marshall drank a culture, developed gastritis, and demonstrated it endoscopically. The years of rejection at conferences and in journals. Then the treatment trials, the consensus conferences, and eventually the 2005 Nobel Prize.

### 25.4 — Why the resistance, analysed

Run the same analytic frame as Semmelweis, and show the reader they are the same shape:
- A mechanistic axiom ("no bacteria in the stomach") overriding direct observation — **Chapter 17's flaw exactly.**
- Professional identity: gastroenterologists, gastric surgeons, and a very large pharmaceutical market in acid suppression.
- Geographic and institutional marginality of the discoverers.
- The claim's *simplicity* worked against it: a curable infection was too neat for a disease everyone knew was complex and constitutional.

### 25.5 — The lag, and the harm inside it

Quantify. From Warren's observations (1979–82) and Marshall's work (1982–84) to widespread eradication therapy took roughly a decade in most health systems, with substantial international variation. In the interim: gastrectomies performed, lifelong acid suppression prescribed, ulcers recurred, and some patients died of complications of a curable infection.

### 25.6 — The lessons, stated as principles

1. **Being right is not sufficient. Being right, persistent, lucky, and eventually institutionally endorsed is what it takes.**
2. **The correction machinery works — on a timescale of decades, at a cost measured in patients.**
3. **Nobel Prizes are lagging indicators.**
4. And the necessary caution: **the fact that a maverick was right once does not mean mavericks are usually right.** Survivorship bias (10.4) applies to heroes too. For every Marshall there are a hundred confident outsiders who were simply wrong, and you never hear about them. **Write this paragraph carefully and prominently — it is your defence against the book being cited by cranks.** Every quack in the world invokes Semmelweis and Marshall. Deny them the use of your book, explicitly, in print.

**Word budget:** 8,000

## CHAPTER 26 — The Citation That Killed a Generation
### *Flaw: evidence laundering — how weak claims acquire authority through repetition*

**The Argument:** The North American opioid crisis is the clearest documented instance of a body of "evidence" that was constructed rather than discovered, and it demonstrates every level of the four-level stack operating together.

**Tone instruction:** This chapter has real villains (tier 3 culpability), which makes it *easier* to write and therefore more dangerous. Do not let the villains crowd out the structural analysis — the marketing only worked because the epistemic terrain was already prepared.

### 26.1 — The prepared ground

Before the marketing, there was a genuine problem: **opioid-phobia meant cancer patients and post-surgical patients were genuinely under-treated for severe pain.** The reform movement was ethically correct and evidence-based *for that population*. Establish this properly; it is essential for fairness, and it explains why so many good clinicians were persuaded.

### 26.2 — Case: The Porter and Jick letter

- **1980:** a five-sentence letter to the editor in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, reporting that among hospitalised inpatients who received at least one narcotic, documented addiction was rare.
- **What it was:** a retrospective chart review of *hospitalised* patients, receiving *supervised, short-term* opioids, with *addiction* defined by chart documentation. It was not a study of outpatient chronic opioid therapy. It was not a study at all in any meaningful sense — it was a letter.
- **What it became:** cited hundreds of times, overwhelmingly as evidence that opioid therapy for **chronic non-cancer pain** carries a low addiction risk. Leung et al. (*NEJM*, 2017) analysed the citations and found the large majority cited it uncritically, and many grossly mischaracterised what it had examined.
- **Name the mechanism: evidence laundering.** A weak source is cited by a stronger source; the second source is cited by a third, which cites only the second; by the fifth generation the claim carries the authority of the entire chain and the original caveats have been stripped. **Nobody lied at any individual step.** Teach the reader to trace citations to the source, always, and show them how quick it is.

### 26.3 — Case: "Pain as the fifth vital sign"

- Promoted from the mid-1990s by professional bodies; adopted in the Veterans Health Administration; embedded in accreditation standards from around 2001.
- **The chain from Chapter 13.3, running perfectly:** an ethical aspiration → a professional recommendation → a measurement mandate → a quality metric → a satisfaction score → a payment consequence.
- **The mechanism of harm:** you cannot mandate a number without creating pressure to move the number. A clinician whose institution is scored on patient-reported pain has an incentive to prescribe. **Goodhart's law in a hospital: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.** Introduce Goodhart formally here; it recurs.

### 26.4 — Case: OxyContin and the marketing apparatus

- The controlled-release formulation, and the claim that its pharmacokinetics reduced abuse potential and addiction risk — a **mechanistic claim about a behavioural outcome**, which is Chapter 1's flaw operating in the most consequential possible register.
- The 2007 guilty plea regarding misbranding; the subsequent litigation and its documentary record.
- Sales force targeting, speaker programmes, funded CME, funded advocacy organisations, and prescriber data purchased to identify high-volume targets.
- The role of KOLs and of a small number of pain-medicine academics.

### 26.5 — The reckoning and the overcorrection

Essential for the book's integrity: the response — abrupt tapering, rigid prescribing limits, and the flight of patients from prescribed opioids to illicit fentanyl — **created new harms**, including in patients on stable long-term therapy who were destabilised or abandoned. The CDC's 2016 guideline was widely applied more rigidly than its authors intended, prompting a 2019 clarification and a 2022 revision.

**The general principle — write it as a law of the book:**

> **The correction of a medical error is itself a medical intervention, and it requires the same evidence, the same care, and the same monitoring for harm as the original.**

Almost nobody says this. It is one of the most valuable original contributions your book can make. Give it its own boxed page.

### 26.6 — The pattern

Evidence laundering + measurement mandates + commercial amplification + a genuine unmet need. Look for that combination. It is present today in several places; name at least two.

**Word budget:** 9,000

## CHAPTER 27 — The Screening Wars
### *Flaw: the popularity paradox and the unfalsifiable good deed*

**The Argument:** Screening produces the most politically protected errors in medicine, because its harms are statistical and its false benefits are personal, photogenic, and organised.

*(Chapter 12 taught the logic. This chapter runs the histories.)*

### 27.1 — Case: PSA screening for prostate cancer

- The test's origin as a monitoring tool for known disease, and its drift into population screening — **indication creep with a laboratory assay.**
- **ERSPC** (European, 2009 and later follow-ups): a modest relative reduction in prostate cancer mortality, with substantial numbers needed to screen and to diagnose to prevent one death.
- **PLCO** (US, 2009): no significant difference — later shown to be heavily compromised by **contamination**, with high rates of PSA testing in the control arm. Use this to teach contamination as a design threat, and to teach that "two trials disagreed" often has a discoverable technical explanation rather than a mystery.
- **The harms:** biopsy complications, overdiagnosis, and treatment consequences — incontinence and erectile dysfunction in a large fraction of those treated.
- **The evolution:** active surveillance, risk-stratified screening, MRI-first pathways, and shared decision-making. **A genuine improvement produced by taking the criticism seriously — say so; it's a success story hiding inside a controversy.**
- **Richard Ablin**, who identified PSA, publicly describing its use as a population screen as a costly public health disaster. Include it, with context.

### 27.2 — Case: Mammography

The most contested topic in the book. **Your job here is not to reach a verdict; it is to teach the reader to understand a live scientific disagreement.** Structure it as:
- What everyone agrees on.
- The specific quantities in dispute (magnitude of mortality reduction; magnitude of overdiagnosis; the right age to start and stop; the right interval).
- Why the trials are hard to interpret (age, era, treatment improvements since the trials were run, screening technology changes, and the way the denominators are chosen).
- The main positions and their strongest arguments — the Cochrane review, the Canadian NBSS and its methodological critics, the USPSTF, and the specialty societies.
- **What evidence would resolve it, and why it will probably never be generated.**

This section, done well, will be the most respected in your book. Done badly, it will be the one that gets you dismissed. Take six months over it and have both camps read it.

### 27.3 — Case: Thyroid cancer in South Korea

The cleanest natural experiment in the overdiagnosis literature (see 12.4). Add the political dimension: physicians defending the practice; a group of doctors publicly campaigning against it; the observable fall in incidence after the campaign, with **mortality unchanged** — the definitive demonstration that the extra cancers were never going to kill anyone.

### 27.4 — Case: Neuroblastoma screening in infants

Japan's mass programme; the controlled evaluations in Quebec and Germany; incidence roughly doubled with no mortality reduction; the programme's discontinuation. **The purest demonstration that a detected cancer can be a non-disease**, because the natural history includes spontaneous regression.

### 27.5 — Case: Lung cancer screening — the counterexample

Include this deliberately. Low-dose CT screening in high-risk smokers (NLST, NELSON) does reduce lung cancer mortality. **This is a screening programme that works.** Its inclusion prevents the reader concluding "screening is bad," which is the wrong lesson and a dangerous one. Then analyse *why* it works: high-risk population (raising prior probability), effective treatment for early disease, and a well-defined target. **Those are the conditions. Teach them as a checklist.**

### 27.6 — The pattern and the live version

Live: whole-body MRI and executive health screening; multi-cancer early detection blood tests (enormous investment, mortality evidence pending — **watch this one, it is the biggest live test of everything in this chapter**); consumer genomic risk scores; incidentalomas from imaging done for other reasons.

**Word budget:** 10,000

## CHAPTER 28 — Thinking Under Fire
### *Flaw: the collapse of evidentiary standards under emergency conditions*

**The Argument:** COVID-19 was a natural experiment on medicine's epistemic system under maximum stress, and it produced, within eighteen months, both the worst and the best examples in this entire book.

**Tone instruction:** This is the chapter where you will be accused of bad faith by everyone. Write it so that a reader from any political position finishes it feeling fairly treated, and so that neither "the experts were always right" nor "the experts were frauds" survives contact with the evidence. That requires **relentless specificity**: name the claim, the date, the evidence available on that date, and the confidence expressed.

### 28.1 — Why emergencies break epistemics

- Desperation lowers evidentiary thresholds (Chapter 5.2), now at population scale.
- Decisions must be made faster than evidence can be generated. **This is a genuine, irreducible dilemma, not a failure — state that clearly and early.**
- Preprints bypass review; media amplification is instantaneous.
- Positions become political identities, and identity makes belief revision costly.
- **Communication asymmetry:** honest uncertainty is heard as incompetence, so officials face pressure to overstate confidence — which then destroys credibility when the guidance changes. **This dynamic is the single most damaging thing that happened to public trust, and it was largely a communication design failure rather than a scientific one.** Argue this properly; it is one of your book's most useful contributions.

### 28.2 — Case: Hydroxychloroquine

- The in-vitro rationale and the early small, non-randomised, methodologically weak studies.
- Rapid political adoption; emergency use authorisation; enormous off-label use; supply shortages affecting lupus and rheumatoid arthritis patients who needed it.
- The large randomised trials — RECOVERY's hydroxychloroquine arm, SOLIDARITY — showing no benefit.
- **The Surgisphere episode:** a paper in *The Lancet* reporting harm, based on a database from an obscure company, retracted within about two weeks after external scrutiny found the data implausible. Note that it was **caught fast, by open criticism**, and that the retraction was in some quarters weaponised as evidence that the drug worked. **Two failures pointing in opposite directions in the same story.** Use it to show the reader that "the establishment was wrong" and "the drug didn't work" are both true and not in tension.

### 28.3 — Case: Ivermectin

- The in-vitro concentration issue — activity at concentrations far above achievable human plasma levels. **A superb, concrete teaching example of why in-vitro results are not clinical results.** Do the arithmetic on the page.
- The meta-analyses driven by a small number of trials later found to be fraudulent or irreproducible (the Elgazzar preprint being the most prominent), and what happened to the pooled estimates when those studies were removed.
- The large well-conducted trials (TOGETHER, ACTIV-6, PRINCIPLE) showing no meaningful benefit.
- **The methodological lesson:** meta-analysis inherits the integrity of its inputs. Garbage in, *authoritative-looking* garbage out. Introduce research-integrity screening of trials before pooling (the "trustworthiness" assessment movement) as the response.

### 28.4 — Case: RECOVERY and dexamethasone — the triumph

**Give this equal space to the failures.** It is the best thing in the chapter and possibly in the book.

- A simple, pragmatic, adaptive platform trial embedded in routine NHS care, with minimal data collection, broad eligibility, and randomisation performed by clinicians in minutes.
- Enrolled tens of thousands of patients at a speed no conventional trial structure could match.
- **June 2020:** dexamethasone reduced mortality in patients requiring respiratory support. A cheap, widely available, off-patent steroid. **Estimated to have saved on the order of a million lives worldwide.**
- Also definitively answered hydroxychloroquine, lopinavir–ritonavir, azithromycin, and convalescent plasma — **the negative answers are as valuable as the positive one**, because each one stopped a global waste of resources and exposure to harm.
- **The lesson — and it must be stated as the chapter's thesis:** the speed of the emergency was not the reason standards collapsed. **RECOVERY proves that rigour and speed are compatible.** What collapsed was the *willingness* to randomise. Every patient given hydroxychloroquine off-trial was a patient who could have been generating knowledge. **The alternative to randomisation is not faster treatment; it is permanent ignorance.**

Then generalise it beyond the pandemic: **why is medicine not organised like this all the time?** Embedded, pragmatic, low-cost randomisation inside routine care is technically feasible today. That question carries the reader into Part IV and is one of the book's genuine policy arguments.

### 28.5 — Shorter cases and honest reckonings

Handle each briefly, precisely, and with the "what was knowable when" discipline:
- **Early ventilation strategies** and the rapid revision of intubation thresholds.
- **Surface transmission and fomite cleaning** — extensive resources spent on a route later assessed as minor relative to aerosol transmission; the delayed formal acknowledgement of aerosol spread and *why* the delay happened (an institutional definition of "airborne" inherited from an older framework — **a wrong taxonomy causing a wrong policy**, which is Chapter 16's flaw in a modern setting).
- **Convalescent plasma** — emergency authorisation on weak data; subsequent trials largely null.
- **School closure and other non-pharmaceutical interventions** — where the evidence was genuinely thin, where the trade-offs were value judgements rather than scientific findings, and where those two categories were conflated in public communication. **Teach the reader to separate empirical claims from value claims; the conflation of the two is the deepest problem in pandemic communication and it is entirely fixable.**

### 28.6 — The pattern

Emergencies do not create new thinking flaws. **They remove the friction that normally slows them down**, and they compress a thirty-year reversal cycle into eighteen months — which means COVID is the best available laboratory for studying the whole process. Frame the chapter that way from its first line.

**Word budget:** 11,000

## CHAPTER 29 — When the Correction Was Wrong
### *Flaw: treating a negative trial as a final verdict*

**The Argument:** The machinery that corrects error can itself err, and a book that teaches only scepticism toward positive findings has taught half a skill.

**Why this chapter exists:** It is the chapter that makes your book trustworthy. Anyone can write "trials show the treatment doesn't work." Only a serious thinker asks **"under what conditions is a negative trial wrong?"** This chapter will distinguish your book from every competitor.

### 29.1 — Why negative trials fail

Enumerate the mechanisms systematically — this is the reader's checklist:
- **Underpowered** — absence of evidence read as evidence of absence.
- **Wrong population** — the effect exists in a subgroup diluted out by everyone else.
- **Wrong dose, wrong timing, wrong duration.**
- **Immature technology or a learning curve** — the intervention was performed badly.
- **Contamination or crossover** — the control group received the intervention.
- **Wrong endpoint or insufficient follow-up.**
- **Improved standard care** — the control arm is now too good to beat (revisit EGDT, 21.3).

### 29.2 — Case: Endovascular thrombectomy for acute ischaemic stroke

The definitive example, and a genuinely thrilling story.

- **2013:** three randomised trials (IMS III, MR RESCUE, SYNTHESIS Expansion) published together, all essentially negative. The reasonable conclusion at the time was that mechanical clot retrieval did not improve outcomes.
- **2015:** five trials (MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT PRIME, REVASCAT) reported dramatic benefit — among the largest treatment effects in modern neurology.
- **Why the reversal-of-the-reversal?** Three concrete changes, and this is the teaching payload:
  1. **Better devices** — second-generation stent retrievers versus the earlier devices used in the 2013 trials, with far higher recanalisation rates.
  2. **Better patient selection** — mandatory CT angiography to confirm a large-vessel occlusion. **The 2013 trials had enrolled patients who did not have a clot to retrieve.** You cannot demonstrate the benefit of removing a clot in patients without one.
  3. **Faster workflow.**
- **The lesson, stated as a rule:** *a negative trial tests a specific intervention, delivered in a specific way, to a specific population, at a specific moment in the technology's development.* It does not test the idea. **"Thrombectomy doesn't work" was never what those trials showed; "this device, in these patients, at this speed, did not work" was.**
- **And the counter-lesson, which you must also give, or you have handed every proponent of every failed therapy a permanent excuse:** this argument is **the single most common defence offered for practices that genuinely do not work.** Every failed intervention's advocates claim wrong population, wrong dose, wrong timing. So the reader needs a discriminator.

### 29.3 — The discriminator — how to tell a real "wrong trial" from an excuse

**This is the most valuable section in the chapter. Write it as an explicit checklist.**

A "the trial was wrong" argument deserves to be taken seriously when:
1. The proposed modification is **pre-specified and mechanistically motivated**, not reverse-engineered from the subgroup that happened to look good.
2. There is a **specific, identifiable technical difference** (a device generation, a defined selection criterion) rather than a general appeal to expertise.
3. The proponents are **willing to run the new trial** — and actually do.
4. The claim makes a **falsifiable prediction** about what the new trial will show.
5. It does not require the previous trialists to have been incompetent or corrupt.

Contrast, item by item, with vertebroplasty and knee arthroscopy advocacy, where the objections were largely post-hoc, unfalsifiable, and unaccompanied by new trials. **Being fair to both cases is what makes the checklist credible.**

### 29.4 — Case: Therapeutic hypothermia after cardiac arrest

- **2002:** two trials (HACA; Bernard et al.) showed benefit from cooling to 32–34°C. Rapid worldwide adoption; guidelines; cooling devices.
- **TTM (2013):** 33°C versus 36°C — **no difference.** Suggesting the benefit was from avoiding fever, not from cooling.
- **TTM2 (2021):** hypothermia versus normothermia with fever control — **no benefit.**
- **A reversal in slow motion**, with the intermediate step (TTM) reframing rather than refuting. Excellent for teaching that reversals are often *decompositions*: the active ingredient was not the one we thought.

### 29.5 — Case: Beta-blockers in heart failure

The inverse story, and essential for balance. Beta-blockade in heart failure was long considered contraindicated on impeccable mechanistic grounds — you do not suppress contractility in a failing heart. **The mechanism was completely wrong**, and trials (CIBIS-II, MERIT-HF, COPERNICUS) showed substantial mortality reduction. **A treatment nearly lost to mechanistic reasoning in the other direction.** Pair it explicitly with Chapter 1 to show that the flaw is *over-reliance on mechanism*, not the direction it points.

### 29.6 — The pattern

> **Trials are not verdicts; they are measurements, with a scope of validity.** The skill is not "believe trials over mechanism" — it is knowing what a given piece of evidence can and cannot support. **That, and not scepticism, is the actual competence this book teaches.** Say it here, plainly, because it is the book's real thesis and the reader is now ready for it.

## CHAPTER 30 — The Control Group
### *What medicine got right, and why*

**The Argument:** The same system that produced this book's catalogue of disasters also produced the greatest reduction in human suffering in history. Understanding what distinguishes the successes is the whole practical payoff.

**Why this chapter exists:** Without it, the book is nihilism with footnotes, and nihilism kills. Place it at the end of Part III so the reader carries it into Parts IV–VII.

### 30.1 — Case: Antenatal corticosteroids

- Liggins and Howie's 1972 trial: corticosteroids given to women in preterm labour reduced neonatal respiratory distress syndrome and death.
- **The lag:** despite further trials, uptake remained patchy for nearly two decades — **many obstetricians did not know**, because the evidence was scattered across small trials.
- **Crowley's systematic review and meta-analysis (1990)** demonstrated a large and unambiguous mortality benefit.
- **The Cochrane Collaboration's logo is a forest plot of these trials** — a visual argument that the evidence had been sufficient long before practice changed.
- **The lesson: this is a reversal of a *non-adoption*.** The failure to give an effective treatment is exactly as much a thinking failure as giving an ineffective one, and it is far less studied. **Make sure your book gives non-adoption real weight** — it is the blind spot of the entire medical-reversal literature, and treating it seriously is an original contribution.

### 30.2 — Case: Hand hygiene, finally

From Semmelweis to modern infection control: alcohol gel, bundles, checklists, surveillance, feedback, and the recognition that **behaviour change requires system design, not exhortation** (8.5). Include the honest fact that compliance remains imperfect in most hospitals to this day.

### 30.3 — Case: Vaccines

- Smallpox eradication (1980) — the only human disease eradicated by deliberate action.
- Polio, measles, Hib, pneumococcus, HPV, rotavirus.
- **Include the failures honestly** — the Cutter incident of 1955, rotavirus (RotaShield) withdrawal and intussusception, narcolepsy and Pandemrix — because a vaccine chapter that acknowledges real harms is a hundred times more persuasive than one that doesn't, and because the honest reckoning is *exactly what the system did*.
- **The analytic point:** vaccines are the domain where the evidence system worked *best* — large trials, mandatory surveillance, transparent adverse event reporting, and rapid withdrawal when harms were found. **Then the paradox to sit with: it is also the domain with the greatest public distrust.** Explore why, seriously (see 45). Do not sneer. The answer involves omission bias (8.3), the invisibility of prevented disease, and — crucially — **the fact that the profession's genuine record of error, catalogued in this very book, is what makes the distrust plausible.** Your book has a responsibility here. Meet it.

### 30.4 — Case: Tobacco and the Bradford Hill criteria

- Doll and Hill's British Doctors Study; the Surgeon General's report of 1964.
- Bradford Hill's 1965 address on association and causation: strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment, analogy — **presented by Hill himself as considerations, not a checklist**, a nuance almost always lost.
- **Why include it:** it shows causal inference done well *without* randomisation, which the reader needs so they don't conclude that only RCTs count.
- The industry's counter-campaign — manufactured doubt, funded contrarian science, the demand for impossible certainty — as the template for later campaigns in other domains.

### 30.5 — Case: HIV/AIDS

An extraordinary success story with hard lessons: activists demanding faster access, the tension between access and evidence, the eventual construction of a rigorous and rapid trial infrastructure, the validation of viral load as one of the *good* surrogates, and combination therapy transforming a fatal disease into a manageable one. **ACT UP is the best case study in the book of patient advocacy improving science rather than distorting it** — contrast explicitly with the ABMT advocacy in Chapter 22, where advocacy destroyed the trials. **What was different?** Answer it: the AIDS activists learned the methodology and demanded better trials; the ABMT advocacy demanded access outside trials. That distinction is enormously important and almost never made.

### 30.6 — What the successes have in common

Extract the pattern as a list, and make it the practical thesis of the book:
1. **Hard, objective endpoints** — death, eradication, incidence.
2. **Large effect sizes**, or large trials.
3. **Randomisation early, before the practice became entrenched.**
4. **A culture of surveillance after adoption**, not just before.
5. **Willingness to withdraw** when harms appeared.
6. **Systematic synthesis** rather than reliance on individual studies.
7. **Advocates who demanded better evidence** rather than more access.

**Then the closing line of Part III:** every failure in the preceding fourteen chapters is missing at least three items from that list. Have the reader go back and check. That exercise is the book's graduation.
