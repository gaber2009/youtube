# Chapter 47
# Sponsorship and Its Effects

> *The most disturbing finding in this book: industry bias survives the tools designed to detect bias.*

---

Everyone knows that industry-funded research tends to favour the funder's product. It is the least surprising claim in this book, and treating it as a slogan means missing what is actually interesting about it.

The interesting question is not *whether* sponsorship shifts results. It does, and the evidence is about as consistent as evidence gets. The interesting question is **how** — because the answer determines whether the appraisal skills you spent Part V acquiring are sufficient.

They are not. That is this chapter.

---

## 47.1 The Story: the bias that survives quality assessment

The definitive work is a Cochrane methodology review by Lundh and colleagues, which pooled studies comparing industry-sponsored research with research funded by other sources.

The findings:

| What was compared | Risk ratio for a favourable result |
|---|---|
| Favourable **efficacy** results | **1.32** (95% CI 1.21 to 1.44) |
| Favourable **harms** results | **1.87** (95% CI 1.54 to 2.27) |
| Favourable **overall conclusions** | **1.31** |

Industry-sponsored drug and device studies are around a third more likely to report favourable efficacy findings, and — look at that middle row — **nearly twice as likely to report favourable findings on harms.** The harms number is the one that should worry you most, and it is the one nobody quotes.

Now the finding that makes this chapter necessary, and that I would put on the wall of every journal club:

> **When the analysis was restricted to studies at low risk of bias, the relationship between sponsorship and favourable conclusions was not weakened. It was strengthened.**

Read that again. If sponsorship worked by producing methodologically shoddy trials, then filtering for good methodology should reduce the effect. It doesn't. **The industry effect cannot be explained by standard risk-of-bias assessment**, which means it operates through channels those tools do not measure.

So: a trial can be properly randomised, with concealed allocation, adequate blinding, complete follow-up, intention-to-treat analysis, and full CONSORT reporting — score green on every domain of RoB 2 — and still be systematically more likely to favour its funder's product.

The bias is not in the execution. **It is in the design decisions that precede execution, and in the reporting decisions that follow it** — and every one of those has already had a chapter in this book.

## 47.2 The mechanisms — a tour of the earlier chapters

Here is where the effect actually lives. Notice that every route is legitimate, defensible, and invisible to a risk-of-bias tool.

**The question (Chapter 15).** You choose which trials to run at all. Trials likely to embarrass the product are not commissioned. This filter operates before anything exists to appraise.

**The comparator (Chapter 18).** The dose, the drug, the schedule, the titration. This is the single largest lever, and Heres's circular antipsychotic results are the demonstration.

**The population (Chapter 15).** Enrolling patients most likely to respond and least likely to suffer harms — younger, healthier, fewer comorbidities, with a run-in period to exclude the intolerant.

**The outcome (Chapter 19).** Surrogates instead of patient-important endpoints; composites carried by their softest component; responder thresholds; the timepoint of maximum divergence.

**The duration (Chapter 15).** Long enough to show benefit, short enough to miss harm. This is a large part of the harms row in that table.

**Harms ascertainment (Chapter 19).** How actively you look. Passive reporting finds fewer adverse events than systematic questioning. Two trials can be equally rigorous and differ threefold in reported harms purely through how hard they looked — and nothing in a risk-of-bias tool asks.

**Analysis choices (Chapter 31).** Covariates, subgroups, handling of missing data, per-protocol versus intention-to-treat.

**Publication (Chapter 43, 45).** The decision to publish, where, and when. Contracts that grant sponsors review or veto rights over publication.

**Outcome reporting (Chapter 46).** What goes in the paper.

**Framing (Chapter 38).** The abstract, the conclusion, the press release.

**Repetition.** The same positive trial published multiple times in different forms, inflating its weight in later meta-analyses.

Now look back at that list. Every item is a decision that has to be made by somebody, in every trial, with genuine discretion and no single right answer. Sponsorship does not require anyone to break a rule. It requires only that a long series of judgement calls be made by people whose interests point consistently in one direction — and the aggregate of many small, defensible choices is a reliable bias.

**This is exactly the structure of Chapter 41's narrative review and Chapter 11's motivated reasoning.** The mechanism is the same at every scale in this book.

## 47.3 Three practices worth naming

Beyond design bias, some specific practices deserve to be known by name.

**Ghostwriting.** A paper is written by the sponsor's medical communications agency, then an academic is recruited to appear as author. The named author may edit the draft, may believe its contents, and did not write it or design the analysis. This has been documented in litigation discovery across several drug classes. It matters because the credibility of academic authorship is precisely the thing being purchased — a paper's persuasive force comes from an independent expert having judged the evidence, and that judgement did not happen.

**Seeding trials.** A trial designed principally as a marketing exercise: enrolling large numbers of prescribers to familiarise them with a new drug, under the appearance of research. The scientific question is secondary or absent; the objective is prescribing behaviour. Internal documents disclosed in litigation have shown such trials being planned by marketing departments. Patients in them consented to research.

**Key opinion leaders and continuing education.** Funding the speakers, the conference symposia and the educational materials through which a field learns what to prescribe. This is not corruption of individuals so much as construction of a landscape — and, per Chapter 11, the people involved will not experience it as influence.

## 47.4 Why disclosure is a weak remedy

The standard response to conflicts of interest is disclosure: state your funding and let the reader adjust.

There are three problems.

**Readers don't adjust well.** Knowing a trial was funded by its manufacturer tells you a bias exists but not where it acted or how large it was. Without the analysis in 47.2, "industry funded" is an uncalibrated warning that mostly produces either blanket dismissal or a shrug.

**Disclosure can license the discloser.** Work in behavioural economics has found that disclosing a conflict can make advisors feel *entitled* to give more biased advice — they have warned you, so the responsibility has transferred — while recipients often fail to discount enough, and may find it harder to discount someone who has just been candid with them. Disclosure is not neutral; it changes both parties' behaviour, and not always in the direction intended.

**It does nothing about the missing trials.** No disclosure statement tells you about the study that was never run or never published.

**Better remedies**, roughly in order of strength: independent conduct of the analysis; access to the raw data (Chapter 50); pre-registration with published protocols and analysis plans (Chapters 46, 53); mandatory results reporting (Chapter 45); excluding conflicted individuals from voting on guidelines (Chapter 44); and independent public funding for the comparative trials nobody has an interest in running (Chapter 18).

Every one of those works by removing a decision from the point where the interest can act. That is the same design principle as blinding, concealment and pre-registration — the principle this book keeps arriving at from every direction.

---

## 47.5 The Tool

**1. Find the funder and the authors' interests.** Always. Then treat it as a prior, not a verdict.

**2. Do not use funding to dismiss a trial.** Use it to decide **where to look hardest**, and go straight to 47.2's list. Specifically: the comparator and its dose; the outcome and whether it's a surrogate or composite; the duration relative to when harms appear; how harms were ascertained; and whether the primary outcome matches the registry.

**3. Look at harms with particular suspicion.** The effect there is nearly twice as large as for efficacy. Ask: how were adverse events collected — actively or passively? Over what period? Were withdrawals for adverse events reported?

**4. Check for independence of the analysis.** Who had access to the data? Who performed the statistics? A statement that the sponsor "had no role in the analysis" is worth more than one that omits the question, and can be checked against the author contributions.

**5. Look for the trial that isn't there.** Which comparison would most usefully inform your decision, and does it exist? (Chapter 18.)

**6. Remember that this applies beyond industry.** Academic careers, reputations and grant renewals are also interests, and a researcher who has built twenty years on a hypothesis is not disinterested. The mechanisms in 47.2 are available to anyone.

## 47.6 The Drill

### Drill 1 — Find the lever *(45 minutes)*
Take three industry-funded trials with positive results. For each, work through the list in 47.2 and identify which levers were actually available and which appear to have been used. Write one paragraph on the most plausible route.

### Drill 2 — Compare harms ascertainment *(30 minutes)*
Take two trials of the same drug class and compare exactly how adverse events were collected. Then compare the reported rates. Note whether the difference in method could explain the difference in result.

### Drill 3 — Trace an authorship *(30 minutes)*
Take a multi-author industry trial. Read the author contributions and acknowledgements. Identify who designed it, who analysed the data, who wrote the first draft, and whether a medical writing agency is acknowledged.

### Drill 4 — Apply it to yourself *(20 minutes)*
List your own interests in the questions you work on: funding, reputation, published positions, professional identity, the practice you were trained in. Then take one belief and ask which of the levers in 47.2 you would have available if you wanted it to come out your way.

## 47.7 The Verdict

> **CHAPTER 47 SUMMARY CARD**
>
> **The uninteresting claim:** industry-funded research favours the funder. True, and well established.
>
> **The interesting finding:** the Lundh Cochrane review found industry sponsorship associated with favourable **efficacy** results (RR **1.32**), favourable **overall conclusions** (RR **1.31**), and — the number nobody quotes — favourable **harms** results (RR **1.87**).
>
> **And the finding that makes this chapter necessary: restricting the analysis to studies at low risk of bias *strengthened* the relationship.** If sponsorship worked by producing shoddy trials, filtering for quality would weaken it. It doesn't. **Standard risk-of-bias assessment does not detect industry bias**, which means Part V is not sufficient on its own.
>
> **Where the bias actually lives — all legitimate, all invisible to RoB tools:** which trials get commissioned at all; the comparator and its dose (the largest lever); the population and run-in; the outcome, surrogate or composite; the duration — long enough for benefit, short enough to miss harm; **how hard anyone looked for adverse events**; analysis choices; the decision to publish; which outcomes get reported; the framing; and repetition inflating weight in later meta-analyses.
>
> **The structural point:** sponsorship requires nobody to break a rule. It requires only that many defensible judgement calls be made by people whose interests point the same way. Same mechanism as the narrative review (Ch. 41) and motivated reasoning (Ch. 11), at a different scale.
>
> **Three practices to know by name:** **ghostwriting** — which purchases exactly the thing that gives a paper its force, an independent expert's judgement, when that judgement never happened; **seeding trials** — research as a prescribing-behaviour exercise, with patients consenting to science; and **key opinion leaders and funded education**, which build the landscape a field learns in.
>
> **Why disclosure is weak:** readers don't calibrate; disclosure can *license* the discloser to give more biased advice while recipients under-discount; and it says nothing about the trials that were never run. Better: independent analysis, raw data access, pre-registration, mandatory reporting, excluding conflicted voters, and public funding for the comparisons nobody wants.
>
> **The practical rule:** never use funding to dismiss a trial. Use it to decide **where to look hardest** — and look hardest at harms, where the effect is nearly twice as large.
>
> **The sentence to carry:** *The bias is not in the execution. It is in the decisions before and after it — and those are the ones no checklist scores.*
