# Chapter 8
# The Placebo Is Real (and Weirder Than You Think)

> *"Just a placebo" is used to mean "nothing happened."*
> *Something happened. It just wasn't the thing on the label.*

---

In the last chapter we used an inert substance to derive, from first principles, why a trial needs a control group. That argument had a hole in it, and I left it there deliberately.

The hole is this. I told you that people in the control group get better because of natural history and regression to the mean — the impostors of Chapter 2. But there is a third thing going on, and it is stranger than either: **people get better because they were treated**, even when the treatment contains nothing at all. Not because they are lying, not because they are imagining it in the dismissive sense, but because the act of being treated is itself a physiological event.

This chapter is about that. And I want to warn you at the start that the topic is a minefield, because almost everything written about placebos in the popular press is wrong in one of two opposite directions:

**The dismissive error:** "It's just a placebo" — meaning nothing real occurred, the patient was fooled, we can ignore it. This is wrong. The improvement is often genuine, measurable, and sometimes traceable to specific neurochemistry.

**The credulous error:** "Placebos are astonishingly powerful, the mind can heal the body, therefore alternative medicine is fine actually." This is also wrong, and it is wrong for a reason almost nobody states clearly — a reason that turns out to be a straightforward arithmetic mistake, made in 1955, and still being repeated today.

Getting this right matters for more than accuracy. If you overestimate placebo effects, you will excuse useless treatments. If you underestimate them, you will design bad trials and misread good ones. And in both cases you will miss the genuinely important thing, which is that a large part of what makes medicine work has nothing to do with what is in the bottle.

---

## 8.1 The Story: the operation that worked, without the operation

In the 1950s, if you had severe angina — crushing chest pain on exertion, from a heart starved of blood — one of the treatments on offer was a procedure called internal mammary artery ligation.

The theory was reasonable. The internal mammary arteries run down the inside of the chest wall, near the heart. If you tied them off, the blood they carried would be diverted, and some of it, the reasoning went, would find its way into the coronary circulation. More blood to the heart, less angina.

And it worked. Not marginally — dramatically. Surgeons reported that a large proportion of patients experienced substantial relief. Patients who had been unable to climb stairs were walking again. Some threw away their nitroglycerin. The improvements were documented, published, and enthusiastically reported. The operation spread.

Then, in 1959, Leonard Cobb and colleagues at the University of Washington did something that would be extremely difficult to get past an ethics committee today. They ran a **sham-controlled trial**.

Patients were taken to theatre. They were anaesthetised. Incisions were made in the chest, exactly as for the real operation. In the control group, the internal mammary arteries were located — and then simply left alone. The incisions were closed. The patients, and the physicians assessing them afterwards, did not know which operation had been performed.

Cobb's results appeared in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1959. In 1960, Dimond and colleagues published a second sham-controlled trial with similar findings.

**Both groups improved. Equally.** Patients who had received nothing but skin incisions reported the same relief from angina as patients whose arteries had been tied. Exercise tolerance did not distinguish them.

The operation was abandoned.

Now, hold two facts in your head at once, because the whole chapter lives in the space between them.

**Fact one: the operation did nothing.** The mechanism was wrong; the ligation contributed no measurable benefit.

**Fact two: the patients got better.** This was not fabrication. People with a serious, objectively diagnosed disease reported large reductions in a symptom that had been dominating their lives, after receiving an intervention that consisted entirely of being cut and sewn up again.

If you find that unsettling, you have understood it correctly.

Henry Beecher, the Harvard anaesthetist, wrote about these trials in a paper he called "Surgery as Placebo," and the phrase captures the difficulty. We are used to thinking of a placebo as a sugar pill — a small deception at the margins of medicine. Here was a whole operating theatre functioning as one.

And note what made the discovery possible. Not better biology, not a bigger case series, not more careful follow-up. **A control group that received the ritual without the substance.** Everything in this chapter follows from that single design move.

## 8.2 The Trap: the mistake everyone makes, including the experts

Here is the most important distinction in this chapter, and the one that separates people who understand placebos from people who merely find them fascinating.

> **The improvement seen in a placebo group is not the placebo effect.**

Read it again, because the entire popular literature on this subject depends on ignoring it.

When you look at the placebo arm of a trial and see that 35% of patients improved, that 35% is a *sum*. It contains:

1. **Natural history.** The illness was going to get better anyway. Most illnesses do. *(Chapter 2)*
2. **Regression to the mean.** Patients enrolled when their symptoms were at their worst, and extremes are followed by less extreme values. *(Chapter 2)*
3. **The placebo effect proper.** A genuine change produced by expectation, conditioning and context.
4. **Measurement artefacts.** Patients who like their doctor report improvement to please them (social desirability). Scales have ceilings and floors. People behave differently when observed. Co-interventions creep in — patients in trials also rest more, take more paracetamol, and get more attention.

Only item 3 is the placebo effect. Items 1, 2 and 4 would have happened to someone who was given nothing at all.

### Beecher's arithmetic

In 1955, Henry Beecher — the same man, and a genuinely important figure in research ethics — published a famous paper called "The Powerful Placebo," in which he surveyed a set of trials, looked at how many patients improved in the placebo arms, and concluded that roughly a third of patients respond to placebo.

That number went everywhere. It is still quoted. It is still in textbooks and journalism and TED talks.

And it is an error of exactly the kind Chapter 1 warned you about: **Beecher took the full contents of one cell of the table and attributed all of it to one cause.** He had the "treated with placebo, got better" cell. He did not have the "treated with nothing, got better" cell. Without that second cell, the placebo effect cannot be separated from the natural course of the illness — and the natural course of most illnesses is improvement.

To measure a placebo effect you need **three arms**: active treatment, placebo, and *no treatment at all*. Almost no trial has the third. Which means almost every number you have ever heard attached to "the placebo effect" is not a measurement of the placebo effect.

### What happens when you actually do it properly

In 2001, Asbjørn Hróbjartsson and Peter Gøtzsche published a systematic review in the *New England Journal of Medicine* with the pointed title *"Is the Placebo Powerless?"* They did the obvious thing that nobody had done: they collected the trials that happened to include **both** a placebo arm **and** a no-treatment arm, and compared those two directly. That comparison isolates the placebo effect, and nothing else does.

They found 114 such trials. They updated the review in 2004 with 52 more.

The conclusion was considerably less exciting than "The Powerful Placebo," and considerably more useful:

- **On binary outcomes — did the infection clear, did the patient survive, did the ulcer heal — placebo showed no significant effect.** This held whether the binary outcome was objective or subjective.
- **On continuous, subjective outcomes — how much pain are you in, on a scale — placebo did show a real effect.** Modest, variable, but real. Pain was the clearest case.

So the honest summary, which you should carry for life:

> **Placebos change how people feel. They do not change the course of disease.**

A placebo does not shrink a tumour, clear an infection, mend a fracture, lower mortality, or repair a heart. A placebo does reliably alter pain, nausea, itch, fatigue, breathlessness, mood, and the subjective experience of almost any symptom.

This is not a small thing — an enormous fraction of human suffering *is* symptom experience, and a treatment that genuinely reduces pain is a treatment. But it is a bounded thing, and the boundary is exactly where the credulous error crosses it.

### Which lets you resolve most arguments instantly

Once you have this distinction, a great many disputes collapse into one question:

**Is the outcome a continuous, self-reported experience, or a discrete, objective event?**

Someone claims their therapy relieves chronic pain and fatigue. Plausible as a placebo effect — and therefore requires a placebo-controlled trial to say anything at all.

Someone claims their therapy shrinks tumours, cures diabetes, or reverses an infection. Not plausible as a placebo effect. If the effect is real it is something else; if the evidence is anecdotal, the impostors of Chapter 2 are the likelier explanation.

That single question will do more work for you than any amount of reading about mind-body medicine.

## 8.3 The Weird Part: placebos have a dose-response

Everything above might make placebos sound like a small statistical nuisance. They are not, and this is where the subject becomes genuinely strange.

**The placebo effect is not a constant.** It varies systematically with the *theatre* surrounding the treatment — and it varies in ways that are predictable, replicable, and slightly absurd.

**Two sugar pills work better than one.** The dose-response relationship, that hallmark of real pharmacology, appears in substances with no pharmacology at all.

**The form of delivery matters, in a clear hierarchy.** A placebo injection generally outperforms a placebo pill. A placebo procedure outperforms a placebo injection. Sham surgery — as the internal mammary story shows — sits at the top. The more elaborate and invasive the ritual, the larger the response.

**Colour and branding matter.** Studies of drug colour have reported that red, orange and yellow placebos tend to work better as stimulants, and blue and green as sedatives. In one well-known analysis, blue placebos worked as sedatives everywhere except among Italian men, where the effect reversed — a finding usually attributed, half-seriously, to the Italian national football team playing in blue. Branded placebos outperform unbranded ones; in one classic study, branded aspirin outperformed unbranded aspirin, and branded placebo outperformed unbranded placebo. (Treat the individual studies here with the scepticism this book teaches — several are small — but the general pattern that presentation matters is robust.)

**Price matters.** In a randomised study of a placebo analgesic, participants told the pill cost $2.50 reported more pain relief than those told it had been discounted to $0.10. The pill was identical.

**The practitioner matters most of all.** This is the finding I would most like you to remember. In a trial of acupuncture for irritable bowel syndrome, Kaptchuk and colleagues used three arms: a waiting list; sham acupuncture delivered with limited, businesslike interaction; and sham acupuncture delivered with an "augmented" consultation — warmth, attention, active listening, expressions of confidence, around twenty minutes longer.

Response rates climbed across the three arms. The augmented-interaction group did substantially better than the limited-interaction group, **with the same sham needles.** The difference was not the treatment. The difference was the relationship.

### It runs on real machinery

If this all sounds like people simply reporting what they think they should, there is direct physiological evidence against that.

In 1978, Levine, Gordon and Fields showed that placebo analgesia could be **blocked by naloxone** — the drug used to reverse opioid overdose. If placebo pain relief were merely polite reporting, an opioid antagonist would have no effect on it. That it does implies placebo analgesia is running, at least partly, on the body's own endogenous opioid system. The patient is not describing relief; they are manufacturing it, with the same chemistry morphine borrows.

Later work using PET imaging found that placebo administration in Parkinson's disease was associated with **dopamine release in the striatum** — the same neurotransmitter the actual medication supplies.

So the mechanism, as best we understand it, is roughly: **expectation and conditioning, delivered through ritual, recruiting endogenous systems the body already has.** Which explains the dose-response — a more elaborate ritual generates a stronger expectation. In a real sense, the ritual *is* the dose.

### The strangest result: it works when you tell them

If placebo effects run on expectation, then telling someone "this pill is inert" should abolish them. This seems obvious. It appears to be false.

In 2010, Kaptchuk and colleagues randomised 80 patients with irritable bowel syndrome to either **open-label placebo** — pills in a bottle labelled "placebo," described honestly as "placebo pills made of an inert substance, like sugar pills, that have been shown in clinical studies to produce significant improvement in IBS symptoms through mind-body self-healing processes" — or to a no-treatment control group **who received the same quality of interaction with the same providers.**

Note that design detail, because it is the whole point: matching the interaction means the difference between groups isolates the *pill ritual*, having already controlled for the relationship.

The open-label placebo group improved significantly more, at eleven days and at twenty-one days.

Honest placebos worked. This has since been replicated in other conditions, including chronic low back pain and cancer-related fatigue, and it remains one of the most genuinely surprising findings in the field. It is not fully explained. It suggests the effect depends less on believing a falsehood than on the ritual, the expectation of care, and possibly on conditioning — a lifetime of taking pills and then feeling better.

I want to be careful here: these are relatively small trials, in conditions with fluctuating subjective symptoms, and the field is young. But the direction is consistent, and if it holds it dissolves the ethical bind we will come to shortly.

## 8.4 Nocebo: the same machinery, running backwards

Everything above has a mirror image, and it does far more damage in practice than the placebo effect does good.

If expectation can produce relief, expectation can produce harm. Tell someone a pill may cause headaches, and some of them will get headaches. This is the **nocebo effect**, and it is the reason placebo arms in trials are full of side effects — real, reported, sometimes severe enough that patients withdraw.

The best study on this is a small masterpiece of design.

**SAMSON** — Self-Assessment Method for Statin side-effects Or Nocebo — took 60 patients who had *already stopped taking statins because of side effects*. Each patient received twelve one-month bottles in randomised order: four containing atorvastatin, four containing placebo, and **four containing nothing at all.** Each patient was their own control, across all three conditions. They recorded symptoms daily.

Look at what those empty bottles do. They are the no-treatment arm — the third cell that Beecher lacked. This design can separate "symptoms caused by the drug" from "symptoms caused by taking a tablet" from "symptoms that happen anyway."

The result: **around 90% of the symptom burden reported on the statin was also present on the placebo.** The pills were producing the symptoms, and the statin within them was contributing almost nothing. The investigators' summary is worth quoting: the side effects are very real, but they are mainly caused by the act of taking the tablet, not by the drug it contains.

And then the part that makes it a clinical result rather than a curiosity: after being shown their own data, **half the patients successfully restarted statin therapy.**

Consider what that means at scale. Very large numbers of people stop taking effective preventive medication because of symptoms that the medication is not causing. The nocebo effect is not a psychological footnote; it is a major cause of untreated cardiovascular risk.

### The ethical bind

Which produces a genuinely unresolved problem, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

Informed consent requires telling patients about possible side effects. Telling patients about possible side effects **causes** some of those side effects. The two obligations are in direct conflict, and there is no clean solution. Both horns are real: withholding information is a violation of autonomy, and reciting a list of symptoms demonstrably induces them.

There is active research on framing — presenting risks in ways that inform without priming, describing how many people *don't* get an effect rather than how many do, and "authorised concealment," where a patient consents in advance to not being told certain details. None of it is settled. When you meet a confident answer to this problem, be suspicious.

## 8.5 What this actually means for trials

Now the methodological payoff, which is the reason this chapter sits in a book about appraisal rather than a book about the mind.

### Why the control group is mandatory

You might think the argument is "placebo effects are large, so we need to subtract them." That is not quite it, and the real argument is better.

The real argument is that **from inside the treated group, you cannot decompose the improvement.** A patient got better. That improvement is some unknowable mixture of natural history, regression to the mean, placebo response, measurement artefact, and — possibly — the drug. There is no measurement you can make on that patient, however sophisticated, that separates the components.

And here is the elegant part, which I think is under-appreciated:

> **You do not need to know the size of any of the impostors. You need only to subject both groups to all of them equally.**

The control group absorbs natural history, regression to the mean and placebo response *simultaneously*, without you having to understand or quantify any of them. That is why the fair test is such a powerful idea, and why it worked in 1747 for a man with no theory of scurvy. The control group is not a measuring instrument for the impostors. It is a device for making them cancel.

### The size of the placebo response doesn't threaten a trial

This confuses people constantly, so let me be explicit. If a trial's placebo arm improves enormously, that does not weaken the trial. It just means both arms improved, and you read the *difference*. A drug that beats a strong placebo response is a good drug.

What a large placebo response *does* do is raise the stakes on blinding. If the ritual is doing a lot of work, then any leakage of information about who is in which group will move the results substantially. That is Chapter 9.

### Where it gets hard

**Surgery and devices.** Sham-controlled surgical trials are ethically difficult and rare, which is precisely why the surgical evidence base is weaker than the pharmaceutical one — and why, when sham trials finally get done, they so often deflate an established procedure (internal mammary ligation, arthroscopy for knee osteoarthritis, vertebroplasty). This is a structural gap in medical knowledge, not a coincidence.

**Drugs with obvious effects.** If your antidepressant causes a dry mouth and your placebo doesn't, patients will work out which arm they are in, and the blind is broken. The response is an **active placebo** — one that mimics the side effects without the hypothesised therapeutic action. It is rarely used, and its absence probably inflates the measured effect of a number of drug classes. Chapter 9.

**Unblindable interventions.** Exercise, surgery, psychotherapy, diet. You cannot conceal from someone that they have been doing yoga. The partial answers — blinded outcome assessment, active comparators, attention controls — are all imperfect, and appraising these trials well is a distinct skill (Chapter 9).

## 8.6 The ethics, honestly

Three positions get argued about, and they deserve separating.

**"Doctors should just prescribe placebos."** Surveys suggest a surprising amount of this happens already, usually in the softened form of prescribing something the doctor believes to be inert but harmless. The objections are real: it requires deception, it can create dependence on the practitioner, and it may medicalise problems that don't need it. Open-label placebo, if the results hold up, is the interesting way out of this — the benefit without the lie.

**"Placebo effects justify alternative medicine."** This is the credulous error in its final form, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a sneer, because the argument is not stupid.

It fails for four reasons. First, it smuggles in the Beecher error — the effect being claimed is usually the whole improvement in the treated group, not the increment over no treatment, and so it is much smaller than advertised. Second, placebo effects act on symptom experience, not disease, so the argument cannot justify offering these treatments for conditions that require actual treatment — and the harm there is not theoretical, it is people dying of treatable illness. Third, the costs are real: money, time, and the reinforcement of a belief system that will be applied again next time, to something more serious. And fourth — the strongest objection — **the ritual and the care can be delivered without the falsehood.** If the acupuncture study tells us the augmented consultation is doing the work, then the honest move is to deliver the consultation, not to preserve the needles as an excuse for it.

**"The placebo effect is medicine's own neglected asset."** This is the position I find most persuasive, and it is the constructive ending to this chapter.

Most of what we label "placebo effect" in clinical practice is the effect of attention, ritual, continuity, confidence and human contact. It is measurable — the augmented-consultation arm proves it. It is ethical. It requires no deception. It is available to every clinician for free.

And it is being systematically squeezed out of mainstream medicine by shortened appointments, fragmented care, and the substitution of the screen for the face.

So the sharpest version of the critique is not that alternative practitioners exploit the placebo effect. It is that they have a near-monopoly on delivering it, because they are the only ones still budgeting forty minutes and a warm room — and that this is an indictment of how we have organised medicine, not a vindication of their theories.

## 8.7 The Tool

### 8.7.1 The decomposition

Given any claim that a treatment produced improvement, break the improvement into its four components and ask which the study can distinguish.

| Component | Present in a placebo arm? | Present in a no-treatment arm? | Separated by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural history | Yes | Yes | Any control group |
| Regression to the mean | Yes | Yes | Any control group |
| Placebo effect | Yes | **No** | A no-treatment arm |
| Measurement artefact | Yes | Partly | Blinded, objective outcomes |
| Treatment effect | No | No | A placebo control |

Read down the last column. A standard two-arm placebo-controlled trial is designed to isolate the bottom row, and it does that job well. It does **not** measure the placebo effect, and it was never trying to.

### 8.7.2 The three questions to ask about any placebo claim

**1. Compared to what?** If someone quotes a number for "the placebo effect," ask whether the study had a no-treatment arm. If it didn't, the number includes natural history and regression to the mean, and is not a measurement of what they say it measures. This applies to most of what you will read.

**2. What kind of outcome is it?** Continuous and self-reported — pain, fatigue, nausea, mood — placebo effects are plausible and can be substantial. Binary and objective — survival, infection cleared, fracture healed — placebo effects are not a plausible explanation, and something else is going on.

**3. Is the ritual matched?** Not just the pill: the number of visits, the length of consultation, the warmth of the practitioner, the invasiveness, the branding, the cost. Any mismatch in ritual between arms is an uncontrolled difference, and it will show up as a treatment effect.

### 8.7.3 The ritual audit

For any intervention you are appraising, list the components of the encounter: the substance, the delivery route, the frequency, the practitioner contact, the setting, the expectation set by the consent process, the cost, the elaborateness.

Now ask: **which of these does the control group also receive?**

Anything on that list present in one arm and not the other is part of the "treatment" being tested, whether the authors think so or not. A trial comparing a drug plus monthly clinic visits against usual care with no visits is not a trial of the drug.

## 8.8 The Drill

### Drill 1 — Find the missing third arm *(30 minutes)*

Find three articles making a specific claim about the size of the placebo effect. For each, trace the claim back to its source and determine whether the underlying study had a no-treatment comparison group.

Most will not. Notice how confidently the number is stated anyway, and how far it has travelled from what the data can support.

### Drill 2 — Sort the claims *(20 minutes)*

Collect ten health claims from advertising, social media or a clinic's website. Sort them into two piles using question 2 above: outcomes where a placebo effect is a plausible explanation, and outcomes where it is not.

The second pile is where the serious problems are. A treatment claiming to reduce back pain has a plausible mechanism for its testimonials; a treatment claiming to shrink tumours does not, and requires a different explanation entirely.

### Drill 3 — Design the sham *(45 minutes)*

Pick an intervention that is not a pill — a physiotherapy technique, a device, a surgical procedure, a talking therapy, a diet.

Design a credible sham control. Specify exactly what the control group experiences. Then answer honestly: is it convincing? Would participants be able to tell? Is it ethical? Would an ethics committee approve it?

For most non-pharmacological interventions you will find this hard, and that difficulty is the reason those evidence bases are weaker. This drill teaches more about the structure of medical knowledge than any amount of reading about it.

### Drill 4 — Audit your own ritual *(15 minutes)*

Think of something you do that you believe helps you — a supplement, a routine, a technique. List every component of the ritual around it: the time of day, the preparation, the expense, the expectation, the fact that you chose it deliberately.

Then ask what an inert version, with the same ritual, would feel like. And whether you could tell the difference.

## 8.9 The Verdict

> **CHAPTER 8 SUMMARY CARD**
>
> **The claim:** "It's just a placebo."
>
> **Wrong in two directions.** Dismissive: the improvement is often real and physiologically mediated. Credulous: placebo effects are much smaller and much more bounded than popular accounts claim.
>
> **The distinction that matters most:** **The improvement in a placebo group is not the placebo effect.** It is the sum of natural history + regression to the mean + placebo effect + measurement artefact. To isolate the placebo effect you need a third arm — *no treatment* — and almost no trial has one.
>
> **Beecher's error (1955):** "The Powerful Placebo" attributed the entire placebo-arm improvement to placebo. The ~35% figure still quoted everywhere is that mistake.
>
> **What the proper comparison shows** (Hróbjartsson & Gøtzsche, 114 trials with both placebo and no-treatment arms, updated with 52 more): **no significant effect on binary outcomes, objective or subjective; a real effect on continuous subjective outcomes, especially pain.**
>
> **The sentence that resolves most arguments:** *Placebos change how people feel. They do not change the course of disease.*
>
> **The weirdness is real and systematic:** two pills beat one; injections beat pills; sham surgery beats injections; colour, branding and price all move the response; and the practitioner's warmth moves it most of all. The ritual is the dose.
>
> **It runs on real machinery:** naloxone blocks placebo analgesia (endogenous opioids); placebo in Parkinson's is associated with striatal dopamine release.
>
> **Open-label placebo:** honestly-labelled inert pills beat a no-treatment group receiving matched interaction (Kaptchuk, IBS, 2010). Deception may not be required. Young field; watch it.
>
> **Nocebo does more damage than placebo does good.** SAMSON: 60 patients, twelve randomised months of statin / placebo / *empty bottle* — around **90% of the symptom burden on the statin was also present on placebo**, and half the patients restarted treatment after seeing their own data.
>
> **The methodological payoff:** you cannot decompose improvement from inside the treated group — but you don't have to. **The control group absorbs natural history, regression to the mean and placebo response simultaneously, without you needing to quantify any of them.** That is why the fair test works even when you don't understand the disease.
>
> **A big placebo response does not weaken a trial** — you read the difference. It does raise the stakes on blinding.
>
> **The honest ethical position:** most of the clinical "placebo effect" is attention, ritual, continuity and care. It is free, ethical, and needs no deception — and mainstream medicine has largely stopped paying for it, which is why alternative practitioners have a near-monopoly on delivering it.
>
> **The sentence to carry:** *Something happened. It just wasn't the thing on the label.*
