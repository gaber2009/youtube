# Chapter 45
# The Missing Trials

> *A perfect trial, unpublished, is worse than no trial at all — because its absence distorts everything built on top of it.*

---

Part VII is about a claim that should be uncomfortable after six parts of learning to appraise studies:

> **Every skill in this book is insufficient, because the most important information is not in the papers you are reading.**

You can appraise a trial expertly. You can appraise a systematic review expertly. You cannot appraise a trial that was never published, and neither can anyone else. Chapter 43 showed the statistical consequence — the visible literature is a biased sample. This chapter is about the thing itself: the trials that were run, on real patients who consented to advance knowledge, whose results nobody outside the sponsor has ever seen.

This is not a historical grievance. It is the reason that, for a number of widely used treatments, nobody in the world knows how well they work.

---

## 45.1 The Story: the drug the world stockpiled

In the 2000s, facing the prospect of an influenza pandemic, governments around the world spent enormous sums stockpiling oseltamivir — Tamiflu. The UK, the US and many other countries bought it in bulk; the global spend ran into billions. It was added to the WHO list of essential medicines. The justification was not that it shortened a bout of flu by a few hours. It was that it would reduce **complications, hospitalisations and deaths** in a pandemic.

That belief rested largely on a published meta-analysis of the manufacturer's trials.

In 2009, when a Cochrane team was updating its review of neuraminidase inhibitors, a Japanese paediatrician named Keiji Hayashi posted a comment that unravelled the whole thing. He pointed out that the meta-analysis underpinning the complications claim drew mostly on trials that **had never been published.** The claim could not be checked, because the evidence for it was not public.

The Cochrane team asked the manufacturer for the full trial reports.

What followed took four years. There were partial offers, conditions, confidentiality agreements that would have prevented publication of the analysis, and long silences. The *BMJ* ran an open campaign. The episode became one of the founding cases for **AllTrials**, launched in 2013 to demand that all trials be registered and all results reported.

In 2013 the manufacturer released the full clinical study reports — around 150,000 pages of them. It is worth pausing on that number. Not a summary, not a paper: the underlying documentation of what was done and found, at a scale which shows how much detail a published journal article omits.

The 2014 Cochrane review, based on that material, concluded that oseltamivir **shortened symptoms in adults by around half a day** — from roughly 7 days to 6.3 — and that there was **no good evidence** it reduced hospitalisations or serious complications, alongside harms including nausea, vomiting and, in prophylactic use, psychiatric and renal events.

I should be fair: this conclusion is contested. Other researchers, drawing on observational data from severely ill patients, argue for real benefits in serious influenza, and the debate has not been settled to everyone's satisfaction. That dispute is legitimate.

**What is not in dispute is the process.** Governments made a multi-billion-pound decision on the basis of evidence that neither they nor any independent researcher could examine, and it took a four-year public campaign to obtain it. If the conclusion had turned out to vindicate the stockpiles entirely, that would still be true.

> **The scandal is not the answer. The scandal is that the question could not be asked.**

---

## 45.2 The scale of it

Tamiflu is the famous case because someone fought about it for four years. It is not exceptional.

Studies tracking cohorts of registered trials have repeatedly found that **a large proportion — historically around half — never publish their results**, and that the ones that vanish are disproportionately those with disappointing findings (Chapter 43).

The consequences compound in a specific way worth spelling out:

**The patients consented under a false premise.** People enter trials, accept risk and inconvenience, and are told that this will contribute to knowledge. If the results are never reported, that contribution does not happen. This is the strongest argument in the whole area and it is an ethical one, not a statistical one.

**The trial may be repeated.** If nobody knows a question was answered, someone else will randomise a new group of patients to find out — exposing them to a risk that has already been characterised.

**The distortion cannot be corrected downstream.** A systematic reviewer cannot include what they cannot obtain. A guideline committee inherits the reviewer's gap. A clinician inherits the guideline. Nothing in the chain can detect the absence.

---

## 45.3 The fixes, and how far they got

**Prospective registration.** From around 2005, the leading medical journals — through the ICMJE — refused to publish trials that had not been registered before enrolling their first patient. This was the single most effective intervention in the history of this problem, because it used the one lever researchers reliably respond to: access to publication.

It creates the **denominator**. After registration, you can ask "which registered trials have not reported?" and get a list with names, rather than an inference from a funnel plot.

**Mandatory results reporting.** Registration alone only tells you what is missing. So legislation followed. In the United States, the FDA Amendments Act of 2007 requires applicable trials to post summary results to ClinicalTrials.gov, generally within twelve months of completion. The European Union has an equivalent requirement for trials on its register.

**Auditing.** Here is the interesting part, and the reason this chapter is in a book about method. Once a legal requirement exists and a registry provides a denominator, **compliance becomes measurable** — and public trackers were built to measure it, sponsor by sponsor, trial by trial.

The results have been sobering. Substantial proportions of trials subject to these requirements have been late or missing, across universities and companies alike, for years after the laws took effect. Academic sponsors have often performed no better than industry, and sometimes worse — which is worth sitting with if your model of this problem is that companies hide things and universities do not.

But the trackers also demonstrated something genuinely encouraging: **when a named list is published and updated, compliance improves.** Not because anyone was fined — enforcement has been minimal — but because being visibly on a list of institutions that have not reported their trials is embarrassing. Transparency turns out to work as an enforcement mechanism when the alternative is nothing.

## 45.4 What is still missing

Three gaps remain, and it is worth being precise about them.

**Old trials.** Registration is prospective. Trials completed before the mandates, including many that established treatments still in wide use, are under no obligation and will mostly never be reported. **A substantial part of the evidence base for current practice is permanently unavailable**, and no reform can retrieve it.

**Summary results versus real data.** Posting a results summary to a registry is far better than nothing, and it is not the same as the clinical study report — the document that let the Cochrane team see what had actually been done in the Tamiflu trials. Publishing summary tables while withholding the underlying reports satisfies the letter of the requirement.

**Individual participant data.** Sharing anonymised patient-level data would allow independent reanalysis, IPD meta-analysis (Chapter 42) and error detection (Chapter 49). It is happening slowly, with genuine complications about privacy, consent and competitive interest, and it is nowhere near routine.

## 45.5 The Tool

**1. Check the registry before believing a literature.** For any treatment question, search the registry as well as the journals. Count completed trials with no results. That count is the size of the hole in the evidence you are reading.

**2. When appraising a systematic review, check whether it searched registries** and whether it lists registered trials it could not obtain (Chapter 43).

**3. For a specific trial, look for the registration**, its dates, and whether results were posted. A registered trial completed five years ago with no results is a fact you can state.

**4. Ask who holds the data.** For a regulatory decision, regulator documents — assessment reports and reviews — often contain far more than the published papers, and are increasingly public. They are underused.

**5. When you are the researcher: register, then report, regardless of the result.** Within twelve months. This is the one chapter of this book where the main action is not appraisal but conduct.

## 45.6 The Drill

### Drill 1 — Measure a hole *(60 minutes)*
Pick a treatment. Search a registry for completed trials. Try to find results for each — in the registry, a journal, or a regulatory document. Produce a count: registered, completed, reported. That fraction is your field's transparency, measured by you.

### Drill 2 — Read a regulatory document *(45 minutes)*
Find a regulator's assessment report for a drug you know. Compare what it contains with the published papers on the same trials. Note anything present in one and absent from the other.

### Drill 3 — Check an institution *(20 minutes)*
Look up a university or company you have a connection to on a public reporting tracker. Find their compliance rate. Consider what an email to their research office would say.

### Drill 4 — Follow a stockpile *(45 minutes)*
Find a public procurement decision — a vaccine, a drug, a device — and trace the evidence that justified it. Ask whether an independent researcher could have examined that evidence at the time.

## 45.7 The Verdict

> **CHAPTER 45 SUMMARY CARD**
>
> **The claim of Part VII:** every skill in this book is insufficient, because the most important information is not in the papers you are reading. **You cannot appraise a trial that was never published.**
>
> **The Story:** governments spent billions stockpiling oseltamivir on the belief it reduced complications and hospitalisations — a belief resting largely on **unpublished** trials. When a Cochrane team asked for the data, it took **four years** and a public campaign; ~150,000 pages of clinical study reports were eventually released. The 2014 review found symptoms shortened by about **half a day** and **no good evidence** of reduced hospitalisation or complications. That conclusion is contested. **The process is not.** The scandal is not the answer — it is that the question could not be asked.
>
> **The scale:** historically around half of registered trials never report, and the vanishing ones are disproportionately negative.
>
> **Why it is worse than a statistical problem:** patients consented on the understanding that their participation would contribute to knowledge, and it did not; trials get needlessly repeated on new patients; and **nothing downstream can detect the absence** — reviewer, guideline committee and clinician all inherit the gap.
>
> **What worked:** prospective registration, enforced by journals refusing to publish unregistered trials — which created the **denominator**. Then mandatory results reporting (FDAAA 2007 in the US, equivalent EU rules). Then **auditing**: once a denominator exists, compliance is measurable, and public trackers measured it, sponsor by sponsor.
>
> **What the audits showed:** compliance has been poor for years after the laws took effect, and **academic sponsors often perform no better than industry** — which should disturb anyone whose model is that companies hide things and universities don't. But also: **naming institutions on a public list improves compliance**, with essentially no enforcement. Transparency works as a sanction when nothing else is available.
>
> **What is still missing:** pre-registration-era trials, permanently — a large part of the evidence for current practice is unavailable and always will be; clinical study reports as opposed to summary tables; and individual patient data.
>
> **The sentence to carry:** *A registry does not make trials honest. It makes their absence countable — and that turns out to be enough to change behaviour.*
