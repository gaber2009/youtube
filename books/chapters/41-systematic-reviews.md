# Chapter 41
# Systematic Reviews

> *A single trial is not the answer. It is one observation of the answer.*

---

Everything in Part III taught you to appraise a study. Part V taught you to do it systematically. This part is about the uncomfortable fact that sits underneath both: **no single study, however good, should change your mind on its own.**

Chapter 20 explained the arithmetic reason. A trial's result is one draw from a distribution. Small trials that reach significance are inflated; large trials are expensive and rare; and any single result — even from an excellent trial — could be the one that came out at the tail. The remedy is not to find the best trial. It is to find **all** of them and look at what they say together.

That sounds like common sense. Doing it properly is a research method in its own right, with its own protocol, its own biases, and its own ways of going wrong. A systematic review is not "reading the literature." It is an experiment performed on the literature, and it can be rigged exactly as a trial can.

---

## 41.1 The Story: two reviews, one literature, opposite conclusions

Here is a pattern documented across many fields — nutrition, tobacco, pharmaceuticals, environmental exposures.

Two review articles address the same question. They are published within a couple of years of each other. They draw on the same body of primary research, which neither has done anything to change. One concludes the evidence shows harm; the other concludes the evidence is inconclusive and more research is needed.

Both are honestly written by qualified people. Neither has fabricated anything. And the difference in conclusion tracks, with depressing reliability, who funded the review.

How? Not by lying about the studies. By **choosing which studies to include.**

Every review makes dozens of decisions: which databases to search, which search terms, which years, which languages, what study designs qualify, what populations count, what outcomes are relevant, what counts as too poor in quality to include. Each decision is defensible. Made *after* seeing what the studies say, each decision is also an opportunity — and the accumulation of a dozen small, individually justifiable choices can move a conclusion from one end of the spectrum to the other.

This is Chapter 31's garden of forking paths, applied one level up. Not multiplicity in the analysis of a trial, but multiplicity in the **selection of trials**.

And this tells you exactly what a systematic review is for:

> **A systematic review is a method for making the selection decisions before you know what difference they will make.**

That is the entire innovation. Not thoroughness — a narrative review can be thorough. **Pre-commitment.** Everything else in this chapter is machinery for enforcing it.

## 41.2 What makes a review systematic

The distinction is not a matter of degree. A "narrative review" and a systematic review are different objects.

A **narrative review** is an expert's account of a field. It can be brilliant, and it is essentially testimony (Chapter 1) — with the expert's judgement doing the selecting, invisibly, and with the same asymmetric scrutiny that Chapter 11 described. If the author has a position, the review will support it, and the author will not experience themselves as having done anything improper.

A **systematic review** commits, in a protocol written in advance, to:

**1. A precise question.** In PICO terms (Chapter 15). "Does exercise help depression?" is not a review question. "In adults with a diagnosis of major depression, does supervised aerobic exercise, compared with usual care or waiting list, reduce depressive symptoms at 12 weeks or later?" is.

**2. A registered protocol.** Published before the review begins — PROSPERO is the main register. This is the review-level equivalent of trial registration (Chapter 46), and it exists for exactly the same reason.

**3. An explicit, reproducible search.** Multiple databases, not just one. The full search string reported, so someone else could rerun it. Crucially, the search should extend beyond journal databases to **trial registries** and regulatory documents, because that is the only route to the studies that were never published (Chapter 43).

**4. Pre-specified eligibility criteria**, applied by two people independently, with disagreements resolved by a third.

**5. Duplicate data extraction.** Two people pull the numbers separately. This is not bureaucratic caution — extraction error rates in single-extractor reviews are high enough to change conclusions.

**6. Risk-of-bias assessment for every included study**, using the appropriate tool from Chapter 36. A review that pools good and bad trials without distinguishing them has laundered the bad ones.

**7. A pre-specified synthesis plan**: whether to pool, how, and what subgroup analyses were planned in advance.

**8. Transparent reporting**, per PRISMA, including a flow diagram showing how many records were found, screened, excluded and why.

Notice that items 2, 4, 5, 7 and 8 are all doing one job: **removing the reviewer's discretion from the point where knowing the results could influence it.** That is the same design principle as blinding (Chapter 9), allocation concealment (Chapter 17) and pre-registration (Chapter 53). The whole book keeps arriving at the same idea.

## 41.3 Where systematic reviews go wrong

Being labelled "systematic" is not a guarantee. The failures are specific and findable.

**The search was too narrow.** One database, English only, published studies only, recent years only. Each restriction removes studies non-randomly. Non-English exclusion is a genuine problem in some fields; restricting to published studies guarantees publication bias.

**The included studies are rubbish and the review says so quietly.** A review can conclude "the evidence supports X" on the basis of eleven small, unblinded, unregistered trials. The risk-of-bias table says so, in a supplementary figure. The abstract does not. **Garbage in, garbage out — a systematic review cannot manufacture reliability that the primary studies lack.** It can only stop you being misled about what is there.

**Pooling things that should not be pooled.** Chapter 42.

**Vote counting.** "Seven studies found a benefit and four did not, so the evidence is mixed." This is an invalid method that ignores study size and precision entirely. Four large null trials outweigh seven tiny positive ones. Counting studies is not synthesis.

**The protocol was written afterwards, or deviated from silently.** Compare the PROSPERO entry with the published review, exactly as Chapter 46 compares a trial's registry entry with its paper. The same discrepancies appear.

**Redundancy and industrial production.** There are now more systematic reviews published than anyone can use, many covering the same questions, some produced with minimal effort and increasingly with automated assistance. Quantity is not the same as coverage, and a field with fifteen overlapping reviews of the same question is not better served than one with a single good one that is kept current.

## 41.4 The Tool: appraising a review

Run these questions in order.

**1. Is there a registered protocol, and does the review match it?** Check PROSPERO.

**2. Is the question precise enough that inclusion decisions are mechanical?**

**3. Was the search adequate?** Multiple databases, search terms reported, registries and grey literature searched, no arbitrary language or date limits.

**4. Was screening and extraction done in duplicate?**

**5. Was risk of bias assessed per study, and does it affect the conclusion?** A review that assesses risk of bias and then ignores it in the synthesis has performed a ritual.

**6. Is the synthesis appropriate?** Chapter 42. If they pooled, should they have? If they counted votes, disregard the conclusion.

**7. Do the authors' conclusions follow from their own results table?** Read the table, form your own conclusion, then read theirs (Chapter 38).

**8. Who funded it, and who wrote it?** Chapter 47 applies here as much as to trials.

The formal instrument for this is **AMSTAR 2**, which is worth knowing about, but the eight questions above will get you most of the way.

## 41.5 The Drill

### Drill 1 — Check a review against its protocol *(30 minutes)*
Find a recent systematic review that cites a PROSPERO registration. Retrieve the registration. Compare the question, eligibility criteria, primary outcome and planned analyses against the published review. Note every difference and whether it was disclosed.

### Drill 2 — Rerun a search *(45 minutes)*
Take a review's reported search string and run it yourself in one of the databases they used. Do you get approximately the number of records their flow diagram reports? If you can't reproduce it, the search was not adequately reported — which is itself a finding.

### Drill 3 — Find the vote count *(20 minutes)*
Find a review that concludes "the evidence is mixed" or "studies have shown conflicting results." Check whether the authors weighted by study size, or simply counted studies. Then look at whether the large studies agree with each other — they often do, and the "conflict" is entirely among the small ones (Chapter 20).

### Drill 4 — Write a protocol *(60 minutes)*
Take a question you care about and write a full review protocol: question in PICO, eligibility criteria, databases, search terms, screening process, risk-of-bias tool, synthesis plan. Do not look at any results first. Notice how many decisions you have to make blind, and how tempting it is to peek.

## 41.6 The Verdict

> **CHAPTER 41 SUMMARY CARD**
>
> **The principle:** no single study should change your mind alone. The unit of evidence is the totality of studies.
>
> **What makes a review systematic is not thoroughness — it is pre-commitment.** A systematic review is a method for making the selection decisions *before you know what difference they will make*. That is the entire innovation.
>
> **The failure it prevents:** the same literature, reviewed by different people, yielding opposite conclusions through a dozen individually defensible choices about what to include. Chapter 31's forking paths, one level up — multiplicity in the selection of trials rather than in the analysis of one.
>
> **The eight components:** precise PICO question; registered protocol (PROSPERO); explicit reproducible search including **trial registries**, not just journals; pre-specified eligibility applied in duplicate; duplicate data extraction; risk-of-bias assessment per study; pre-specified synthesis plan; PRISMA reporting with a flow diagram.
>
> **Notice what most of those do:** remove the reviewer's discretion from the point where knowing the results could influence it — the same design principle as blinding, concealment and pre-registration.
>
> **A narrative review is testimony** (Chapter 1) with an expert doing the selecting invisibly. It can be brilliant. It cannot be checked.
>
> **How reviews fail:** searches too narrow; garbage inputs laundered by a systematic wrapper; inappropriate pooling; **vote counting**, which ignores size and precision entirely; protocols written afterwards or silently deviated from; and industrial redundancy.
>
> **The sentence to carry:** *A systematic review cannot manufacture reliability the primary studies lack. It can only stop you being misled about what is there.*
