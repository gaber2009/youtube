# Chapter 1
# The Testimony Problem

> *"But where are the pictures of those who prayed and then drowned?"*
> — attributed to Diagoras of Melos, c. 5th century BC

---

I want to begin with the strongest evidence there is.

Not a trial. Not a paper. Not a statistic. Something far more powerful than any of those: **someone you trust, looking you in the eye, telling you what happened to them.**

You know how this goes. Your aunt had migraines for eleven years, tried everything, and then someone gave her a supplement and within a fortnight they stopped. A colleague's back pain vanished after a chiropractor. A friend swears by the thing she takes at the first sign of a cold — she's tracked it, she says, and it works every time. Your grandmother lived to ninety-four and had a glass of whisky every night, so.

These stories are the water we swim in. They are how humans have transmitted medical knowledge for almost all of history, and they carry a force that no table of numbers can match. When a friend tells you what happened to their own body, disbelieving them feels close to calling them a liar.

This chapter is going to argue that this — the single most persuasive form of information in human experience — is very nearly worthless for answering the question *does this treatment work?*

And I think you already suspect this. Because you do not believe the testimonials for treatments you consider nonsense. You have read the five-star reviews for the crystal pendant and the detox foot pad and the psychic, and you did not update your beliefs at all. You already apply a heavy discount to testimony. You just apply it *selectively* — steeply to claims you doubt, and hardly at all to claims you find plausible, or to claims from people you love, or to things that happened to you.

The work of this chapter is to make you apply that discount evenly. Which is much harder than it sounds, and much more uncomfortable.

## 1.1 The Story: two thousand years of eyewitnesses

Let me show you the largest experiment ever conducted on the reliability of personal experience.

For roughly two thousand years, the central therapeutic act of Western medicine was cutting a vein and letting blood run out of the patient. Not a fringe practice. Not a folk remedy. **The** treatment — for fever, for pneumonia, for headache, for melancholy, for inflammation, for apoplexy, for almost anything that could be named. It ran from Hippocrates and Galen through the Islamic Golden Age, through medieval Europe, through the Renaissance, and deep into the nineteenth century. Barber-surgeons advertised it with a red-and-white striped pole — white for the bandage, red for the blood. When Thomas Wakley founded a crusading new medical journal in 1823, he called it *The Lancet*, after the blade used to open a vein.

Think about the scale for a moment, because the scale is the entire point.

Hundreds of millions of patients. Millions of practitioners. Across dozens of cultures and every social class, over roughly eighty generations. And every single one of those practitioners had what you have when you tell me the supplement fixed your migraines: **direct personal experience that it worked.**

They were not fools, and they were not lying. They watched it work. A patient burning with fever would be bled, and the fever would break. An agitated, thrashing patient would be bled, and grow calm. A patient with a pounding head would be bled, and the pounding would ease. Physicians recorded these observations carefully, over careers spanning decades. They wrote them up. They taught them. They refined the practice — how much to take, from which vein, at which phase of the moon — on the basis of accumulated clinical experience, which is another way of saying: on the basis of a very large number of anecdotes.

And in December 1799, this two-thousand-year edifice of clinical experience was brought to the bedside of the most important man in America.

George Washington, sixty-seven, had spent a day riding around his estate in snow and freezing rain. He woke in the night barely able to breathe or speak — most likely acute epiglottitis, a bacterial infection that swells the airway shut. Three physicians attended him, all experienced, all competent by the standards of their day, all acting from genuine care.

They bled him. Then they bled him again. Over roughly twelve hours he was bled four times. Estimates of the total vary — the figures usually given run to about five pints, something in the region of forty per cent or more of his total blood volume, removed from a sixty-seven-year-old man already fighting for air. He was also blistered, purged, and given an emetic.

He died that night. His last recorded concern was that he not be buried too soon.

Now, here is the question I want you to sit with, because it is the question the whole book is built on.

**What would the doctors have seen, if bloodletting were completely useless?**

Take a moment. Genuinely try to answer it before reading on.

They would have seen exactly what they did see. Patients who were going to recover anyway would recover after being bled, and the physician would record a success. Patients who were going to die would die, and the physician would record that the disease was too far advanced, or that they had been called too late, or that the patient's constitution was too weak — all of which are sincere, reasonable, humane explanations that any of us would reach for. The practice generates a steady stream of confirming experiences whether or not it works, and it generates comforting explanations for the failures.

That is the structure of the trap, and I want to name it now because you will meet it in every chapter of this book:

> **A practice that does nothing produces the same testimony as a practice that works.**

If the evidence would look identical either way, then the evidence cannot tell the two apart. And something that cannot tell two possibilities apart is not evidence about them. It is noise that feels like signal.

### The man who counted

The thing that eventually killed bloodletting was not a better theory. It was arithmetic.

In Paris in the 1820s and 30s, a physician named Pierre Charles Alexandre Louis did something that sounds trivial and was in fact revolutionary. He wrote things down in a systematic way, and then he counted them. He called it the *méthode numérique* — the numerical method.

Louis took patients with pneumonia at the Hôpital de la Pitié and La Charité, and grouped them by how they had been treated: how many times they were bled, and crucially, **how early** in the illness the bleeding began. Then he counted who lived and who died.

The result, published in his *Recherches sur les effets de la saignée* in 1835, was that patients bled early in the course of their illness died at a higher rate than those bled later. He was cautious in his conclusions — he wrote of "narrow limits to the utility" of bloodletting rather than declaring it lethal — but the direction was clear, and it was the opposite of what every experienced physician in Europe knew from their own practice.

The reaction is the part worth learning. Louis was attacked, sometimes furiously, and the objection was not that his counting was wrong. The objection was that counting was the wrong thing to do. Medicine was an art. Each patient was an individual. To lump patients into groups and tally them was to abandon clinical judgement, to treat the sick as interchangeable units, to substitute the arithmetic of the ledger for the wisdom of the bedside. One critic complained that the numerical method would reduce the physician to a mere bookkeeper.

You will hear this objection for the rest of your life, in every field, usually from intelligent and well-meaning people. *You can't reduce this to numbers. Every case is different. Statistics don't capture what I see every day.*

There is something real in it — Chapter 40 is entirely about the fact that a trial result is not automatically true of the person in front of you, and Chapter 25 is about the kinds of knowledge that only qualitative work can reach. But as a defence of a practice against measurement, it is exactly backwards, and the two-thousand-year career of bloodletting is what it costs.

Here is the summary, and it should be uncomfortable:

**If personal clinical experience were a reliable guide to whether treatments work, bloodletting would be the best-supported therapy in the history of medicine.** It had more eyewitness testimony behind it than any intervention has ever had or will ever have again. It had the considered clinical judgement of the most educated people in every society that practised it. And it was, for most of the conditions it was used for, useless at best and often lethal.

The testimony was not weak. The testimony was overwhelming. That is what should frighten you.

## 1.2 The Trap: why testimony feels like the strongest evidence

Let's take the mechanism apart. "People are bad at anecdotes" is not an explanation; it's a slogan. There is a specific structure here, made of about six separate failures that happen to point the same way.

### 1.2.1 Nobody in this story is lying

Start here, because everything else depends on it.

The aunt with the migraines is not lying. She really did have migraines for eleven years. She really did take the supplement. The migraines really did stop. Every element of her account is true, and if you dispute any of it you will be wrong and she will be rightly annoyed.

The problem is not in her honesty, her memory, or her intelligence. **The problem is in the structure of what she is able to observe.** She has access to precisely one person's outcome, once, under one condition, with no comparison — and no amount of sincerity can turn that into knowledge about whether the supplement works.

This matters practically, too. If you go through life treating anecdote-tellers as liars or idiots, you will be an insufferable person and you will also be wrong. The correct stance is: *I believe every word you have told me, and it still doesn't tell us whether it works.* Learning to hold those two thoughts at once is one of the genuine skills this book is trying to teach.

### 1.2.2 One cell of a four-cell table

Here is the single most useful image in this chapter. Keep it.

To know whether a treatment works, you need to know four things:

|  | **Got better** | **Didn't get better** |
|---|---|---|
| **Took the treatment** | ✓ *the anecdote* | ? |
| **Didn't take it** | ? | ? |

An anecdote fills in exactly one cell. One. The top-left.

And notice: the top-left cell is guaranteed to have people in it no matter what. Some people take the treatment and get better *even if the treatment does nothing*, because people get better. So a full top-left cell is compatible with every possible truth about the treatment, which means it discriminates between none of them.

Everything else in this book — randomisation, control groups, blinding, sample size, confidence intervals, meta-analysis, all of it — is machinery for filling in the other three cells. That is genuinely all a clinical trial is. It is a device for finding out what happens in the cells nobody tells you stories about.

Once you see this, you cannot unsee it, and you will start noticing that almost every claim you encounter in daily life is a single cell presented as if it were a table.

### 1.2.3 The silent cell: nobody writes a testimonial from the grave

The bottom-left and bottom-right cells are hard to fill because they're expensive to measure. The top-right cell — *took the treatment, didn't get better* — is hard to fill for a more interesting reason: **that cell actively hides itself.**

The ancient version of this observation is the best. Cicero relates that Diagoras of Melos was taken to a temple and shown the votive tablets left by sailors who had prayed to the gods during a storm and survived. Impressive, isn't it? And Diagoras asked: *but where are the pictures of those who prayed and then drowned?*

They are at the bottom of the sea. They do not paint tablets. This is the whole problem in one line, twenty-five centuries old.

The modern versions are everywhere once you look:

- The cancer clinic advertising its remarkable survivors. The patients who died are not in the brochure, and their families are not writing reviews.
- Product reviews, which are written overwhelmingly by the delighted and the furious, and almost never by the vast middle who felt nothing much.
- The alternative practitioner's waiting room, full of people for whom it seemed to work — because the ones for whom it didn't stopped coming, months ago, and are now somewhere else entirely.
- The trader, the entrepreneur, the influencer with the method. The people who followed the same method and failed are not giving talks about it.

And note that the disappearance is not usually anyone's fault. Nobody is suppressing the failures. The failures simply have no reason to speak, no platform, and often no continued contact with the person collecting the stories. **Absence of complaint is not evidence of success; it is evidence of absence.**

This has a formal name — *survivorship bias* — and a formal descendant that will reappear in Chapter 43, when we find that the same thing happens to entire clinical trials. Trials with disappointing results are quietly not published, exactly as unhappy patients quietly do not return. The mechanism is identical at both scales, which is why this chapter has to come first.

### 1.2.4 You didn't take the pill at a random moment

This one is subtle and it is my favourite, because almost nobody notices it and it does an enormous amount of the work.

Think about *when* you actually take a remedy. Not on a random Tuesday. You take it when the pain is at its worst. When the cold is at its most miserable. When the migraine has gone on for four days and you are desperate. When you have finally had enough.

You are, in other words, **systematically choosing to intervene at the extreme.**

And here is the thing about extremes: they are, by definition, unusual. Whatever is going on has to be unusually bad for you to reach that point — an unlucky confluence of a bad night's sleep, a stressful week, the illness at its natural peak. Unusual combinations don't persist. The next few days are, on average, going to be closer to your normal, whatever you do.

So the sequence is: things get as bad as they ever get → you take the remedy → things get better. And your brain, which is a superb pattern-detector and a poor experimental scientist, records: *the remedy worked*.

This is called **regression to the mean**, and it is so important that Chapter 2 is largely about it. But I want you to meet it here, in Chapter 1, in its most personal form, because I want you to understand something about your own life: the very act of choosing to treat guarantees that the treatment will appear to work. The selection is built into the moment of decision. You cannot escape it by being careful or honest, because it happens before you do anything at all.

Every therapy in history, effective and useless alike, has enjoyed this free tailwind.

### 1.2.5 Memory is not a recording

We talk about "remembering what happened" as if memory were a video file we replay. It isn't. Memory is reconstructive: each time you recall an event, you rebuild it, and you rebuild it in a way that is shaped by what you now believe, what you've said out loud since, and how the story goes.

So when your aunt recalls her migraines stopping "within a fortnight," that number was not stored at the time. It was assembled later, in the light of a conclusion she had already reached, in a narrative she has now told a dozen times, each telling smoothing and sharpening it a little further. If the improvement actually took four months and had two relapses, the story would still, quite honestly, come out as a fortnight.

And the same reconstruction runs in the other direction for the failures. The three previous supplements that didn't work are not weighed and recorded as disconfirmations; they simply fade, because a thing that didn't happen leaves no landmark in memory. Nobody keeps a mental tally of the remedies that did nothing. The hits are vivid and dated; the misses are a fog.

None of this is a defect of your aunt. It's a defect of a memory system built for getting through the world, not for auditing hypotheses.

### 1.2.6 A thousand anecdotes is still one anecdote

Here is where people's intuition most reliably fails, and where the most damage is done in public argument.

Surely, you think, one story is weak — but a *thousand* stories? Ten thousand? A whole internet forum of people saying the same thing? At some point volume must become evidence.

It doesn't. And the reason is simple once stated:

> **If every story was collected by the same biased process, then collecting more of them multiplies the stories without reducing the bias.**

A thousand testimonials gathered from people who chose to come forward, who chose to try the treatment at their worst moment, who reconstructed the timeline afterwards, and who are being counted only if they improved — that is not a thousand independent observations. It is *one* observation with a thousand copies, and the copies are not evidence, they are just louder.

Worse: the number itself is a product of popularity, not effect. A treatment used by ten million people will generate a hundred thousand glowing testimonials while doing precisely nothing, and a superb treatment used by five hundred people will generate a handful. **Testimonial count measures reach, not efficacy.** Bloodletting had, over its career, more supporting testimony than anything else in medical history, and the size of that pile is exactly proportional to how long and how widely it was used, and not at all to whether it worked.

This is why "millions of people can't be wrong" is not an argument. Millions of people can be wrong, in an identical way, for two thousand years, and the fact that they are all wrong in the same way is precisely what makes it so convincing.

### 1.2.7 What evidence actually is

Let me now give you the definition that underwrites the whole book. This is the most valuable paragraph in this chapter, and it's worth memorising.

> **Something is evidence for a claim only if you would have been less likely to see it had the claim been false.**

That's it. That's the whole concept. Evidence is *discrimination between possibilities*. If an observation is equally likely whether or not the claim is true, it carries no information about the claim, however dramatic or emotionally powerful it is.

Now apply it to testimonials. Ask honestly: if this treatment did nothing whatsoever, would there still be a large supply of sincere, moving accounts of it working?

Yes. Obviously and abundantly yes. We know this empirically, not just theoretically, because we can look at treatments we are extremely confident do nothing — and find testimony for them that is indistinguishable in volume, sincerity, detail and emotional force from the testimony for treatments that genuinely work. The testimonials for homeopathy are not weaker or vaguer or less heartfelt than the testimonials for antibiotics. If you stripped the labels off, you could not sort them.

That is a devastating fact, and it is the empirical proof of the theoretical point. **Testimony's discriminating power is close to zero, because its form does not change with the truth of the claim.**

You will spend the rest of this book learning increasingly sophisticated methods, and every one of them is a way of engineering an observation that *would* have come out differently if the claim were false. That's what a control group is for. That's what blinding is for. That's what randomisation is for. Keep this definition and you will be able to derive most of the methodology yourself.

### 1.2.8 Why the true thing loses to the vivid thing

The last piece is psychological, and you need to know it about yourself.

A single vivid, specific, human story reliably beats a large, careful, correct number — in your intuitions, in a jury's, in a newspaper editor's, and in a policymaker's. One named child with one named illness will move more money than a robust estimate of ten thousand anonymous deaths. Psychologists call the related effects the identifiable victim effect and base-rate neglect. Novelists have known it forever.

This is not a bug you can debug. It is the standard configuration of the human mind, and the trained epidemiologist reading a moving case report feels it too. What training gives you is not immunity — it's the ability to notice the feeling and decline to act on it.

Which is why the honest version of this chapter's conclusion is not "anecdotes are unpersuasive." They are enormously persuasive, permanently, including to you. The conclusion is that **their persuasiveness is uncorrelated with their truth**, and you will have to hold that line consciously, every time, for the rest of your life. It does not become automatic. I have been doing this for years and my aunt's migraine story still lands.

## 1.3 What testimony is genuinely for

Now the correction, because a rule you apply without limits becomes a different kind of stupidity. Testimony is bad at one specific job. It is excellent at several others, and a scientist who sneers at case reports is missing real information.

**It generates hypotheses — and this is not a small thing.** Almost everything worth testing was noticed by somebody first. The clinician who thinks "that's odd, that's the third patient this month" is doing indispensable work. Testimony is where the questions come from. It is simply not where the answers come from.

**It detects rare and dramatic harms, which trials are terrible at.** This is testimony's genuine superpower, and it deserves emphasis. A trial of 3,000 people will not reliably detect a side effect that occurs in one patient in 20,000 — but an alert clinician noticing two impossible cases might. In 1961, an Australian obstetrician, William McBride, wrote a short letter to *The Lancet* about a cluster of severe limb malformations in babies; in Germany, Widukind Lenz was independently reaching the same conclusion. That is how thalidomide was caught: not by a trial, but by people noticing something that should not have been happening and saying so out loud. The entire field of pharmacovigilance is built on structured, systematic anecdote. Chapter 25 covers how to do it properly.

Note the asymmetry, because it's precise and it's not arbitrary. Testimony is nearly useless for detecting **benefit** and quite good at detecting **rare catastrophic harm**. Why? Go back to the definition of evidence. Improvements happen constantly for a hundred boring reasons, so an improvement after treatment is unsurprising whether or not the drug works — no discrimination, no information. But phocomelia in a newborn was so rare that a cluster of cases is *wildly* unlikely if the drug is harmless. The observation would have come out differently had the claim been false. That's why it counts. Same principle, opposite verdict.

**It tells you what "working" should even mean.** A trial can only measure the outcomes someone chose to measure. Which outcomes matter — pain, function, independence, dignity, being able to walk to the shops, not being nauseated at your daughter's wedding — is not a question numbers can answer. It comes from listening to people. Chapter 19 is about choosing outcomes, and the honest answer to "which outcome?" always begins with testimony. Trials tell you whether something works. Patients tell you what would count as working.

**It is legitimately evidence about you, once.** Your experience really is data about your own body — it's just data with all the problems described above. And there is a formal way to fix that: the **n-of-1 trial**, where a single patient cycles through treatment and placebo in randomised, blinded order, with outcomes recorded prospectively rather than remembered. It is testimony with the biases engineered out, and it works. Chapter 23 shows you how to run one on yourself.

So the summary, which deliberately rhymes with a rule you'll meet again in Chapter 6:

> **Testimony is a detector, not a verdict.** It is superb at telling you where to look. It is close to useless at telling you what you'll find.

## 1.4 The Tool

### 1.4.1 The four missing questions

Whenever you meet an anecdote — from a stranger, a friend, a doctor, a headline, or your own memory — ask these four. They take fifteen seconds and they do most of the work of this entire book.

**1. Compared to what?**
What would have happened to this person if they had done nothing? If you cannot answer, you have no idea whether the treatment did anything. This is the missing-cells problem, and it is the deep one. *(Chapter 3)*

**2. How many others?**
This person is a numerator. Where is the denominator? How many people took this treatment in total, and what happened to them? One success out of five is a promising signal; one success out of five thousand is a coincidence, and both are described by the identical sentence "it worked for my friend."

**3. Who selected this story, and how?**
Why am I hearing this particular account? Who is telling it, who is not here to tell theirs, and what process filtered the ones that reached me? Follow the filter backwards and you will usually find the silent cell.

**4. What else changed?**
Almost nobody changes one thing. She started the supplement — and also it was spring, and she'd changed jobs, and she was sleeping better, and she'd started three other things at once, and she took it in the week her symptoms peaked. Which of those did it? *(Chapter 2)*

If a claim survives all four, it is worth investigating properly. Almost none do, and noticing that quickly is a genuine practical skill.

### 1.4.2 The Testimony Ledger

The formal version, for when you want to be rigorous rather than fast. Take any anecdotal claim and draw the table:

|  | Got better | Didn't get better |
|---|---|---|
| **Took the treatment** | **A** | **B** |
| **Didn't take it** | **C** | **D** |

Then fill in what you actually know, honestly, marking unknowns as unknown.

- The anecdote gives you a single case in **A**.
- **B** is the people it failed. Ask specifically: *how would I ever hear from them?* If the honest answer is "I wouldn't," that tells you the collection process is broken, not that B is empty.
- **C** is people who recovered without treatment. This is the number that destroys most claims, and it's often knowable: for most self-limiting conditions, C is large. Colds resolve. Back pain resolves. Most acute misery resolves.
- **D** is people who didn't take it and didn't improve.

You cannot fill this table from anecdotes. That is not a failure of your diligence — **it is the definition of the problem**, and the reason clinical trials exist. But drawing the empty table converts a vague unease into a precise list of what you don't know, and that is the beginning of thinking like a scientist rather than merely feeling sceptical.

Later in the book these four cells will acquire names and arithmetic: risk in each group, risk ratio, risk difference, number needed to treat (Chapters 10 and 29). It is worth knowing that every one of those numbers is just a way of comparing A, B, C and D. The whole edifice sits on this table.

## 1.5 The Drill

### Drill 1 — Fill the ledger *(20 minutes)*

Find a testimonial for any health product — an advert, a review page, a social media post, a documentary. Draw the four-cell table. Fill in what the testimonial gives you. Then, for each empty cell, write one sentence on how you would find that number if you had unlimited resources.

The exercise is not about the answer. It is about feeling the size of the gap between what you were shown and what you would need to know.

### Drill 2 — Hunt the silent cell *(30 minutes)*

Pick a treatment or product with an enthusiastic online community. Now deliberately go looking for the people it failed. Search for accounts of it not working. Look for people who tried it and stopped.

Notice three things: how much harder this is than finding the successes; how the failures tend to explain their failure in ways that protect the treatment ("I didn't do it properly", "I didn't stick with it", "my case was too advanced"); and how the community itself often supplies those explanations. That last mechanism is worth remembering — Chapter 11 comes back to it.

### Drill 3 — Turn it on yourself *(20 minutes, and this is the important one)*

Write down one thing **you personally believe** on the basis of your own experience. Something about your body, your diet, your sleep, your productivity, your mood. Something you would defend.

Now run the four questions on it. Honestly.

- Compared to what? Did you ever try *not* doing it, under comparable conditions?
- How many others? How many times did you do it and *nothing* happened — and did you count those, or forget them?
- Who selected the story? Are you remembering the times it worked because they confirmed what you'd started to think?
- What else changed? What else was going on in the weeks you noticed the effect?

Most people find this exercise genuinely unpleasant, and that discomfort is the actual content of the drill. Applying this to other people's beliefs is entertainment. Applying it to your own is the skill.

### Drill 4 — The label test *(15 minutes)*

Collect five testimonials for a treatment with good trial evidence, and five for something you're confident is inert. Strip the product names out. Now try to sort them.

You will not be able to. That failure *is* the result: it is direct empirical proof that testimony carries almost no information about whether a treatment works.

## 1.6 The Verdict

> **CHAPTER 1 SUMMARY CARD**
>
> **The claim:** "It worked for me." / "I've seen it work with my own eyes."
>
> **What's true about it:** Everything. The person is not lying, misremembering the big picture, or stupid. They took the treatment and they got better.
>
> **The trap:** An anecdote fills **one cell of a four-cell table** — took it, got better. That cell is full no matter what the truth is, because people get better. Six mechanisms guarantee this: no comparison; a hidden denominator; the failures don't speak (survivorship); you intervene at your worst moment, so improvement follows automatically (regression to the mean); memory reconstructs the timeline to fit the conclusion; and volume multiplies the stories without reducing the bias.
>
> **Why volume doesn't help:** A thousand testimonials collected by the same broken process is one observation with a thousand copies. Testimonial count measures *reach*, not *efficacy*.
>
> **The historical proof:** Bloodletting. Two thousand years, hundreds of millions of patients, universal clinical experience that it worked, and it was useless or lethal. If personal experience were reliable, it would be the best-evidenced treatment ever.
>
> **The definition to keep:** *Something is evidence only if you'd have been less likely to see it had the claim been false.* Glowing testimony appears whether or not a treatment works — so it discriminates nothing, so it isn't evidence about efficacy.
>
> **What testimony is genuinely for:** generating hypotheses; detecting rare catastrophic harms (thalidomide); telling you which outcomes matter; and, done formally as an n-of-1 trial, telling you about yourself.
>
> **The asymmetry, and why it isn't arbitrary:** useless for detecting benefit (improvement is unsurprising either way), good for detecting rare harm (a cluster of impossible events is very surprising if the drug is safe). Same definition of evidence, opposite verdict.
>
> **The tool:** The four questions — *Compared to what? How many others? Who selected this story? What else changed?* — and the Testimony Ledger (cells A, B, C, D).
>
> **The honest warning:** Anecdotes will not stop being persuasive to you. Training doesn't grant immunity; it grants the ability to notice the pull and decline it.
>
> **The sentence to carry:** *Testimony is a detector, not a verdict.*

## Where this goes next

You now know that "it worked for me" cannot establish that a treatment works, and you know the shape of what's missing: three empty cells.

The obvious next question is *why* people get better anyway — because if you don't understand that, the empty cells stay abstract. **Chapter 2** answers it, and identifies the three specific processes that manufacture the illusion of cure: the natural history of disease, regression to the mean, and the placebo response. Those three are the reason the top-left cell is always full, and they are the engine underneath every story in this chapter.

Then **Chapter 3** builds the fix — the fair test — and from there the rest of the book is just increasingly careful ways of filling in cells B, C and D.
