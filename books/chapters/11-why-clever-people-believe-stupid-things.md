# Chapter 11
# Why Clever People Believe Stupid Things

> *Reasoning did not evolve to find the truth. It evolved to win arguments.*
> *This is why being good at it is not the protection you were promised.*

---

There is a comfortable theory about why people believe wrong things, and almost everyone holds it. It goes: people believe nonsense because they lack information, or lack education, or lack the ability to reason carefully. Give them the facts, teach them some statistics, and the problem dissolves.

Call it the deficit model. It is the implicit theory behind most science communication, most public health campaigns, and most arguments you have ever had at a dinner table.

It is wrong, and the way it is wrong is worse than a simple failure. On the questions that matter most, more education and more reasoning ability do not reliably move people toward the truth. **Sometimes they move people further away — and the effect is strongest in the most capable.**

That is not a rhetorical flourish. It has been measured, deliberately, in an experiment designed to test exactly this question, and the result is the subject of this chapter.

I want to be careful about what follows, because this material is dangerous in a specific way. A chapter about cognitive bias is very easy to read as a chapter about *other people's* cognitive bias — a catalogue of the ways your opponents are stupid. Read that way it makes you worse: better armed, more confident, and still wrong.

So the test of whether you have understood this chapter is simple. If you finish it thinking mainly about people who disagree with you, you have failed. If you finish it slightly less sure of something you currently believe, it worked.

---

## 11.1 The Story: the maths problem that got harder when it got political

In 2013, Dan Kahan and colleagues ran an experiment with a very sharp design.

They gave participants a genuinely difficult problem in quantitative reasoning: a two-by-two table of data from which you have to work out whether a treatment worked. The trap is built in. The raw counts point one way; the correct comparison of *proportions* points the other. To get it right you have to notice that you need ratios rather than totals — the sort of thing Chapter 1's four-cell table exists to teach. Most people get it wrong. People who are good at maths get it right more often.

Here is the manipulation. Half the participants were told the table showed results of a trial of a **skin-rash cream**. The other half were told the identical numbers showed results of a study on whether a city's ban on carrying **concealed handguns** had reduced crime.

Same table. Same arithmetic. Same correct answer.

**On the skin cream, the result was exactly what the deficit model predicts.** The more numerate participants did substantially better than the less numerate ones. Numeracy worked as advertised: it helped people get the right answer.

**On the gun control version, something else happened entirely.**

Participants tended to get the answer right when the correct answer flattered their politics, and wrong when it didn't. Liberal Democrats solved the table competently when it showed the ban working, and fumbled it when it showed the ban failing. Conservative Republicans did the reverse.

And now the finding that should genuinely disturb you:

> **The polarisation did not shrink among the most numerate. It grew.**

The gap between liberals and conservatives was *wider* among the highest-numeracy participants than among the lowest. The people best equipped to extract the truth from the table were the people whose answers were most reliably determined by their politics.

Sit with that, because the deficit model predicts the exact opposite. If bad beliefs came from insufficient reasoning ability, then ability should close the gap. Instead, ability widened it.

The explanation Kahan and colleagues give is **identity-protective cognition**. The high-numeracy participants weren't failing to reason. They were reasoning superbly — in the service of a conclusion they had already arrived at for reasons that had nothing to do with the table. Their skill was deployed not as a searchlight but as a defence lawyer: available when the evidence was friendly, and available in a different way when it wasn't.

That is the thesis of this chapter, and it generalises far beyond politics:

> **Intelligence, education and technical skill do not protect you from believing wrong things. They upgrade your ability to justify whatever you already believe.**

Which means the smartest person in a field can be the hardest to correct, and the person with the most impressive credentials can hold the most elaborately defended error. You have already met two examples in this book: Ronald Fisher deploying the concept of confounding to defend cigarettes (Chapter 4), and Linus Pauling — two Nobel Prizes — on vitamin C (Chapter 9). Neither man lacked reasoning ability. That was the problem.

## 11.2 The Trap: the machinery

Let's take the mechanism apart. I am going to organise the biases by what they *do*, rather than as an alphabetical list, because a list is forgettable and a function is not.

### 11.2.1 Reasoning is a lawyer, not a scientist

Start with the deepest layer, because it explains the rest.

We assume reasoning exists to find truth — that its function is to take in evidence and output correct beliefs, and that when it produces errors it is malfunctioning. On that view, every bias in this chapter is a bug.

Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber have argued at length that this is backwards. On their account, reasoning evolved substantially for a *social* function: to produce arguments that persuade others, and to evaluate arguments that others produce. It is equipment for a creature that lives by cooperation and negotiation, in which being persuasive and not being duped both carry enormous fitness value.

If that is even partly right, then most of what we call bias is not malfunction. It is the system working as designed:

- **Confirmation bias** is exactly what you want in an advocate assembling a case.
- **Motivated reasoning** is exactly what you want when the goal is to defend a position.
- **The asymmetry in how hard we scrutinise friendly and unfriendly evidence** is what a competent lawyer does deliberately.

And it explains the finding in 11.1 perfectly. A better reasoner is a better lawyer. Give a better lawyer a case they want to win, and they win it more convincingly. The skill is real; the direction it points is set by something else.

This also explains something otherwise puzzling: reasoning works much better in groups that argue than in individuals who think alone. Individually, we are advocates. Collectively — *if* the group actually contains disagreement — the advocacy of each is checked by the advocacy of others, and something like truth-seeking emerges from the argument even though it exists in none of the participants.

Hold onto that, because it is the seed of section 11.4.

### 11.2.2 Machinery for seeing patterns that aren't there

Human beings detect patterns aggressively, and the threshold is set low. This is sensible engineering — the cost of missing a real pattern (a predator, a poisonous plant) has historically exceeded the cost of one false alarm.

**The clustering illusion.** Randomness is lumpy, and we expect it to be even. Scatter points at random on a map and you will get clumps; people looking at those clumps see a cause. This is behind a great many disease-cluster scares, and it is why a cluster only becomes evidence when the *rate* is compared against what randomness would produce.

**The Texas sharpshooter.** Fire at the barn wall, then draw the target around the tightest group of holes. Formally: choosing your hypothesis after seeing which patch of data looks interesting. This is the amateur version of the industrialised research problem in Chapter 31 — subgroup analysis, multiplicity, and the garden of forking paths. The astrology subgroup in Chapter 4 is a sharpshooter target drawn deliberately, as a joke, to make exactly this point.

**Coincidence arithmetic.** We are bad at counting opportunities. An event with a one-in-a-million chance per person per day happens to about sixty people a day in a country of sixty million. The dream that came true, the phone call from the person you were thinking of — these are not evidence of anything, because with enough draws the improbable is guaranteed. The correct question is never "how unlikely was that?" but **"how many chances were there for something like that to happen?"**

### 11.2.3 Machinery for keeping a belief once you have it

**Confirmation bias**, properly understood, is not "ignoring contrary evidence." It is subtler and much harder to catch: it is *searching* preferentially for confirming instances.

Here is the classic demonstration, and I want you to actually play it before reading on.

> I have a rule in mind that generates sequences of three numbers. The sequence **2, 4, 6** obeys my rule.
>
> You may propose sequences, and I will tell you whether each obeys the rule. When you are confident, state the rule.
>
> What do you propose?

Most people propose **8, 10, 12**. Then **20, 22, 24**. Then **1, 3, 5**. All obey the rule. So they announce, confidently: *ascending even numbers increasing by two* — or some near variant.

Wrong. The rule is **any three ascending numbers.**

Notice what went wrong. Every sequence they proposed was one they *expected to pass*. Every "yes" felt like evidence. But a rule that says "ascending even numbers by 2" and a rule that says "any ascending numbers" both say yes to 8-10-12 — so that test discriminated nothing (Chapter 1's definition of evidence, in miniature).

The only informative move is to propose something you expect to be **rejected** — 3, 2, 1, say, or 2, 7, 400. A "no" would have told you something. A "yes" to 2, 7, 400 would have demolished your hypothesis instantly.

This is not a trick question. It is a compact model of how people actually investigate the world, and it is why the entire apparatus of falsification exists. **Seeking confirmation feels like doing research and is nearly worthless. Seeking disconfirmation feels like sabotage and is the whole job.**

**Biased assimilation.** Worse than ignoring contrary evidence: *processing* it in a way that strengthens you. In a well-known study, people who supported and people who opposed capital punishment were shown the same mixed body of evidence — some studies supporting deterrence, some undermining it. Both groups became **more** confident in their original positions. Each side found the supportive studies compelling and the unsupportive ones methodologically flawed.

And here is the sting: **they were often right about the flaws.** Every study has weaknesses. Critical appraisal skill — the thing this book is teaching you — is precisely the ability to find them. Deployed asymmetrically, that skill doesn't correct your beliefs; it insulates them. The better you get at this book's material, the more capable you become of dismissing anything you dislike on entirely legitimate technical grounds.

I want that to worry you, because it should.

**The effort asymmetry** is the mechanism. For evidence we like, we ask: *Can I believe this?* — and we need only one reason to say yes. For evidence we dislike, we ask: *Must I believe this?* — and we need only one reason to say no. Both questions feel like honest inquiry from the inside. The bias lives in which question gets asked, and that is chosen before any reasoning happens.

### 11.2.4 Machinery for what comes to mind

**Availability.** We judge how common something is by how easily examples come to mind — which tracks how *memorable* and how *reported* things are, not how frequent. Hence systematic overestimation of dramatic, recent and heavily covered risks (terrorism, plane crashes, child abduction, rare drug side effects) and underestimation of statistical ones (diet, hypertension, the slow accumulation of ordinary risk).

The media doesn't cause this by lying. It causes it by **selecting** — reporting the unusual, because the usual is not news. A perfectly accurate news service reporting only true things will systematically distort your sense of frequency, simply by its selection rule. That is Chapter 13.

### 11.2.5 Machinery from other people

**Social proof and conformity.** In Solomon Asch's experiments, a substantial proportion of participants gave obviously wrong answers about the length of a line when confederates gave that answer first. The perceptual judgement was trivial; the social pressure was enough.

**Information cascades.** If I see four people choose A, I infer they had reasons, so I choose A — and I have now added a fifth data point that carries no independent information. Large consensuses can be built on very little evidence, and from the inside they are indistinguishable from large consensuses built on a lot. This is why **counting how many people believe something tells you much less than it feels like it should** — Chapter 1's multiplication rule, in social form.

**Belief as membership.** This is the important one. Many beliefs function primarily as **badges** — statements about which group you belong to. Their truth is close to irrelevant to that function, and their falsity may even improve it (a belief that costs something to hold is a more credible signal of loyalty).

### 11.2.6 The reflex that makes beliefs unfalsifiable

You met this in Chapter 1's second drill: go looking for the people a treatment failed, and notice that when you find them, they explain their own failure in ways that protect the treatment. *I didn't do it properly. I didn't stick with it long enough. My case was too advanced. I was taking something else that interfered.*

This is worth naming as a mechanism because of how it works. Each individual excuse is **plausible**, and often true. Nobody is being irrational in any single instance. But the *system* has the property that no possible observation counts against the belief — every success confirms, every failure is explained away — and a belief that cannot be contradicted by any observation is not a claim about the world.

The tell is not the presence of excuses. It is the **asymmetry**: are explanations produced with equal enthusiasm for the successes? Nobody ever says "she got better, but she was probably going to anyway, so let's not count it."

### 11.2.7 Machinery about yourself

**Overconfidence.** When people say they are 90% sure, they are right substantially less than 90% of the time. This is measurable, it applies to experts within their expertise, and the standard fix is calibration training — making many predictions with explicit probabilities and scoring yourself.

**The illusion of explanatory depth**, which you met in Chapter 6: we feel we understand things far better than we do, and the feeling is generated by coherence rather than correctness.

**The bias blind spot.** People readily accept that biases affect human judgement in general, and reliably rate themselves as less affected than average. Including, it must be said, people who have just read a chapter about cognitive bias.

**A necessary honesty about Dunning–Kruger.** You will have heard that incompetent people systematically overestimate their ability. The popular version — a graph with a "peak of Mount Stupid" — is not what the original research showed, and a substantial critical literature argues that much of the classic pattern can be generated by **regression to the mean** plus a general better-than-average effect, without any special relationship between incompetence and self-assessment.

I include this deliberately, and not as a digression. Two reasons.

First, honesty: the most-cited cognitive bias in popular discourse may be substantially a statistical artefact — and specifically, an artefact of the impostor from Chapter 2. The tools in this book apply to this book.

Second, because it demonstrates the chapter's own thesis in the cleanest possible way. Dunning–Kruger is popular because it is *satisfying*. It licenses the thought that the people who disagree with you are too stupid to know they are stupid. That is precisely the kind of belief that spreads on the strength of what it does for its holder rather than what the evidence supports — which is the subject of the next section.

And while we are being honest: psychology's replication crisis (Chapter 48) has damaged a number of famous findings from this literature. The effects I have relied on here — confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, availability, conformity, biased assimilation — are among the more robust. Others you may have heard of are not. Apply this book to this chapter.

## 11.3 The master bias: belief as membership

Now the layer that explains why the biases point where they point.

Biases are not random noise. If they were, they would cancel, and we would be merely imprecise rather than systematically wrong. They point in a consistent direction, and that direction is set by **what a belief costs and what it buys**.

Consider a person deciding what to believe about a contested empirical question — one where their personal actions cannot change the outcome.

**The cost of being factually wrong is approximately zero.** Their belief does not affect the climate, the policy, the disease, the economy. Nothing happens to them.

**The cost of dissenting from their community is substantial and immediate.** Friction with friends and family. Loss of standing. In a profession, loss of collaborators, funding, invitations, and the low hum of being regarded as unsound.

So for the individual, holding the belief their group holds is **rational** — not in the sense of being truth-tracking, but in the sense of being the option that serves their interests. Kahan calls the aggregate result a tragedy of the science communications commons: each person behaving sensibly for themselves produces a society that cannot converge on facts.

This reframes the whole problem, and it has two practical consequences.

**First, it explains why facts don't work.** If someone's belief is doing identity work, more evidence attacks their standing rather than informing their view — and they will defend it with everything they have, which in a capable person is a great deal. Handing better arguments to a motivated reasoner is handing them better ammunition.

**Second, it tells you where to look in yourself.** The question is not "am I biased?" — the answer is yes, always, and the answer is useless. The question is:

> **On this specific belief, what would it cost me to be wrong?**

Cost in status, in relationships, in identity, in having to revisit things you have said publicly, in the sunk expense of a career built on it. Where that cost is high, expect your reasoning to be motivated, and discount yourself accordingly. Where it is genuinely zero, you can trust yourself a little more.

The most dangerous beliefs you hold are not the ones you hold weakly. They are the ones you hold as part of who you are.

## 11.4 Why "being aware of bias" doesn't work — and what does

Here is the part where most treatments of this subject go soft, and I would rather not.

**Knowing about cognitive biases does not much protect you from them.** You have just read a catalogue, and you are not now debiased. The bias blind spot means the most likely effect of this chapter is that you become better at diagnosing others. Debiasing interventions in the lab tend to produce small effects that fade and transfer poorly. Willpower and vigilance are not the answer, because the biases operate before the vigilance engages.

What actually works is **external, structural and procedural.** You do not fix a mind that cannot check itself by asking it to check itself harder. You build machinery that does the checking from outside.

And this is the moment to notice what this whole book has been describing.

> **Every method in this book is a prosthetic for a mind that cannot be trusted to audit itself.**

- **Randomisation** exists because you cannot be trusted to allocate patients fairly, even when you are trying. *(Ch. 16)*
- **Allocation concealment** exists because knowing the next assignment will bend your judgement about who to enrol. *(Ch. 17)*
- **Blinding** exists because knowing who got what will bend your assessment of how they did. *(Ch. 9)*
- **Pre-registration** exists because you cannot be trusted to remember accurately what you predicted before you saw the data. *(Ch. 53)*
- **Pre-specified primary outcomes** exist because after the fact, the outcome that moved will look like the one you cared about. *(Ch. 19, 31)*
- **Systematic review protocols** exist because you cannot be trusted to select studies impartially. *(Ch. 41)*
- **Peer review, adversarial collaboration, red teams** exist because Mercier and Sperber's point cuts both ways: individual reasoning is advocacy, but *structured disagreement between advocates* is the thing that works.

None of these require the participants to be unbiased. That is the entire design insight. They are built on the assumption that everyone involved is biased, and they work anyway, because the bias is denied the opportunity to act.

That is why this chapter sits where it does. Everything before it told you that your perception and reasoning cannot be trusted. Everything after it is engineering for that fact.

### The few individual techniques with support

Not nothing, but modest, and all of them work by simulating the external check:

**Consider the opposite.** Explicitly generate reasons the contrary conclusion might be right, and arguments against your own. One of the few debiasing techniques that has held up reasonably well.

**Pre-commit in writing.** Before seeing the data, write down what you predict and what would change your mind. The writing matters — memory rewrites predictions to match outcomes (Chapter 1).

**Keep a calibrated track record.** Predictions, with dates and explicit probabilities, scored afterwards. This is the only way to find out whether your confidence means anything, and it is humbling in a way that no amount of reading achieves.

**Argue with someone who disagrees and is competent.** Not to win. Specifically to find out where they are right. If you cannot state their strongest argument in a form they would accept, you do not understand the question.

## 11.5 The Tool

### 11.5.1 The four questions to ask yourself

**1. What would change my mind?** Name the specific observation. If you cannot name one — if no possible evidence would move you — this is not a belief about the world, and you should relabel it as a value or an identity, which is fine, as long as you stop arguing about it as though it were empirical.

**2. What would it cost me to be wrong?** Status, relationships, money, published positions, a sense of who you are. High cost means expect motivated reasoning and discount yourself.

**3. Am I applying the same standard both ways?** The symmetry test. Take the study you found compelling and the study you dismissed; swap their conclusions; would your appraisal survive? If you scrutinised one and not the other, you were assimilating, not appraising.

**4. Am I asking "can I believe this?" or "must I believe this?"** Catch which question you are asking. It is chosen before the reasoning, and it determines the answer.

### 11.5.2 The disconfirmation habit

From the 2-4-6 task, applied for life: **when you want to test a belief, look for the observation that would break it, not the observation that would fit it.**

In practice: search for the strongest case against, not the best case for. Read the best critic rather than the worst. When you find yourself accumulating supporting examples, stop — that activity feels like investigation and produces almost no information.

### 11.5.3 The coincidence question

Never ask "what are the odds of that?" Always ask **"how many opportunities were there for something like that to happen?"** The first question makes anything look like a miracle. The second usually resolves it.

## 11.6 The Drill

### Drill 1 — Play 2-4-6 on someone *(15 minutes)*

Run the task on a friend or colleague, ideally a clever one. Record their proposed sequences. Count how many they expected to be rejected.

Then reflect on the last hypothesis you investigated in your own work, and count the same thing.

### Drill 2 — The symmetry audit *(30 minutes)*

Find a study supporting something you believe and a study opposing it, in the same field, of roughly comparable design. Appraise both properly, using the tools from Chapters 4 and 9. Time yourself on each.

Two things to notice: whether you spent longer on one, and whether the flaws you found in the unwelcome study are flaws you would have accepted in the welcome one. The time difference is usually the more revealing measurement.

### Drill 3 — Write down what would change your mind *(20 minutes)*

Take three beliefs you hold about contested empirical questions. For each, write the specific observation that would make you abandon it. Be concrete: a study design, a result, a magnitude.

Any belief for which you cannot complete this exercise goes in a separate list. That list is your identity, not your knowledge. Keep them apart.

### Drill 4 — Steelman *(30 minutes)*

Take a position you consider clearly wrong. Write the strongest possible case for it — the version its most intelligent, best-informed advocate would make, not the version you find most convenient to argue against.

Then have someone who actually holds the view read it. If they say "yes, that's it, but you've missed X," you have learned something. If they say "that isn't what we think at all," you have been arguing with a phantom, possibly for years.

### Drill 5 — Start a prediction log *(ongoing)*

Write down predictions with dates and probabilities. Anything: project deadlines, election results, whether a finding will replicate, whether a treatment will hold up. Score yourself when they resolve.

This is the only drill in this book that gives you a real measurement of your own reliability. Everything else is theory about how minds fail. This is data about how yours does.

## 11.7 The Verdict

> **CHAPTER 11 SUMMARY CARD**
>
> **The comfortable theory (wrong):** people believe wrong things through a deficit of information or reasoning ability; education fixes it.
>
> **The Story:** Kahan's motivated numeracy experiment. Identical two-by-two table, labelled either as a skin-cream trial or a gun-control study. On skin cream, the numerate did better — as predicted. On gun control, people got it right when the answer suited their politics — **and the polarisation was *greater* among the most numerate, not smaller.**
>
> **The thesis:** intelligence, education and technical skill do not protect you from believing wrong things. They upgrade your ability to justify what you already believe.
>
> **Why:** reasoning is substantially built for argument and persuasion, not solitary truth-seeking (Mercier & Sperber). A better reasoner is a better lawyer. Confirmation bias and motivated reasoning are the system working as designed.
>
> **Seeing patterns:** clustering illusion (randomness is lumpy); the Texas sharpshooter (target drawn after the shots — the amateur form of Ch. 31's multiplicity); coincidence arithmetic. *Never ask "what are the odds?" — ask "how many opportunities were there?"*
>
> **Keeping beliefs:** the 2-4-6 task — people propose only sequences they expect to pass, and learn nothing. **Seeking confirmation feels like research and is worthless; seeking disconfirmation feels like sabotage and is the job.** Biased assimilation: the same mixed evidence makes both sides *more* confident. The effort asymmetry: *can I believe this?* versus *must I believe this?*
>
> **The warning specific to this book:** critical appraisal skill applied asymmetrically doesn't correct your beliefs — it insulates them. The better you get at finding flaws, the more capable you become of dismissing anything you dislike on entirely legitimate technical grounds.
>
> **The master bias — identity-protective cognition:** the cost of being factually wrong is usually zero; the cost of dissenting from your group is immediate and real. So holding your group's belief is individually rational and collectively disastrous. **Ask not "am I biased?" but "what would it cost me to be wrong about this?"**
>
> **An honesty note:** the popular Dunning–Kruger graph is substantially disputed, with critics arguing much of it is regression to the mean (Chapter 2's impostor). It is popular because it is satisfying — which is this chapter's thesis, demonstrating itself.
>
> **What doesn't work:** knowing about biases. You are not debiased by reading this. The bias blind spot means you will apply it to others.
>
> **What does work — and it is the point of the whole book:** *every method in this book is a prosthetic for a mind that cannot audit itself.* Randomisation because you can't be trusted to allocate; blinding because you can't be trusted to assess; pre-registration because you can't be trusted to remember what you predicted. **None of them require anyone to be unbiased. That is the design insight.**
>
> **The sentence to carry:** *Intelligence doesn't protect you. It arms you.*
