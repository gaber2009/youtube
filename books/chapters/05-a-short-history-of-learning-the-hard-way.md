# Chapter 5
# A Short History of Learning the Hard Way

> *Every line on a modern trial checklist is a gravestone.*

---

If you have ever looked at the reporting requirements for a clinical trial — the CONSORT checklist, twenty-five items with sub-items, demanding that you state how the allocation sequence was generated, who enrolled participants, who assigned them, how blinding was implemented, how many were lost to follow-up and why — your first reaction was probably that this is bureaucracy. Forms invented by administrators. The tax you pay for doing research.

It isn't. It is a memorial.

Every item on that list is there because its absence killed people, and because it took someone years or centuries to work out that its absence was what had killed them. Read as a document, CONSORT is dull. Read as a history, it is a list of the specific ways that intelligent, well-meaning people have been fooled, each one paid for in full.

This chapter is that history. Not because history is decorative, but because the machine described in Part III makes no sense as a set of rules to memorise, and complete sense as a series of scars. You will remember why allocation concealment matters if you know what happened without it. You will never remember it as item 8b.

There is a second reason for this chapter, and it is the more uncomfortable one. In almost every episode below, the delay between *knowing* and *doing* was measured in decades. The discovery is usually the fast part. What takes generations is getting anyone to act on it — and nobody counts the bodies from that delay, because they are invisible, statistical, and nobody's fault in particular.

---

## 5.1 The Story: twelve sailors, and forty-seven years

Scurvy killed more sailors than combat. On long voyages it killed a substantial fraction of a ship's company — gums rotting, old wounds reopening, teeth falling out, exhaustion, then death. Over the age of sail it is estimated to have killed on the order of two million seamen. It was, for centuries, simply the price of going to sea.

There were dozens of proposed cures, all confidently advocated: vinegar, sulphuric acid, sea water, cider, wort of malt, purges, fresh air, exercise, and, yes, citrus fruit. Each had its champions, each had its testimonials, and there was no way to tell them apart — because, as Chapter 1 established, testimony cannot tell them apart.

On 20 May 1747, aboard HMS *Salisbury* in the Channel, a naval surgeon named James Lind did something about it.

He took twelve sailors with scurvy. And here is the part that matters, the part that makes this the founding act of the discipline: **he chose them to be as alike as he could manage.** In his own account, their cases were as similar as he could make them; they were housed in the same part of the ship, and they ate the same basic diet. He had grasped, without any of the vocabulary we now have, that a comparison is only informative if the groups differ in the one thing you are testing.

He split them into six pairs, and gave each pair a different treatment:

| Pair | Treatment |
|---|---|
| 1 | A quart of cider daily |
| 2 | Twenty-five drops of elixir of vitriol (dilute sulphuric acid) |
| 3 | Two spoonfuls of vinegar, three times a day |
| 4 | Half a pint of sea water daily |
| 5 | **Two oranges and one lemon daily** |
| 6 | A medicinal paste plus barley water |

Within six days, one of the citrus pair was fit for duty and the other was well enough to nurse the rest. Nobody else improved much. The oranges ran out after six days — the ship simply had no more — and it didn't matter, because the answer was already visible.

Twelve men. No statistics. No randomisation, no blinding, no ethics committee. And a result so clean it is still taught three centuries later, because Lind had stumbled onto the one idea that makes knowledge possible: **compare like with like, change one thing, and look.**

### And then nothing happened

Here is the part of the Lind story that is usually left out, and it is the more important half.

The Royal Navy did not adopt lemon juice for the fleet until 1795 — around forty-seven years later. Lind died the year before. In the interval, scurvy went on killing sailors in enormous numbers, in a navy that possessed, in print, the answer.

Why?

Partly because **Lind himself was ambivalent.** He did not present his trial as the decisive demonstration we now consider it. It occupies a few pages of a four-hundred-page treatise, buried among other material, and Lind continued to entertain competing theories about the cause of scurvy — blocked perspiration, damp air, the general foulness of shipboard life. He was a man of his time, and his own greatest finding did not overturn his framework.

Partly because of a **technical failure with an unlucky twist.** Lind proposed concentrating citrus juice by boiling it down into a syrup — a "rob" — so it could be stored on long voyages. Heating destroys much of the vitamin C. So the preparation actually issued in some quantities was substantially inert, and produced disappointing results, which for a while looked like evidence *against* citrus.

Partly because of **competing authority.** Better-connected men advocated wort of malt, and were listened to.

And partly because there was no mechanism, anywhere in the world, for turning a demonstrated result into a practice. No systematic reviews, no guidelines, no regulator, nothing that scanned the literature and asked: *is there anything in here we should be doing?* The finding sat in a book, and books do not implement themselves.

It took Gilbert Blane — physician to the fleet, and a man with access to the Admiralty — to push it through. Once it happened, scurvy in the Royal Navy collapsed, and the strategic consequences for a country dependent on sea power were considerable.

**The two lessons of Lind, and they are of equal weight:**

1. The fair test works, and you do not need mathematics or large numbers to run one.
2. **Producing the evidence is not the same as acting on it, and the gap between them is where most of the bodies are.** That gap is the subject of Part VI and Chapter 44, and it has not closed. It has narrowed.

## 5.2 The machine, assembled one piece at a time

What follows is the modern randomised trial being built, component by component, over four centuries. Notice as you read that nobody was building a trial. Each person was solving one local problem, and only in retrospect do the parts assemble into a machine.

### 1537 — Ambroise Paré, and the accidental control

The standard treatment for gunshot wounds was boiling oil, poured into the wound, on the theory that gunpowder was poisonous. It was agonising.

At the siege of Turin, the French army surgeon Ambroise Paré ran out of oil. Having nothing else, he dressed the remaining wounded with a mixture of egg yolk, oil of roses and turpentine, and went to bed expecting them to die.

He recorded that he slept badly, and rose early to check. The men treated with his improvised dressing were resting comfortably, their wounds not inflamed. The men treated with boiling oil were feverish and in torment.

He resolved never to burn wounded men again.

**Contribution: the accidental control.** This is the oldest and still one of the commonest ways medicine learns — the supply runs out, a machine breaks, a shortage forces two groups to be treated differently, and someone notices. It is not a designed experiment, and it retains every one of the four rivals from Chapter 4. But it is the beginning of comparison, and comparison is the root of everything.

### 1747 — Lind, and the deliberate concurrent comparison

Covered above. **Contribution: groups made alike on purpose, treated at the same time, differing in one thing.** Concurrency matters more than it looks: comparing this year's patients against last year's is a different and much weaker act, because everything else changed too.

### 1784 — the Franklin Commission, and the invention of blinding

This one deserves to be far better known than it is.

Franz Mesmer had taken Paris by storm with "animal magnetism" — an invisible fluid he claimed to channel to cure illness. His patients had spectacular responses: convulsions, crises, cures. The testimony was abundant, sincere and dramatic.

In 1784, Louis XVI appointed a royal commission to investigate. Its members included **Benjamin Franklin**, chairing; **Antoine Lavoisier**, the greatest chemist alive; the astronomer Jean-Sylvain Bailly; and Joseph-Ignace Guillotin.

Now look at what they did, because it is astonishing for 1784. They understood that the question was not "do these patients improve?" — obviously they did — but "does the improvement come from the magnetism, or from the patient's *belief* that magnetism is being applied?" And they realised those two things could be separated by controlling what the patient knew.

So they ran, in their own phrasing:

- **magnetism without imagination** — subjects were magnetised without their knowledge, often blindfolded;
- **imagination without magnetism** — subjects were told they were being magnetised when they were not; sham objects were presented as magnetised.

Look at the shape of that. It is Chapter 1's four-cell table, executed as an experiment, in the eighteenth century.

The results were unambiguous. The dramatic effects appeared whenever the subject *believed* they were being magnetised, and failed to appear when they were magnetised without knowing. The commission concluded that the cause was imagination, not any fluid.

**Contribution: blinding, and the sham control.** They isolated the treatment from the belief about the treatment — which is the single most important trick in all of therapeutics research, and the direct ancestor of every placebo-controlled trial (Chapters 8 and 9). It was invented not to test a drug but to expose a fraud, and it took roughly a century and a half to become routine in medicine.

### 1830s — Louis, and counting

You met this in Chapter 1: Pierre Louis's *méthode numérique*, tabulating outcomes rather than recalling impressions, and finding that bloodletting did not do what every physician in Europe knew it did.

**Contribution: systematic counting replaces clinical impression.** The objection he faced — *every patient is an individual, you cannot reduce medicine to arithmetic* — is still the standard objection today, in every field, and it is still wrong in the same way.

### 1847 — Semmelweis, and the natural experiment

Also met in Chapter 1. Two maternity wards, one staffed by doctors coming from the dissecting room, one by midwives; wildly different death rates; handwashing; the deaths collapse; the profession rejects it.

**Contribution:** methodologically, the natural experiment and the power of a comparison you did not design but can exploit. Sociologically, something more important — the demonstration that **evidence has to survive the people who receive it**, and that the strength of a finding is no protection against a professional community's need not to believe it. It is worth remembering that his data implied that doctors were killing their patients, which is a message no group has ever received graciously.

### 1898–1931 — alternation, and then the coin

Once you accept that groups must be comparable, an obvious question follows: *who decides who goes in which group?*

The first answer was **alternation** — every other patient. Johannes Fibiger used it in a study of diphtheria serum in 1898. It is a genuine advance over letting the doctor choose, and it has a fatal flaw: it is predictable. If I know the next patient will go to the treatment group, and this patient is very sick, I can delay their enrolment until tomorrow. This need not be conscious dishonesty. It happens through a hundred small, well-intentioned judgements about who is "suitable for the study."

By 1931, James Burns Amberson's trial of sanocrysin for tuberculosis went further: two comparable groups were formed, and **the flip of a coin** decided which group received the drug. The patients did not know which they were in.

**Contribution: the recognition that allocation must be unpredictable, not merely systematic.** This is the seed of Chapter 17 — allocation concealment — which is a different and more violated idea than blinding, and empirically one of the most important safeguards there is.

### 1948 — Bradford Hill, streptomycin, and the modern trial

And here the parts come together.

Streptomycin was the first antibiotic effective against tuberculosis, then a mass killer. Britain after the war had almost no dollars and could obtain only a tiny quantity. There was nowhere near enough for everyone who needed it.

Austin Bradford Hill, a statistician at the Medical Research Council, saw what that scarcity made possible. If the drug could not be given to everyone, then withholding it from some patients was not a decision the investigators were imposing — it was already a fact. And that made a randomised comparison ethically acceptable in a way it might not otherwise have been.

The 1948 MRC trial of streptomycin in pulmonary tuberculosis is, by common consent, the beginning of the modern era. Look at what it contained:

- **Random allocation**, using a table of random sampling numbers — not alternation, not judgement.
- **Concealed allocation**: the assignments sat in a set of sealed envelopes, each marked on the outside only with the hospital's name and a number. The clinicians enrolling patients could not know what came next, and so could not, consciously or otherwise, steer anyone.
- **Objective outcomes, assessed blind**: chest radiographs were read independently by assessors who did not know which treatment the patient had received. Given that reading an X-ray involves judgement, this mattered enormously.
- **Everyone accounted for** at the end.

The result: **4 of 55 patients died in the streptomycin group (7%), against 14 of 52 in the control group (27%)**, within six months.

**Contribution: randomisation, allocation concealment and blinded outcome assessment, in one design.** Read that list against Chapter 4's table of rivals and you can see precisely what Hill built: a machine for deleting confounding, reverse causation, and the main routes by which bias enters — leaving only chance, which he, as a statistician, could calculate.

It is worth sitting with the fact that this happened in **1948**. The germ theory of disease, the periodic table, general relativity and the structure of DNA all predate or nearly coincide with the moment medicine worked out how to tell whether its own treatments worked.

### 1961 — thalidomide, and the arrival of the law

Thalidomide was marketed as a safe sedative, including for morning sickness in pregnancy. It caused severe limb malformations in thousands of babies.

Two things in this story belong in this chapter.

First, **how it was caught**: not by a trial, but by clinicians noticing something that should not have been happening — William McBride's letter to *The Lancet* in 1961, and Widukind Lenz's independent work in Germany. This is the case from Chapter 1 that shows what testimony is genuinely good for.

Second, **what it changed**: at the time, a drug could be sold in most jurisdictions without ever having demonstrated that it *worked*, let alone that it was safe in pregnancy. In the United States, Frances Kelsey at the FDA had repeatedly refused to approve thalidomide, demanding better data, and in doing so spared the US the worst of it. The scandal produced the Kefauver–Harris Amendment of 1962, which for the first time required manufacturers to demonstrate **efficacy**, by adequate and well-controlled studies, before marketing a drug.

**Contribution: the requirement for evidence became law rather than good manners.** Note what this created as a side effect — a regulatory chokepoint, which is enormously valuable and which the rest of the system then learned to game. That is Chapter 45.

### 1972 — Archie Cochrane, and the scandal of not knowing what we know

By the 1970s, randomised trials existed in large numbers. Archie Cochrane pointed out, in *Effectiveness and Efficiency*, that this was not the same as knowing anything, because nobody was systematically collecting the results. Thousands of trials, scattered across thousands of journals, in several languages, unindexed and unsummarised. The knowledge existed and was inaccessible.

The case that became emblematic: trials of corticosteroids given to women in premature labour, to mature the baby's lungs. The first was published by Liggins and Howie in 1972. More followed. Individually, several were too small to be conclusive. Pooled, they showed a substantial reduction in infant death.

Nobody pooled them for years. In the meantime, the treatment was not standard, and premature babies died of a cause we already had the collective evidence to prevent — the evidence was simply distributed across separate papers with nobody adding it up.

When the Cochrane Collaboration was founded in 1993, it took as its logo the forest plot of those trials: a column of individual studies, several straddling the line of no effect, and beneath them the pooled result sitting clearly on the side of benefit.

**Contribution: synthesis. The recognition that the unit of evidence is not the study but the totality of studies** — and that failing to summarise is itself a way of killing people. Part VI is this idea.

### 1989 and 2002 — CAST and the WHI

Two more lessons, covered properly in Chapter 6, listed here for the shape of the history:

**CAST (1989)** established that a drug can fix the biological marker beautifully and kill the patient — that surrogate outcomes are not a safe substitute for outcomes that matter.

**The Women's Health Initiative (2002)** established, at enormous scale, that large, careful, adjusted observational data can point confidently in the wrong direction, and that when observation and randomisation disagree, randomisation wins.

**Contribution: the maturity to distrust our own best non-randomised evidence, and our own best biology.**

### 1996 onwards — reporting, registration, and auditing the system

CONSORT, first published in 1996, standardised what a trial report must contain — because it turned out that trials were being conducted better than they were being described, and a reader could not tell a good trial from a bad one when key details were simply omitted.

Then trial registration: the requirement, from around 2005, that a trial be registered *before* it begins, with its outcomes declared in advance. This was aimed at two problems at once — trials that vanish when the results disappoint, and trials whose declared outcome quietly changes to whichever one came out best.

**Contribution: the recognition that the individual study is no longer the unit of failure — the literature is.** A perfect trial, unpublished, is worse than useless, because its absence distorts everything built on top of it. That is Part VII, and it is where the field's current frontier lies.

## 5.3 The Trap: why it always takes so long

Look back at the timeline. Paré in 1537 to Hill in 1948 is four hundred years. Lind's trial to the Navy's adoption is forty-seven. The first corticosteroid trial to routine use is around twenty. The Franklin Commission's blinding to blinding as standard practice is about a century and a half.

This is not because our ancestors were stupid. The same delays are happening now, in your field. There are recurring reasons, and they are worth naming because they are the same reasons every time.

**The finding threatens the identity of the people who must act on it.** Semmelweis's data said doctors were carrying death on their hands. Louis's data said a physician's clinical experience was worth less than a clerk's tally. This is not received as information; it is received as an accusation, and it is answered as one.

**The discoverer is rarely a clean advocate.** Lind hedged, buried his trial in a long book, and boiled the vitamin C out of his own remedy. Real discoveries arrive embedded in confusion, alongside their author's other and wronger ideas. We recognise the signal in retrospect and imagine it was obvious at the time; it was not.

**The ethical argument runs backwards.** The commonest objection to running a trial is that it would be unethical to withhold a treatment we believe works. But if we genuinely knew it worked, the trial would be unnecessary; the fact that we are arguing means we don't know. The formal resolution is **equipoise** — genuine uncertainty in the expert community about which arm is better — and it is the condition that makes randomisation not merely permissible but obligatory. Every case in Chapter 6 is a case where somebody was certain, refused to test, and was wrong. Withholding an unproven treatment is not the risky act. *Giving* it is.

**Nobody is accountable for the delay.** If a trial harms a patient, there is an investigator, a committee, a record. If a treatment that works goes unadopted for twenty years, the resulting deaths have no name attached, appear in no adverse event report, and are attributed to the disease. Errors of omission are invisible, and invisible errors are never punished, which is why they persist.

**And nobody was adding it up.** For most of this history there was no mechanism to notice that an answer existed. That is the least glamorous cause and possibly the largest.

## 5.4 The Tool

### 5.4.1 The checklist, read as a memorial

Here is the payoff. Take the components of a modern trial, and put beside each one the episode that produced it and the rival from Chapter 4 that it defeats.

| Component | Bought by | Rival it defeats | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| A comparison group at all | Paré, 1537 (by accident) | All four | 3 |
| Concurrent, comparable groups | Lind, 1747 | Confounding, bias | 3, 15 |
| Blinding and sham controls | Franklin Commission, 1784 | Bias (observer, reporting), placebo | 8, 9 |
| Counting instead of impressions | Louis, 1830s | Bias, chance | 1, 26 |
| Unpredictable allocation | Amberson, 1931 | Selection bias at entry | 17 |
| Randomisation | Hill, 1948 | Confounding — including unknown | 16 |
| Allocation concealment | Hill, 1948 | Bias at the point of assignment | 17 |
| Blinded outcome assessment | Hill, 1948 | Observer bias | 9 |
| Accounting for every participant | Hill, 1948 | Attrition bias | 21, 22 |
| Proof of efficacy before sale | Thalidomide, 1962 | The whole list, enforced by law | 45 |
| Post-marketing harm surveillance | Thalidomide, 1961 | Rare harms trials can't see | 25 |
| Systematic review and meta-analysis | Cochrane, 1972–93 | Chance, cherry-picking | 41, 42 |
| Patient-important outcomes | CAST, 1989 | Surrogate substitution | 6, 19 |
| Randomisation over observation | WHI, 2002 | Residual confounding | 6, 24 |
| Standardised reporting (CONSORT) | 1996 | Undetectable bias | 37 |
| Prospective registration | c. 2005 | Publication bias, outcome switching | 45, 46 |

Read the left column on its own and it is bureaucracy. Read it with the second column attached and it is a casualty list.

When you next fill in a risk-of-bias assessment and find yourself irritated by how pedantic it is, remember that "was the allocation sequence concealed?" is a question we learned to ask because, without it, well-meaning doctors put the sicker patients in the group they expected to do better, and then the treatment looked like it worked.

### 5.4.2 The delay audit

A tool for your own field, and the practical use of this chapter.

For any practice you believe should change, ask four questions:

1. **When was the evidence first sufficient?** Not first published — first *sufficient*, which is often earlier, and often only visible when several studies are pooled.
2. **When did practice actually change,** or has it?
3. **What is in the gap?** Estimate, crudely and honestly, the number of people affected during the delay. Crude is fine; the number's job is to be a magnitude, not a measurement.
4. **Which of the five reasons in 5.3 is operating?** Identity, an ambivalent discoverer, the inverted ethical argument, unaccountable omission, or no mechanism for noticing.

That fourth answer tells you what to do about it, and the answers are entirely different. A delay caused by nobody having synthesised the literature is fixed by writing a systematic review. A delay caused by a profession's self-image is not fixed by more evidence, and producing more evidence in that situation is a way of feeling productive while changing nothing.

## 5.5 The Drill

### Drill 1 — Reconstruct the trial *(30 minutes)*

Design Lind's 1747 experiment as you would run it today. Specify: the question in PICO form, the allocation method, whether blinding is possible and of whom, the primary outcome and how it is measured, the sample size you'd want, and who assesses the outcome.

Then ask the interesting question: **would your modern version have found the answer faster than Lind's twelve men did?** For an effect this large, probably not. That tells you something true and often forgotten — the elaborate apparatus of modern trials exists mainly to detect *modest* effects reliably. Huge effects were always easy. Most effects are not huge.

### Drill 2 — Read a checklist as a history *(30 minutes)*

Open the CONSORT 2010 checklist. Go through it item by item and, for each, write one sentence: *what goes wrong if this is missing?* Where you can't answer, mark it — those are your gaps, and the chapter references in the table above will fill them.

This converts a form into a mental model, and it is the single most efficient thing you can do to prepare for Part V.

### Drill 3 — Find the delay in your own field *(45 minutes)*

Identify one practice in your field — medical or not — where you believe the evidence is settled and the practice has not changed. Run the delay audit. Name which of the five causes is operating.

Then write the honest paragraph: what would actually shift it? If your answer is "more research," check whether that is true, or whether it is the comfortable answer.

### Drill 4 — The equipoise test *(20 minutes)*

Find a current controversy where someone argues that a randomised trial would be unethical. Write the argument for the other side: *given genuine uncertainty, is it not unethical to keep giving an unproven treatment to everyone, outside a trial, while learning nothing?*

You do not have to end up agreeing. You have to be able to construct it, because it is the argument that made streptomycin, and it is the argument that CAST proved was right.

## 5.6 The Verdict

> **CHAPTER 5 SUMMARY CARD**
>
> **The claim:** Trial methodology is bureaucratic box-ticking.
>
> **The truth:** Every component of a modern trial was purchased with a specific disaster. The checklist is a casualty list.
>
> **The assembly, in order:**
> Paré (1537) — the accidental control, when the boiling oil ran out.
> Lind (1747) — deliberately comparable, concurrent groups; twelve sailors, six pairs, citrus wins in six days.
> Franklin Commission (1784) — **blinding and the sham control**, invented to test mesmerism: "magnetism without imagination" against "imagination without magnetism."
> Louis (1830s) — counting instead of clinical impression.
> Semmelweis (1847) — the natural experiment, and the fact that evidence must survive its audience.
> Amberson (1931) — allocation must be unpredictable, not merely systematic.
> **Hill, streptomycin (1948)** — randomisation, concealed allocation in sealed envelopes, blinded radiological assessment. Deaths 4/55 (7%) vs 14/52 (27%). The modern trial exists.
> Thalidomide (1961–62) — caught by case reports, not trials; produced the legal requirement to prove efficacy.
> Cochrane (1972–93) — synthesis; the corticosteroid trials nobody added up.
> CAST (1989), WHI (2002) — surrogates mislead; observational data mislead; randomisation arbitrates.
> CONSORT (1996), registration (c. 2005) — the literature, not the study, is now the unit of failure.
>
> **The second lesson of Lind:** Producing evidence and acting on it are different problems. The Navy took ~47 years. The gap is where most of the bodies are, and nobody is accountable for deaths from delay.
>
> **Why delays happen:** the finding threatens the identity of those who must act; the discoverer is rarely a clean advocate; the ethical argument runs backwards; errors of omission are invisible and therefore unpunished; and often nobody was adding it up.
>
> **Equipoise:** If we truly knew it worked, no trial would be needed. The fact that we're arguing means we don't know — which makes randomising obligatory, not optional. Giving an unproven treatment is the risky act, not withholding it.
>
> **The date to remember:** 1948. Medicine worked out how to tell whether its own treatments worked *within living memory*.
>
> **The sentence to carry:** *Every line on a modern trial checklist is a gravestone.*
