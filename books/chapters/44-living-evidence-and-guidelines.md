# Chapter 44
# Living Evidence and Guidelines

> *A guideline is where evidence meets values.*
> *The evidence is usually visible. The values usually are not, and they are usually not yours.*

---

Almost nobody reads primary trials to decide what to do. They read a guideline — or they follow one without reading it, because it has been built into a pathway, a protocol, a quality metric, or a piece of software that will not let them proceed otherwise.

This makes guidelines the most consequential documents in medicine, and the least appraised. A systematic review is a summary of evidence. **A guideline is an instruction**, and turning evidence into an instruction requires something evidence cannot supply: a judgement about how to weigh benefits against harms, costs and inconvenience, on behalf of people who were not in the room.

This chapter is about reading that judgement rather than obeying it.

---

## 44.1 The Story: the two halves of a recommendation

Take a guideline recommendation of the kind you have read a hundred times:

> *"We recommend offering treatment X to all patients with condition Y."*

That sentence contains two entirely separate claims, and they are routinely conflated.

**Claim one, about the evidence:** we are confident that X produces a particular effect of a particular size. This is a factual claim, and Part VI has given you the tools to check it.

**Claim two, about the decision:** that effect, weighed against the harms, burdens and costs, is worth it for essentially everyone with condition Y. **This is a value judgement.** No amount of evidence establishes it, because it depends on how much a given person minds the side effects, the daily pill, the monitoring, the cost, and the label of being a patient.

The two claims can vary independently, which produces four combinations — and this is the single most useful table in the chapter:

| | **Strong recommendation** | **Weak / conditional recommendation** |
|---|---|---|
| **High certainty evidence** | We know it works, and nearly everyone would want it | We know the effect, and reasonable people would differ about whether it's worth it |
| **Low certainty evidence** | Rare and usually a warning sign — but sometimes justified (e.g. a catastrophic outcome, a cheap safe intervention) | We don't know much, and even if we did, it would be a close call |

GRADE (Chapter 39) formalises exactly this by separating **certainty of evidence** from **strength of recommendation**. It is a genuine intellectual advance and it is regularly undermined in practice, because a "conditional" recommendation gets implemented as a target, an audit standard, or a mandatory field in a form — at which point the conditionality evaporates and a close judgement call becomes a rule.

**The reading rule that follows:** whenever you see a recommendation, separate the two claims. Ask what the certainty of evidence was, and ask whose values were used to convert it into an instruction. Good guidelines state both explicitly. Bad ones present a value judgement in the grammar of a fact.

## 44.2 How a guideline is actually made, and where it bends

**The scope is set.** Which questions the guideline will answer — and therefore which it will not. Scope decisions are made before any evidence is reviewed and are rarely revisited.

**A panel is assembled.** This is the step that determines the output more than any other. Who is on it: specialists in the condition, generalists, methodologists, patients, health economists? A panel composed entirely of specialists in a disease will, entirely sincerely, tend to recommend more intervention for that disease, because that is what specialising in something does to your sense of its importance.

**Evidence is reviewed**, ideally by a systematic review (Chapter 41), ideally conducted independently of the panel.

**Recommendations are drafted**, ideally through a structured evidence-to-decision framework that forces the panel to state, in writing: the size of the benefits and harms, the certainty, the values assumed, the resource implications, and the effects on equity.

**Consultation and publication.** Then, crucially, **implementation** — where a nuanced document becomes a checkbox.

Two structural problems recur.

**Conflicts of interest.** Guideline panels frequently include members with financial relationships to the manufacturers of the treatments they are recommending. This has been documented repeatedly across specialties. The usual defence is that the leading experts are precisely the people companies fund, so excluding them excludes expertise. That is a real dilemma, not a fake one. But the response — disclosure — has a weak record (Chapter 47), and better options exist: excluding conflicted members from voting, requiring a conflict-free chair and methodologist, and making the evidence-to-decision reasoning public so the argument can be checked independently of who made it.

**Recommendations that outrun their evidence.** Studies examining the evidence base behind guideline recommendations across specialties have repeatedly found that only a minority rest on high-quality randomised evidence, with a substantial proportion resting on expert opinion or low-quality studies. This is not necessarily scandalous — decisions must be made under uncertainty, and "we don't know" is not implementable. It becomes a problem when the resulting recommendation is presented, and enforced, with the confidence appropriate to the strongest category.

## 44.3 Definition creep

A specific mechanism worth its own section, because it is powerful, quiet and largely invisible to clinicians.

Guidelines do not only say what to do for a condition. They frequently define **who has the condition** — by setting a threshold: a blood pressure, a blood sugar, a bone density, a cholesterol level, a score.

Lower the threshold and you have not changed anyone's health. You have changed how many people are patients.

Now recall Chapter 10. **Relative benefits are roughly stable across risk levels; absolute benefits are not.** People brought into a category by a lowered threshold are, by definition, at lower risk than those already in it. So they stand to gain less in absolute terms — while the harms of treatment, which often do not scale with baseline risk, stay the same.

There is a threshold below which treating people does more harm than good, and it is not usually where the guideline puts it.

Three questions for any threshold change:
1. **How many additional people become patients?**
2. **What is their absolute benefit, in events per 1,000 over a stated period?**
3. **What are their absolute harms, in the same units?**

If a guideline changing a threshold does not report all three, it has not shown its working.

## 44.4 Living evidence

A structural problem: guidelines have historically been produced in multi-year cycles, so a recommendation could be years out of date on the day it was published, and could remain in force long after the evidence had moved.

The response is **living evidence**: systematic reviews and guidelines maintained continuously, with searches rerun frequently and recommendations updated when new evidence crosses a threshold, rather than on a calendar.

This is a genuine improvement and it was demonstrated at scale during the COVID-19 pandemic, when living guidelines updated within weeks of major trial results. It also creates new problems worth knowing about: recommendations that change frequently are harder to implement and can look like inconstancy to the public (Chapter 57), and continuous updating requires continuous funding, which few bodies have.

**And the neglected half: de-implementation.** Guidelines are good at adding recommendations and poor at removing them. Practices that the evidence has abandoned persist for years because no mechanism exists to actively withdraw them — the delay problem from Chapter 5, running in the other direction. When you read a guideline, notice what it *stopped* recommending. Usually the answer is nothing.

## 44.5 The Tool: appraising a guideline

The formal instrument is **AGREE II**, across six domains. The practical short version:

**1. Who wrote it, and who is missing?** Panel composition, including whether patients and generalists were present.

**2. What are the conflicts of interest, and how were they managed?** Disclosure alone is weak. Look for exclusion from voting, conflict-free chairs, and independent evidence review.

**3. Is there a systematic review behind it, or a reading list?** Was the review independent of the panel?

**4. Is certainty of evidence reported separately from strength of recommendation?** If not, the guideline is conflating fact and value.

**5. For the recommendations you care about, what is the underlying evidence?** Follow the citations. Frequently, a strong recommendation traces back to something surprisingly thin.

**6. Are the benefits and harms quantified in absolute terms?** Or does the guideline say "reduces risk" without numbers?

**7. Whose values were assumed?** Explicitly stated, or silently the panel's?

**8. Does it define a threshold, and did that threshold change?** Apply 44.3.

**9. When was it last updated, and what would trigger the next update?**

## 44.6 The Drill

### Drill 1 — Split the recommendation *(30 minutes)*
Take five recommendations from a guideline in your field. For each, write the factual claim and the value claim as separate sentences. Then check whether the guideline reports certainty and strength separately.

### Drill 2 — Follow one recommendation to the bottom *(60 minutes)*
Pick a single recommendation you have followed without question. Trace it to the evidence: the guideline's citation, the review, the trials. Then appraise the trials with Part III and V.

This drill changes people. Sometimes the evidence is excellent. Sometimes a practice you have followed for years rests on two small unblinded trials from the 1990s.

### Drill 3 — Count the conflicts *(20 minutes)*
Find the conflict-of-interest declarations for a guideline panel. Count how many members declare financial relationships with manufacturers of the recommended treatments, and check how those conflicts were managed.

### Drill 4 — Price a threshold *(45 minutes)*
Find a guideline that lowered a diagnostic or treatment threshold. Estimate how many additional people qualify, and their absolute benefit and harm per 1,000 over a stated period. Then judge whether the change was justified.

## 44.7 The Verdict

> **CHAPTER 44 SUMMARY CARD**
>
> **What a guideline is:** not a summary of evidence but an **instruction** — and converting evidence into an instruction requires a value judgement that evidence cannot supply, made on behalf of people who were not in the room.
>
> **Every recommendation contains two separable claims:** a factual one (this treatment produces this effect, with this certainty) and a **value** one (that effect is worth the harms, burdens and costs for essentially everyone). GRADE formalises this by separating **certainty of evidence** from **strength of recommendation** — and implementation routinely destroys it, converting a "conditional" recommendation into an audit target.
>
> **Where guidelines bend:** scope decisions made before any evidence is seen; panel composition (a panel of specialists in a disease will sincerely recommend more intervention for it); conflicts of interest, where disclosure has a weak record and exclusion from voting is better; and recommendations presented with more confidence than their evidence supports — a large share of guideline recommendations across specialties rest on low-quality evidence or expert opinion.
>
> **Definition creep:** guidelines often define *who has the condition*. Lowering a threshold changes nobody's health and changes how many people are patients. Since relative benefits are stable and **absolute** benefits are not (Ch. 10), the newly included are at lower risk and gain less — while harms stay constant. Demand three numbers: how many more people, their absolute benefit per 1,000, their absolute harm per 1,000.
>
> **Living evidence** — continuously updated reviews and guidelines — fixes the multi-year lag and creates new problems: apparent inconstancy, and a need for permanent funding. **And the neglected half is de-implementation**: guidelines add recommendations well and remove them badly, so abandoned practices persist for years.
>
> **The sentence to carry:** *Separate the fact from the value. Good guidelines state both; bad ones present a value judgement in the grammar of a fact.*
